## Objectives

- Turn an observation into an insight and a recommendation, and say what distinguishes them.
- Size a recommendation with a stated scenario, so that the expected impact is honest.
- Find and state caveats from the data: small groups, gaps, incomplete periods, dependence on one month.
- Prepare for stakeholder questions by computing the likely answers in advance.
- Write a short executive summary with the recommendation first and check it against a word budget.

## Understand

### Observation, insight, recommendation

Analysis produces observations, and stakeholders need insights and recommendations. An **observation** is a fact: "Spain revenue was 16,642.50". An **insight** adds a comparison and a meaning: "Spain grew 62.6% against 68.1% for the UK, the slowest of the four markets". The comparison is what makes it informative, since a number alone cannot be judged (Day 140). A **recommendation** adds a decision for someone: "Review the Spain plan before the next planning round." You can test a sentence with three questions. Does it have a number? Does it compare that number with something? Does it say what to do? A sentence that passes the first is an observation, the first two an insight, all three a recommendation. Stakeholders can do something with the last; they can only nod at the first.

The step from observation to insight needs the question "compared with what?": a target, a previous period, another group, a benchmark. The step from insight to recommendation needs "so what?" and "who should do what by when?". If you cannot answer "so what?", the finding may be true but is not yet worth the stakeholder's time. State it as background or leave it out.

### Sizing a recommendation

A recommendation that says "improve Spain" is vague; one that says what size of prize is available is useful and checkable. Size it with a **scenario**: a simple, stated assumption applied to the data. For example: "If Spain had grown at the UK's rate, revenue would have been 570.10 higher, 0.85% of the total." The assumption (Spain grows at the UK rate) is visible, so the reader can disagree with it. The figure is small, which is itself an insight: the Spain gap is real but does not justify an emergency. Honest sizing builds trust, because a stakeholder who later checks a large promise against a small result stops listening. Always say that a scenario is an estimate, not a forecast, and show the assumption next to the number.

### Caveats

A **caveat** is a limitation of the data or the analysis that a reader needs in order to weigh the finding. Naming caveats is not apologising: it shows that you know where the evidence is weak, and it protects the stakeholder from over-reading it. Check for the common ones before presenting, and prefer to compute them. Small groups: a market with 83 orders can swing with a few customers, so differences between markets should not be over-interpreted. Gaps and incomplete periods: days with no orders, or a last month that is not complete. Dependence on a single period or customer: if one month or one customer drives the finding, say so. Definitions: whether returns or cancellations are included. Causation: a pattern in the data does not show why it happens. Place the one or two caveats that change the decision next to the recommendation, and the rest in the appendix. A long list of caveats on the first slide buries the message; none at all is not credible.

### Handling questions

Stakeholders ask questions, and the best preparation is to anticipate them and compute the answers in advance. List the five questions you would ask if you were the audience: "Is August a one-off?", "Which customers are behind Spain?", "Does it change if we exclude the largest customer?", "How was this calculated?", "What do you want us to do?". For each, prepare a number or a small table and keep it in the appendix or in a notebook. When a question comes that you cannot answer, say so, state how you will find out and by when, and do not guess. Answer the question that was asked, briefly, and return to the recommendation. If a challenge is about the definition of a number, the answer should be a reconciliation to a figure they already trust.

### Presenting

Open with the recommendation and the decision you need, then give the two or three facts that support it, then the caveat that matters. Use the structure of Day 138: situation, complication, resolution, with the answer first. Keep the written version short: a summary of around 100 words that someone can read in the time before a meeting. Spell out units and periods ("revenue in 2025, in thousands"). End by asking for the decision or the next step by name and date. Offer detail on request and not by default.

### Why it matters

Insights that stakeholders cannot use, or do not trust, are wasted analysis. Trust comes from a visible method, honest sizing and stated limits, and use comes from a clear recommendation with an owner. The misconception is that showing more charts demonstrates more rigour. Rigour is shown by the checks you did and the limits you state.

## Syntax and key functions

This is a communication lesson; the tools are checklists and small Python functions. The reference lists the three-question test and the content of each form.

| Form | Contains | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Observation | A number | Spain revenue was 16,642.50. |
| Insight | A number and a comparison | Spain grew 62.6% against 68.1% for the UK. |
| Recommendation | Insight and an action for someone | ... so review the Spain plan before planning. |
| Scenario | A stated assumption and its size | If Spain grew at the UK rate: +570.10. |
| Caveat | A limit that could change the reading | UK has 83 orders. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Observation, insight or recommendation

Classify draft sentences with the three-question test.

```python run
import re

COMPARE = ("against", "than", "from", "versus", "compared", "slowest", "fastest")

def classify(text):
    t = text.lower()
    has_number = bool(re.search(r"\d", t))
    has_comparison = any(w in t for w in COMPARE)
    has_action = any(w in t for w in ("recommend", "should", "review", "investigate"))
    if has_number and has_comparison and has_action:
        return "recommendation"
    if has_number and has_comparison:
        return "insight"
    if has_number:
        return "observation"
    return "none"

drafts = [
    "Revenue by country is shown below.",
    "Spain revenue was 16,642.50.",
    "Spain grew 62.6% against 68.1% for the UK.",
    "Spain grew 62.6% against 68.1% for the UK, so we recommend a review of the Spain plan.",
]
for d in drafts:
    print(classify(d).ljust(15), d)
```

```output
none            Revenue by country is shown below.
observation     Spain revenue was 16,642.50.
insight         Spain grew 62.6% against 68.1% for the UK.
recommendation  Spain grew 62.6% against 68.1% for the UK, so we recommend a review of the Spain plan.
```

The four drafts climb the ladder: none, observation, insight, recommendation. The word lists are a crude screen, not language understanding, but they make the point: most analysis stops at the second rung, and the stakeholder needs the fourth.

### Example 2: Size the recommendation with a scenario

State the assumption, compute the effect and show its size next to the total.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
countries = ["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"]
cats = ["Hardware", "Software", "Services"]
customers = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": range(1, 41),
    "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
    "country": [countries[i % 4] for i in range(40)],
    "segment": [["Retail", "Corporate"][(i // 4) % 2] for i in range(40)],
})
products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, 13),
    "product_name": [f"Product {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)],
    "category": [cats[i % 3] for i in range(12)],
    "unit_cost": [float(10 + 5 * i) for i in range(12)],
})
n = 400
sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n + 1),
    "order_date": pd.to_datetime("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, n),
    "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, n),
    "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
})
sales["unit_price"] = (sales["product_id"].map(products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]) * 1.5).round(2)
calendar = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31")})
calendar["year"] = calendar["date"].dt.year
calendar["quarter"] = calendar["date"].dt.quarter
calendar["month_number"] = calendar["date"].dt.month
calendar["month_name"] = calendar["date"].dt.strftime("%B")
sales["revenue"] = sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"]
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
s24 = sales.copy()
s24["order_date"] = s24["order_date"] - pd.DateOffset(years=1)
s24["quantity"] = (s24["quantity"] + 1) // 2
s24["revenue"] = s24["quantity"] * s24["unit_price"]
both = pd.concat([s24, sales], ignore_index=True)
monthly = both.groupby(both["order_date"].dt.to_period("M"))["revenue"].sum()
t24 = s24.merge(customers, on="customer_id")
c24 = t24.groupby("country")["revenue"].sum()
c25 = fact.groupby("country")["revenue"].sum()
uk_rate = c25["UK"] / c24["UK"]
gain = c24["Spain"] * uk_rate - c25["Spain"]
print(f"UK growth factor {uk_rate:.4f}")
print(f"Spain 2025 {c25['Spain']:,.2f}; at the UK rate {c24['Spain'] * uk_rate:,.2f}")
print(f"Gap {gain:,.2f}, {gain / c25.sum() * 100:.2f}% of total revenue")
```

```output
UK growth factor 1.6813
Spain 2025 16,642.50; at the UK rate 17,212.60
Gap 570.10, 0.85% of total revenue
```

If Spain had grown at the UK's rate (a factor of 1.6813), 2025 revenue there would be 17,212.60 instead of 16,642.50, a gap of 570.10 or 0.85% of the total. The recommendation can honestly say that Spain deserves a review and not an emergency, and it states the assumption in the first line so the reader can challenge it.

### Example 3: Caveats from the data

Compute the caveats instead of remembering them.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
countries = ["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"]
cats = ["Hardware", "Software", "Services"]
customers = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": range(1, 41),
    "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
    "country": [countries[i % 4] for i in range(40)],
    "segment": [["Retail", "Corporate"][(i // 4) % 2] for i in range(40)],
})
products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, 13),
    "product_name": [f"Product {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)],
    "category": [cats[i % 3] for i in range(12)],
    "unit_cost": [float(10 + 5 * i) for i in range(12)],
})
n = 400
sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n + 1),
    "order_date": pd.to_datetime("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, n),
    "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, n),
    "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
})
sales["unit_price"] = (sales["product_id"].map(products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]) * 1.5).round(2)
calendar = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31")})
calendar["year"] = calendar["date"].dt.year
calendar["quarter"] = calendar["date"].dt.quarter
calendar["month_number"] = calendar["date"].dt.month
calendar["month_name"] = calendar["date"].dt.strftime("%B")
sales["revenue"] = sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"]
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
def caveats(df, min_orders=100):
    notes = []
    orders = df.groupby("country")["order_id"].nunique()
    for country, n in orders.items():
        if n < min_orders:
            notes.append(f"{country}: only {n} orders, differences may be noise")
    days = pd.date_range(df["order_date"].min(), df["order_date"].max())
    empty = len(days) - df["order_date"].dt.normalize().nunique()
    notes.append(f"{empty} of {len(days)} days have no orders")
    by_month = df.groupby(df["order_date"].dt.to_period("M"))["order_id"].nunique()
    thin = by_month.idxmin()
    notes.append(f"thinnest month {thin} has {int(by_month.min())} orders")
    return notes

for note in caveats(fact):
    print(note)
```

```output
UK: only 83 orders, differences may be noise
114 of 364 days have no orders
thinnest month 2025-04 has 28 orders
```

The UK has 83 orders, below the course threshold of 100 orders, so a comparison of the UK with other markets should carry a caveat. About a third of the days between the first and last order (114 of 364) have no orders, which does not matter for annual totals but matters for any day-level analysis. The thinnest month is April with 28 orders, which tells the reader how much any single month can be trusted. Only the first and last caveats would normally reach the front of a presentation.

### Example 4: Prepare the answers to likely questions

Build a dictionary of questions and the functions that answer them from the data.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
countries = ["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"]
cats = ["Hardware", "Software", "Services"]
customers = pd.DataFrame({

    "country": [countries[i % 4] for i in range(40)],
    "segment": [["Retail", "Corporate"][(i // 4) % 2] for i in range(40)],
})
products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, 13),
    "product_name": [f"Product {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)],
    "category": [cats[i % 3] for i in range(12)],
    "unit_cost": [float(10 + 5 * i) for i in range(12)],
})
n = 400
sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n + 1),
    "order_date": pd.to_datetime("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, n),
    "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, n),
    "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
})
sales["unit_price"] = (sales["product_id"].map(products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]) * 1.5).round(2)
calendar = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31")})

calendar["quarter"] = calendar["date"].dt.quarter
calendar["month_number"] = calendar["date"].dt.month
calendar["month_name"] = calendar["date"].dt.strftime("%B")
sales["revenue"] = sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"]
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
monthly = fact.groupby(fact["order_date"].dt.to_period("M"))["revenue"].sum()

answers = {
    "Is August a one-off?": lambda: f"August {monthly['2025-08']:,.2f} against a monthly mean of {monthly.mean():,.2f}; July was {monthly['2025-07']:,.2f}",
    "Which customers drive Spain?": lambda: ", ".join(f"{n} ({v:,.2f})" for n, v in fact[fact["country"] == "Spain"].groupby("customer_name")["revenue"].sum().nlargest(3).items()),
    "What if we exclude the largest customer?": lambda: f"{fact['revenue'].sum() - fact.groupby('customer_name')['revenue'].sum().max():,.2f} of {fact['revenue'].sum():,.2f}",
}
for q, f in answers.items():
    print(q)
    print("  " + f())
```

```output
Is August a one-off?
  August 5,235.00 against a monthly mean of 5,594.38; July was 6,225.00
Which customers drive Spain?
  Customer 16 (2,797.50), Customer 36 (2,505.00), Customer 08 (1,995.00)
What if we exclude the largest customer?
  63,937.50 of 67,132.50
```

Each prepared answer is one line of numbers. August at 5,235.00 is below the monthly mean of 5,594.38, and well below July's 6,225.00, so it is a dip, not a collapse. Spain depends on Customer 16, Customer 36 and Customer 08. Without the largest customer (Customer 13, 3,195.00) the total is 63,937.50. If a question has no prepared answer, the honest reply is "I will find out and send it by Thursday".

### Example 5: An executive summary with a word budget

Assemble the summary from facts, answer first, and check its length.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
countries = ["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"]
cats = ["Hardware", "Software", "Services"]
customers = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": range(1, 41),
    "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
    "country": [countries[i % 4] for i in range(40)],
    "segment": [["Retail", "Corporate"][(i // 4) % 2] for i in range(40)],
})
products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, 13),
    "product_name": [f"Product {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)],
    "category": [cats[i % 3] for i in range(12)],
    "unit_cost": [float(10 + 5 * i) for i in range(12)],
})
n = 400
sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n + 1),
    "order_date": pd.to_datetime("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, n),
    "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, n),
    "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
})
sales["unit_price"] = (sales["product_id"].map(products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]) * 1.5).round(2)
calendar = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31")})
calendar["year"] = calendar["date"].dt.year
calendar["quarter"] = calendar["date"].dt.quarter
calendar["month_number"] = calendar["date"].dt.month
calendar["month_name"] = calendar["date"].dt.strftime("%B")
sales["revenue"] = sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"]
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
s24 = sales.copy()
s24["order_date"] = s24["order_date"] - pd.DateOffset(years=1)
s24["quantity"] = (s24["quantity"] + 1) // 2
s24["revenue"] = s24["quantity"] * s24["unit_price"]
both = pd.concat([s24, sales], ignore_index=True)
monthly = both.groupby(both["order_date"].dt.to_period("M"))["revenue"].sum()
t24 = s24.merge(customers, on="customer_id")
c24 = t24.groupby("country")["revenue"].sum()
c25 = fact.groupby("country")["revenue"].sum()
growth = (c25 / c24 - 1) * 100
slow = growth.idxmin()
gain = c24[slow] * (c25["UK"] / c24["UK"]) - c25[slow]
summary = (
    f"Recommendation: review the {slow} plan before the next planning round. "
    f"Revenue grew {(c25.sum() / c24.sum() - 1) * 100:.1f}% to {c25.sum():,.2f}, but {slow} grew only {growth[slow]:.1f}%, "
    f"the slowest of four markets. Matching the UK growth rate would add about {gain:,.0f} "
    f"({gain / c25.sum() * 100:.1f}% of revenue). Caveat: the UK has 83 orders, so market comparisons are indicative."
)
print(summary)
print(len(summary.split()), len(summary.split()) <= 70)
```

```output
Recommendation: review the Spain plan before the next planning round. Revenue grew 65.7% to 67,132.50, but Spain grew only 62.6%, the slowest of four markets. Matching the UK growth rate would add about 570 (0.8% of revenue). Caveat: the UK has 83 orders, so market comparisons are indicative.
48 True
```

The summary opens with the recommendation, supports it with two comparisons and a sized scenario, and ends with the one caveat that matters. It stays within the 70-word budget. Every number comes from the data, so the summary updates if the data does.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- An insight compares; a recommendation says who should do what.
- Size recommendations with a stated scenario, and say it is an estimate.
- Compute caveats (small groups, gaps, incomplete periods, dependence on one month or customer) and show the ones that matter.
- Anticipate questions and prepare numbers; say "I will find out" when you do not know.
- Recommendation first, short summary, ask for the decision.

### Common mistakes

- Presenting observations and calling them insights, so the stakeholder asks "so what?".
- Overstating the impact of a recommendation, so a small result later destroys trust.
- Burying the one caveat that matters in a list of ten.
- Guessing an answer in a meeting instead of promising a follow-up.
- Treating correlation in the data as the cause.

### Revision notes

- Number, comparison, action: observation, insight, recommendation.
- Scenario: state the assumption, compute the gap, express as share of total.
- Caveats: n, gaps, completeness, concentration, definitions, causation.
- Q&A list with prepared numbers; BLUF summary within a word budget.
