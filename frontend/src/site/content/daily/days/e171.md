## Objectives

- Answer a pipeline design question in a fixed order: requirements, scale, architecture, data model, failure handling, monitoring and trade-offs.
- Estimate data volume and storage with simple arithmetic and use the numbers to justify a choice.
- Explain delivery guarantees (at-least-once, at-most-once, effectively-once) and how idempotent writes give effectively-once results.
- Design a small dimensional model by stating the grain first, and check that a summary table reconciles to its detail.
- Describe failure handling: retries, checkpoints, quarantine, backfills and alerts, and give the trade-offs of each design choice.

## Understand

### What a design interview is

In a design interview there is no single correct answer and no code to run. You are given a vague request ("we need orders from our shop in the warehouse every day", "design a pipeline for click events") and the interviewer watches how you turn it into a system: which questions you ask, how you reason about scale and failure, and whether you can explain trade-offs. Weak answers jump straight to tool names. Strong answers start from requirements, reach tools last, and say what each choice costs. The interview is a conversation, so check in with the interviewer at the end of each step.

### A structure to follow

1. **Requirements.** Ask what the data is used for, how fresh it must be (daily, hourly, near real time), who consumes it, what accuracy is needed, the retention period and what happens if data is late or wrong. Separate **functional** requirements (what the pipeline produces) from **non-functional** ones (freshness, reliability, cost, security, privacy). Write them down and confirm them.
2. **Scale.** Estimate volume: events per day, bytes per event, growth, peak versus average. A calculation such as "5 million events a day at 500 bytes is about 2.5 GB a day, or about 0.9 TB a year before compression" tells you whether a single machine and a relational database are enough or you need a distributed engine. State your assumptions as assumptions.
3. **Architecture.** Describe the flow from **sources** (APIs, databases, files, event streams) through **ingestion** (batch pull, change data capture, streaming) to **storage** (a raw landing area that is kept unchanged, then cleaned layers, then curated tables) to **serving** (a warehouse, marts, dashboards). Say whether it is batch or streaming and why: batch is simpler, cheaper and easier to repair, and streaming is justified only when the freshness requirement is minutes, not hours.
4. **Data model.** Name the main tables, their keys and their **grain**: what one row means. For analytics a fact table with the measures (order lines, with amounts) joined to dimensions (customer, product, date) is the usual shape. Say how you handle changes in dimensions (overwrite or keep history) and keep the raw data so that you can rebuild.
5. **Failure handling.** What if the source is down, a file is late, a row is malformed, a job runs twice or crashes in the middle? The standard answers: retries with backoff for temporary errors, a **dead-letter or quarantine** location for bad records, **idempotent** writes (upserts or partition overwrites) so that a re-run is safe, **checkpoints** (a watermark or the last completed stage) so that a restart resumes instead of starting again, and a **backfill** procedure for reprocessing history.
6. **Monitoring and quality.** Run metrics (rows read, rejected, loaded, duration), freshness checks, reconciliation between source and target counts and sums, schema-change detection, and alerts that name an owner. Without these a pipeline fails silently.
7. **Trade-offs and next steps.** Close by naming what you chose not to do and why, and what you would add with more time.

### Delivery guarantees

A message system can deliver **at most once** (a message may be lost, never repeated), **at least once** (never lost, may be repeated) or **exactly once** (a strong guarantee that is hard and often only holds inside one system). In practice engineers combine at-least-once delivery with an **idempotent consumer**: each record carries a unique key, and writing it twice has the same result as writing it once (an upsert on the key, or a check of "already processed"). The result is called **effectively once**. It is the answer to most "what if the job runs twice?" questions.

### Batch or streaming

Streaming adds complexity: ordering, late events, state, exactly-once semantics, and more things to operate. Choose it when a decision depends on data within seconds or minutes. A common middle path is **micro-batching** (a batch job every few minutes). A good answer ties the choice to a requirement ("fraud checks need seconds, the finance report needs a day") rather than to fashion.

### Modelling questions

Modelling interviews ask "model an e-commerce system" or "design the tables for ride sharing". Begin with the business process, then state the grain ("one row per order line"), then the measures (quantity, amount) and the dimensions (customer, product, date, location). Then discuss normalisation: the operational database is normalised to avoid update anomalies, the analytical model is denormalised into a star for simple, fast queries. Mention keys: natural keys from the source, surrogate keys in the warehouse, and how you load late-arriving data. Mention that a derived **summary table** (for example daily revenue) makes dashboards cheap but must **reconcile** to the detail, which you check with a query.

### Common pitfalls

Naming tools before requirements; designing for a scale you did not estimate; forgetting that jobs rerun and fail; claiming exactly-once without saying where; ignoring privacy (personal data must be protected, minimised and sometimes deleted on request); no plan for schema change in the source; and leaving ownership of alerts undefined.

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Design skill is what separates someone who runs a script from someone who owns a system. The misconception is that the best answer uses the most components. A design with a few well-justified parts and an explicit failure story beats a diagram full of tools.

## Syntax and key functions

```text
sources -> ingestion -> raw (kept as received) -> clean (typed, validated)
        -> curated (facts and dimensions) -> marts / dashboards
        quarantine (bad records) and metrics (counts, freshness) on the side
```

```sql-syntax
CREATE TABLE fact_order_line (
    order_id    integer NOT NULL,
    line_no     integer NOT NULL,
    customer_key integer NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_customer (customer_key),
    amount      numeric(12,2) NOT NULL CHECK (amount >= 0),
    PRIMARY KEY (order_id, line_no)          -- the grain: one row per order line
);
```

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| grain | What one row of a table represents. |
| idempotent write | Writing the same record twice equals writing it once. |
| checkpoint | A saved position (watermark or completed stage) for restarting. |
| quarantine / dead-letter | Storage for records that failed validation. |
| effectively once | At-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer. |
| reconciliation | Checking that two tables agree on counts and sums. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Back-of-the-envelope sizing

The interviewer says: "5 million events a day, about 500 bytes each, we keep two years, and the peak hour has 10 percent of the day's events." The code turns the assumptions into daily storage, retention storage, the average and peak rates per second and a first decision. The assumptions are variables that you would confirm.

```python run
events_per_day, bytes_per_event, years, peak_share = 5_000_000, 500, 2, 0.10
gb_per_day = events_per_day * bytes_per_event / 1e9
tb_retained = gb_per_day * 365 * years / 1000
avg_per_sec = events_per_day / 86400
peak_per_sec = events_per_day * peak_share / 3600
print(f"daily volume: {gb_per_day:.1f} GB | retained for {years} years: {tb_retained:.2f} TB (uncompressed)")
print(f"average rate: {avg_per_sec:.0f} events/s | peak hour: {peak_per_sec:.0f} events/s")
print("decision: a daily batch on one machine is enough" if gb_per_day < 10 else "decision: consider a distributed engine")
```

```output
daily volume: 2.5 GB | retained for 2 years: 1.82 TB (uncompressed)
average rate: 58 events/s | peak hour: 139 events/s
decision: a daily batch on one machine is enough
```

About 58 events per second on average and 139 at peak is modest. The numbers justify a simple batch design, and they are stated as assumptions that change the decision if they change.

### Example 2: At-least-once delivery and idempotent writes

The source delivers the same message twice (a retry after a timeout). Appending each delivery doubles it. Writing by key (an upsert) gives the same result however many times a message arrives, which is effectively-once processing.

```python run
deliveries = [("o1", 10), ("o2", 25), ("o1", 10), ("o3", 5), ("o2", 25)]

appended = []
for key, amount in deliveries:
    appended.append((key, amount))

upserted = {}
for key, amount in deliveries:
    upserted[key] = amount

print("append total:", sum(a for _, a in appended), "| rows:", len(appended))
print("upsert total:", sum(upserted.values()), "| rows:", len(upserted))
```

```output
append total: 75 | rows: 5
upsert total: 40 | rows: 3
```

The duplicate deliveries inflate the appended total from 40 to 75. The upsert gives the correct total because the key makes the write idempotent.

### Example 3: Model a small star and check its grain

The grain is one row per order line. The tables are created in the block with a primary key on the grain, and a query reports revenue by country by joining the fact to the dimension. A check query confirms that the grain is unique.

```sql run
DROP TABLE IF EXISTS fact_line;
DROP TABLE IF EXISTS dim_cust;
CREATE TEMP TABLE dim_cust (customer_key int PRIMARY KEY, country text NOT NULL);
CREATE TEMP TABLE fact_line (
  order_id int NOT NULL, line_no int NOT NULL,
  customer_key int NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_cust (customer_key),
  amount numeric(10,2) NOT NULL CHECK (amount >= 0),
  PRIMARY KEY (order_id, line_no));
INSERT INTO dim_cust VALUES (1, 'DE'), (2, 'FR'), (3, 'DE');
INSERT INTO fact_line VALUES (100, 1, 1, 10.00), (100, 2, 1, 5.50), (101, 1, 2, 20.00), (102, 1, 3, 7.25);
SELECT d.country, COUNT(*) AS lines, SUM(f.amount) AS revenue,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM (SELECT order_id, line_no FROM fact_line GROUP BY 1, 2 HAVING COUNT(*) > 1) x) AS duplicate_grain_rows
FROM fact_line f JOIN dim_cust d ON d.customer_key = f.customer_key
GROUP BY d.country
ORDER BY d.country;
```

```output
country | lines | revenue | duplicate_grain_rows
DE | 3 | 22.75 | 0
FR | 1 | 20.00 | 0
```

The key on `(order_id, line_no)` enforces the grain, so the duplicate count is zero by construction. The `REFERENCES` clause stops orphan facts and `CHECK` stops negative amounts, so the database itself rejects bad rows.

### Example 4: Checkpoints decide how much work a restart repeats

A three-stage job fails once in stage 2. Without checkpoints the restart runs stage 1 again. With a checkpoint of the last completed stage, the restart resumes at stage 2. The log counts stage executions.

```python run
def run(checkpointed):
    done = 0                      # last completed stage (checkpoint)
    executions = []
    failed_once = False
    attempt = 0
    while done < 3 and attempt < 5:
        attempt += 1
        start = done if checkpointed else 0
        try:
            for stage in range(start + 1, 4):
                executions.append(stage)
                if stage == 2 and not failed_once:
                    failed_once = True
                    raise RuntimeError("stage 2 failed")
                done = stage
        except RuntimeError:
            continue
    return executions

print("without checkpoint:", run(False))
print("with checkpoint:   ", run(True))
```

```output
without checkpoint: [1, 2, 1, 2, 3]
with checkpoint:    [1, 2, 2, 3]
```

Without a checkpoint stage 1 runs twice. With one, only the failed stage is repeated. On a job whose stage 1 takes three hours, that difference is the argument for checkpoints, and the idempotent write makes the repeated stage safe.

### Example 5: A summary table must reconcile to the detail

A daily summary table is built from the detail with `CREATE TABLE AS`. The check query compares the summary's total and row count with the detail's, and returns a verdict. If an incremental job misses a day, this check is what notices.

```sql run
DROP TABLE IF EXISTS detail;
DROP TABLE IF EXISTS summary;
CREATE TEMP TABLE detail (d date, amount numeric(10,2));
INSERT INTO detail VALUES ('2025-03-01', 10), ('2025-03-01', 5), ('2025-03-02', 20), ('2025-03-03', 7.5);
CREATE TEMP TABLE summary AS SELECT d, SUM(amount) AS revenue FROM detail GROUP BY d;
SELECT (SELECT SUM(amount) FROM detail) AS detail_total,
       (SELECT SUM(revenue) FROM summary) AS summary_total,
       (SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT d) FROM detail) AS detail_days,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM summary) AS summary_days,
       CASE WHEN (SELECT SUM(amount) FROM detail) = (SELECT SUM(revenue) FROM summary) THEN 'reconciled' ELSE 'MISMATCH' END AS verdict;
```

```output
detail_total | summary_total | detail_days | summary_days | verdict
42.50 | 42.50 | 3 | 3 | reconciled
```

The totals and the number of days agree, so the summary is trustworthy. In production the same query runs after every load and alerts on a mismatch.

## Real-world applications

- **Design interviews and design reviews**: the same seven steps structure the design document that a team reviews before building a pipeline.
- **Capacity planning**: a sizing calculation like Example 1 decides whether to use a single database or a distributed engine, and what it will cost.
- **Operational runbooks**: the failure-handling section becomes the runbook that the on-call engineer follows when a job fails.
- **Warehouse design**: stating the grain and keys first (Example 3) prevents duplicated facts and ambiguous measures.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Follow requirements, scale, architecture, model, failure handling, monitoring, trade-offs.
- Estimate with explicit assumptions; let the numbers choose the technology.
- At-least-once plus an idempotent write gives effectively-once results.
- State the grain first, enforce it with keys, and reconcile summaries to detail.
- Checkpoints, quarantine, retries and backfills are the standard failure tools; name the trade-off of each.

### Common mistakes

- Naming tools before the requirements are clear. You may solve the wrong problem; ask about freshness, consumers and accuracy first.
- Choosing streaming without a freshness requirement. The system gets harder to run for no benefit.
- Claiming exactly-once delivery without saying where it holds. Explain it as at-least-once plus idempotent writes.
- Forgetting reruns. A job that appends is not safe to retry; use upserts or partition overwrites.
- Presenting a diagram with no monitoring or owner. Say how you would know it failed and who is told.

### Revision notes

- Seven steps: requirements, scale, architecture, model, failure handling, monitoring, trade-offs.
- Effectively once = at-least-once + idempotent consumer.
- Grain first; primary key on the grain; reconcile summaries.
- Checkpoint = restart point; quarantine = bad records.
