## Objectives

- Build a customer-level table from order-level rows and use it to define segments by rules and by quantiles.
- Compare segments with a metric, the segment size and lift, and explain what lift does and does not tell you.
- Assign each customer to a cohort (the month of their first order) and compute months since the first order.
- Build a cohort table of active customers and convert it to retention percentages.
- Read a cohort table correctly, including the triangle shape, small cohorts and the limits of the data window.

## Understand

### Why segment

Averages over all customers hide the groups that behave differently. A **segment** is a group of customers (or orders) that share a defining characteristic and are expected to behave alike: big spenders against occasional buyers, mobile users against desktop users, customers from paid advertising against organic visitors. Segmenting turns one average into several, so that a question such as "why is the return rate 7.7%?" becomes "which segment returns most, and is that segment large enough to matter?". Segments must be defined **before** you compare them, by a rule you can state and repeat, otherwise you are only cutting the data until something looks different (the false-discovery problem from Day 106).

### From orders to customers

The orders table has one row per order, but segments of customers need one row per customer. You create it with `groupby("customer_id").agg(...)`, computing for each customer the number of orders, total revenue, first and last order dates and number of returns (Day 80). This new table has a different **grain**, meaning what one row represents. Keeping track of grain prevents the classic error of counting a customer once per order. When you join customer attributes back onto orders, the customer values repeat on every order row, so summing a customer-level number over order rows counts it several times.

### Rule-based and quantile-based segments

A **rule-based segment** uses a threshold the business understands: "repeat customers have two or more orders", "new customers have exactly one". A **quantile-based segment** splits customers into equal-sized groups by a metric, for example revenue quartiles with `pd.qcut`. Quantile segments are always the same size by design, so the interesting number is the share of revenue each group produces. In many shops a small share of customers produces most of the revenue, often described as a Pareto pattern (the "80/20" idea): measure it instead of assuming it. When many customers tie on the metric, `qcut` can fail on duplicate edges, and ranking first with `rank(method="first")` breaks ties in a repeatable way.

### Comparing segments: metric, size and lift

To compare a segment with the whole, report three things: the segment's size, its metric and its **lift**. Lift is the segment's metric divided by the overall metric. A lift of 1.0 means the segment is the same as the whole, 1.5 means 50% higher, 0.5 means half. If electronics have a return rate of 12.6% against an overall 7.7%, the lift is 1.64. Lift is simple and easy to communicate, but it has three weaknesses. It ignores size: a segment of 15 orders can have a lift of 2.0 purely by chance. It ignores how much the segment contributes to the total: a high-lift group may be tiny. And it describes association, not cause. Always print the count beside lift, and consider a confidence interval or the chi-square test of Day 104 before acting.

### Cohorts

A **cohort** is a group of customers who share a starting event in the same period, most often the month of their first order. Cohort analysis follows each group over time to see how its behaviour changes with age, rather than with the calendar. It answers questions that a total cannot: are customers acquired in March coming back as often as those from January? To build it, find each customer's first order month, mark every order with its month, and compute **months since first order**, which is the order month minus the first month. The first month of a customer's life is month 0.

### The cohort table and retention

The **cohort table** has one row per cohort and one column per month since first order. Each cell holds the number of distinct customers from that cohort who ordered in that month. The month-0 cell is the cohort size. Dividing each row by its month-0 value gives **retention**: the share of the cohort active in month k. Retention is usually highest in month 0 (100% by construction) and falls, and a healthy business shows each newer cohort at least matching the older ones.

The table has a triangle shape: the January cohort can be observed for six months, but the June cohort for one, so the lower right corner is empty. Empty cells are not zeros; they are unobserved. Also notice that retention here means "ordered again in that exact month", not "ordered at some point since", and the two are different definitions that must be stated.

### Reading cohort tables with care

Small cohorts are noisy: a cohort of 15 customers moves 7 percentage points when two customers behave differently. The first cohort in a dataset is unreliable for another reason: it contains every customer who was already active when the data begins, not only new ones, so its "first order" is really their first order inside the window. Any customer seen for the first time in the first month may in truth have joined long before. Report both caveats beside the table.

### Common misconception

"Lift above 1 means the segment matters." It means the segment's metric is above the overall one; whether it matters depends on how many customers it contains and how large the gap is relative to chance. The second misconception is that a segment is a cause: customers in the top revenue quartile are defined by high revenue, so finding that they "generate more revenue" is circular.

### Why it matters

Marketing budgets, retention programmes and product decisions are made at segment level. A cohort table is also the most honest picture of customer loyalty, because it separates ageing from acquisition changes.

## Syntax and key functions

Customer table and segments:

```python
cust = df.groupby("customer_id").agg(orders=("order_id", "size"), revenue=("order_total", "sum"))
cust["segment"] = pd.qcut(cust["revenue"].rank(method="first"), 4, labels=["low", "mid", "high", "top"])

```

Cohort table:

```python
df["month"] = df["order_date"].dt.month
df["first_month"] = df.groupby("customer_id")["month"].transform("min")
df["age"] = df["month"] - df["first_month"]
table = df.pivot_table(index="first_month", columns="age", values="customer_id", aggfunc="nunique")

```

| Function | Returns |
|---|---|
| `groupby(...).agg(name=(col, func))` | One row per group with named summaries. |
| `pd.qcut(x, q, labels=...)` | Equal-count bins of x. |
| `s.rank(method="first")` | Ranks with ties broken by order of appearance. |
| `groupby(...)[col].transform("min")` | Group minimum repeated on each row. |
| `pivot_table(..., aggfunc="nunique")` | Table of distinct counts. |
| `table.div(series, axis=0)` | Divides each row by a value. |
| `cumcount()` | Position of each row within its group, starting at 0. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: One row per customer

Aggregating by `customer_id` changes the grain: 600 orders become one row per customer. The distribution of orders per customer shows that most customers ordered once or twice. Notice that the revenue per customer is far more spread out than the order count.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

pd.set_option("display.width", 200)
pd.set_option("display.max_columns", 20)


def make_orders(messy=False):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 600
    category = rng.choice(["home", "beauty", "sports", "electronics", "toys"], size=n, p=[0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10])
    base = pd.Series({"home": 40, "beauty": 25, "sports": 55, "electronics": 180, "toys": 20})
    discount = rng.choice([0, 5, 10, 20], size=n, p=[0.6, 0.15, 0.15, 0.1])
    ship = np.clip(rng.poisson(3.5, n) + 1, 1, 12).astype(float)
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1001 + n),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 180, n), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 260, n),
        "channel": rng.choice(["organic", "paid", "email", "referral"], size=n, p=[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]),
        "device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop", "tablet"], size=n, p=[0.55, 0.35, 0.10]),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": np.clip(rng.poisson(0.8 + discount / 20, n) + 1, 1, 6),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * rng.lognormal(0, 0.25, n)).round(2),
        "discount_pct": discount,
        "shipping_days": ship,
    })
    df.loc[rng.choice(n, 4, replace=False), "unit_price"] *= 8
    df["order_total"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"] * (1 - df["discount_pct"] / 100)).round(2)
    df["rating"] = np.clip(np.round(4.3 - 0.2 * ship + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)), 1, 5)
    df["returned"] = rng.random(n) < 0.06 + 0.06 * (category == "electronics") + 0.03 * (ship > 6)
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.05, "shipping_days"] = np.nan
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.25, "rating"] = np.nan
    if messy:
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 25, replace=False), "channel"] = "Paid "
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 3, replace=False), "quantity"] = -1
        df = pd.concat([df, df.sample(6, random_state=1)], ignore_index=True)
    return df
orders = make_orders()
cust = orders.groupby("customer_id").agg(
    orders=("order_id", "size"), revenue=("order_total", "sum"),
    returns=("returned", "sum"))
print("customers:", len(cust), "| orders:", len(orders))
print(cust["orders"].value_counts().sort_index().to_string())
print()
print(cust["revenue"].describe().round(1).to_string())
```

```python run
def make_orders(messy=False):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 600
    category = rng.choice(["home", "beauty", "sports", "electronics", "toys"], size=n, p=[0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10])
    base = pd.Series({"home": 40, "beauty": 25, "sports": 55, "electronics": 180, "toys": 20})
    discount = rng.choice([0, 5, 10, 20], size=n, p=[0.6, 0.15, 0.15, 0.1])
    ship = np.clip(rng.poisson(3.5, n) + 1, 1, 12).astype(float)
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1001 + n),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 180, n), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 260, n),
        "channel": rng.choice(["organic", "paid", "email", "referral"], size=n, p=[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]),
        "device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop", "tablet"], size=n, p=[0.55, 0.35, 0.10]),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": np.clip(rng.poisson(0.8 + discount / 20, n) + 1, 1, 6),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * rng.lognormal(0, 0.25, n)).round(2),
        "discount_pct": discount,
        "shipping_days": ship,
    })
    df.loc[rng.choice(n, 4, replace=False), "unit_price"] *= 8
    df["order_total"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"] * (1 - df["discount_pct"] / 100)).round(2)
    df["rating"] = np.clip(np.round(4.3 - 0.2 * ship + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)), 1, 5)
    df["returned"] = rng.random(n) < 0.06 + 0.06 * (category == "electronics") + 0.03 * (ship > 6)
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.05, "shipping_days"] = np.nan
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.25, "rating"] = np.nan
    if messy:
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 25, replace=False), "channel"] = "Paid "
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 3, replace=False), "quantity"] = -1
        df = pd.concat([df, df.sample(6, random_state=1)], ignore_index=True)
    return df
orders = make_orders()
ordered = orders.sort_values(["customer_id", "order_date", "order_id"])
orders["order_type"] = np.where(ordered.groupby("customer_id").cumcount().reindex(orders.index) == 0, "first", "repeat")
seg = orders.groupby("order_type").agg(
    orders=("order_id", "size"), aov=("order_total", "mean"), return_rate=("returned", "mean"))
seg["aov_lift"] = seg["aov"] / orders["order_total"].mean()
seg["return_lift"] = seg["return_rate"] / orders["returned"].mean()
print(seg.round(3).to_string())
```

## Real-world applications

- **Customer value programmes.** The revenue-share table in Example 2 tells marketing how much of the business depends on the top quarter of customers, and so how much a loyalty offer for them could matter.
- **Acquisition quality.** Comparing channels on return rate and AOV with lift, as in Example 3, helps decide where acquisition spend goes, provided the order counts are large enough.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Aggregating to one row per customer changes the grain; segments of customers need that table.
- Define segments by a stated rule or by quantiles, before comparing them.
- Lift is segment metric divided by overall metric; always show the segment size beside it.
- A cohort is the group of customers who started in the same month; age is months since the first order.
- A cohort table counts distinct active customers by cohort and age; retention divides each row by its month-0 size, and empty cells are unobserved.

### Common mistakes

- Counting customers once per order after joining customer data onto orders. Fix: aggregate at the customer grain with `nunique` or a customer table.
- Quoting a lift from a tiny segment as if it were reliable. Fix: print the size and use a test or interval.
- Treating the empty corner of a cohort table as zero retention. Fix: leave it blank or mark it unobserved.
- Reading the first cohort as "new customers" when the data window cut off their real first order. Fix: state that caveat or exclude that cohort from comparisons.
- Using `pd.qcut` on a column with many tied values and getting a "Bin edges must be unique" error. Fix: rank first with `rank(method="first")`.
- Mixing retention definitions (active in exactly month k versus active at any time up to month k) between reports. Fix: write the definition under the table.

### Revision notes

- `groupby("customer_id").agg(...)` gives the customer table.
- `pd.qcut(x.rank(method="first"), 4, labels=[...])` makes four equal groups.
- Lift = segment metric / overall metric.
- Cohort: `transform("min")` for the first month, age = month - first month.
- `pivot_table(aggfunc="nunique")`, then `table.div(table[0], axis=0)`.
