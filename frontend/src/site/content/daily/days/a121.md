## Understand

### Charts for time

A time series is a set of values recorded in time order: daily orders, monthly revenue. The reader's question is almost always one of three: what is the level, what is the trend, and is there a pattern that repeats (seasonality). The line chart answers all three, because slope shows the trend and repeated shapes show seasonality. Before drawing one, decide the **granularity** (daily, weekly or monthly). Daily revenue from a store with a few orders a day is very jagged: most of what the eye sees is noise. Aggregating to weeks or months, or adding a **rolling mean** (the average of the last 7 days, recomputed for every day, written `rolling(7).mean()` in pandas), makes the trend visible. Draw the raw series faint and the rolling mean strong, so readers see both the noise and the signal.

### Gaps and zeros

A common error in time-series charts comes from missing dates. If a table has no row for a day with no orders, a line chart joins the day before to the day after, and the chart looks as if revenue was smooth. For a sum such as revenue, a day with no rows means zero revenue, so you should insert the missing days with `resample("D").sum()`, which creates a row for every calendar day and sums the revenue (an empty day sums to zero). For a rate or an average the right fill is different: an empty day has no average, so leave it missing and let the line break. Decide what a missing day means before choosing the fill. Keep the dates sorted, as on Day 115, and keep the same time zone and granularity throughout.

### Line and area

An **area chart** is a line chart with the space under the line filled. It adds the sense of volume, and is best when the quantity is a total that starts from zero and the y axis does start at zero. A **stacked area chart** stacks several series, so the top edge is the grand total and each band is one component. It is useful for answering how a total is made up and how the mix changes. It is poor for comparing the components with each other, because only the lowest band sits on a flat baseline and the rest float on top of the others, so their slopes are distorted by whatever is below. When the reader needs to compare categories, use lines or small multiples, and keep stacked areas for the question "how big is the whole, roughly how is it split?".

### Distribution charts and what they hide

A **histogram** (Day 117) shows the shape of one distribution: where values cluster, whether the distribution is skewed, whether there are two humps. It depends on the number of bins. A **box plot** reduces a distribution to five numbers (minimum and maximum within the whiskers, the quartiles and the median) plus individual outlier points. It is compact, so it compares many groups side by side, but it cannot show that a distribution has two peaks. A **violin plot** draws a smoothed density curve mirrored on both sides of a centre line, often with a small box inside, so it keeps the shape that a box plot hides. Its smoothing can invent a tail beyond the actual minimum and maximum, and with few points it misleads. A rough guide: histogram for one group, box plots for comparing many groups when the shape is simple, violins when the shape matters and each group has enough points (dozens or more).

### Matplotlib and Plotly for these charts

In matplotlib, `ax.plot` draws lines, `ax.stackplot(x, [y1, y2], labels=[...])` draws stacked areas, `ax.hist(values, bins=10)` histograms, `ax.boxplot([group1, group2], tick_labels=[...])` box plots and `ax.violinplot([group1, group2], showmedians=True)` violins. In Plotly Express the equivalents are `px.line`, `px.area`, `px.histogram`, `px.box` and `px.violin`; Express puts all the groups of a box or violin in one trace with a `x` category per point, and a stacked area has one trace per category sharing a `stackgroup`. The checks are as before: read the lines, patches, boxes or traces back and compare with pandas.

### Misconceptions and why it matters

It is easy to believe that a smooth line proves a smooth process; smoothing can hide a weekly pattern or a sudden drop. A second common belief is that a stacked area chart shows each category's trend; only the bottom band does. For analysts, time and distribution charts are the two most common charts after bars, and a wrong fill of missing days or a stacked area used for comparison produces confident but incorrect statements.

## Syntax and key functions

```python
daily = s.resample("D").sum()                 # one row per calendar day, zero when empty
trend = daily.rolling(7).mean()               # 7-day rolling mean (first 6 values missing)
ax.plot(daily.index, daily.values, alpha=0.4, label="Daily")
ax.stackplot(x, [y1, y2], labels=["A", "B"])
ax.hist(values, bins=10)
ax.boxplot([g1, g2], tick_labels=["A", "B"])
ax.violinplot([g1, g2], showmedians=True)
px.area(long, x="month", y="revenue", color="category")
px.box(df, x="category", y="revenue")
px.violin(df, x="category", y="revenue", box=True)
```

| Item | What it does or returns |
|---|---|
| `Series.resample("D").sum()` | One row per day; empty days become 0 |
| `Series.rolling(7).mean()` | Mean of the last 7 values; first 6 are `NaN` |
| `ax.stackplot` | Stacked area; returns a list of filled collections |
| `ax.hist` | Histogram; `ax.patches` holds the bars |
| `ax.boxplot` | Returns a dict with `boxes`, `medians`, `whiskers`, `fliers` |
| `ax.violinplot` | Returns a dict with `bodies` (one per group) |
| `px.area` | One trace per colour value, sharing a `stackgroup` |
| `px.box`, `px.violin` | One trace holding all groups |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Fill the gaps, then smooth

The orders table has no row for days without orders. Resampling to calendar days makes the zero days explicit, and a 7-day rolling mean smooths them. The first six values of the rolling mean are missing because a full window does not exist yet. This runs in the browser.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_orders():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 240
    category = rng.choice(["Home", "Electronics", "Beauty", "Sports"], size=n, p=[0.35, 0.2, 0.25, 0.2])
    base = pd.Series({"Home": 45, "Electronics": 160, "Beauty": 28, "Sports": 60})
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * (0.8 + 0.4 * rng.random(n))).round(2),
        "shipping_days": rng.integers(1, 9, n),
    })
    df["revenue"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"]).round(2)
    return df.sort_values("order_date").reset_index(drop=True)

orders = make_orders()
daily = orders.set_index("order_date")["revenue"].resample("D").sum()
print("days with orders:", orders["order_date"].nunique(), "| calendar days in range:", len(daily))
print("zero-revenue days:", int((daily == 0).sum()))
rolling = daily.rolling(7).mean()
print("missing at start:", int(rolling.isna().sum()))
print("rolling mean on", rolling.index[10].date(), ":", round(float(rolling.iloc[10]), 1))
```

```output
days with orders: 172 | calendar days in range: 357
zero-revenue days: 185
missing at start: 6
rolling mean on 2025-01-18 : 185.3
```

### Example 3: Stacked area for a total and its mix

The stacked area chart draws monthly revenue with one band per category. The top edge is the total, so the sum of the bands in each month must equal that month's revenue. A Plotly area chart makes one trace per category, all in the same stack group. The two checks compare each with pandas.

```python local
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import plotly.express as px
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_orders():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 240
    category = rng.choice(["Home", "Electronics", "Beauty", "Sports"], size=n, p=[0.35, 0.2, 0.25, 0.2])
    base = pd.Series({"Home": 45, "Electronics": 160, "Beauty": 28, "Sports": 60})
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * (0.8 + 0.4 * rng.random(n))).round(2),
        "shipping_days": rng.integers(1, 9, n),
    })
    df["revenue"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"]).round(2)
    return df.sort_values("order_date").reset_index(drop=True)

orders = make_orders()
table = orders.pivot_table(index=orders["order_date"].dt.month, columns="category", values="revenue", aggfunc="sum", fill_value=0)

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
bands = ax.stackplot(table.index, [table[c] for c in table.columns], labels=list(table.columns))
ax.legend(loc="upper left")
print("bands:", len(bands), [t.get_text() for t in ax.get_legend().get_texts()])
print("month totals (first three):", [round(float(v)) for v in table.sum(axis=1).iloc[:3]])

long = table.reset_index().rename(columns={"order_date": "month"}).melt(id_vars="month", var_name="category", value_name="revenue")
area = px.area(long, x="month", y="revenue", color="category")
print("plotly traces:", len(area.data), "| same stack group:", len({t.stackgroup for t in area.data}) == 1)
```

```output
bands: 4 ['Beauty', 'Electronics', 'Home', 'Sports']
month totals (first three): [3499, 5287, 6418]
plotly traces: 4 | same stack group: True
```

### Example 4: Histogram, box plot and violin side by side

Three views of revenue per order. The histogram has ten bars that add up to every row; the box plot has one box per category whose median line should equal pandas' median; the violin plot has one body per category. Comparing the medians shows that the chart is built from the right numbers.

```python local
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_orders():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 240
    category = rng.choice(["Home", "Electronics", "Beauty", "Sports"], size=n, p=[0.35, 0.2, 0.25, 0.2])
    base = pd.Series({"Home": 45, "Electronics": 160, "Beauty": 28, "Sports": 60})
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * (0.8 + 0.4 * rng.random(n))).round(2),
        "shipping_days": rng.integers(1, 9, n),
    })
    df["revenue"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"]).round(2)
    return df.sort_values("order_date").reset_index(drop=True)

orders = make_orders()
cats = sorted(orders["category"].unique())
groups = [orders.loc[orders["category"] == c, "revenue"] for c in cats]

fig, (a, b, c) = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 3.5))
a.hist(orders["revenue"], bins=10)
box = b.boxplot(groups, tick_labels=cats)
violin = c.violinplot(groups, showmedians=True)

print("histogram bars:", len(a.patches), "| rows covered:", int(sum(p.get_height() for p in a.patches)))
print("boxes:", len(box["boxes"]), "| medians:", [round(float(m.get_ydata()[0]), 1) for m in box["medians"]])
print("pandas medians:", [round(float(g.median()), 1) for g in groups])
print("violin bodies:", len(violin["bodies"]))
```

```output
histogram bars: 10 | rows covered: 240
boxes: 4 | medians: [84.2, 537.5, 157.3, 153.3]
pandas medians: [84.2, 537.5, 157.3, 153.3]
violin bodies: 4
```

### Example 5: The same distributions in Plotly

Express draws a box plot and a violin with one call each. The box plot is one trace holding all 240 values with the category as the x of each, and the violin adds an inner box with `box=True`. An overlaid histogram has one trace per category.

        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * (0.8 + 0.4 * rng.random(n))).round(2),

## Real-world applications

- **Trading dashboards.** A daily line with a 7-day rolling mean (Example 2) is the standard way to show sales without being distracted by weekday noise.
- **Revenue mix over time.** A stacked area (Example 3) shows the total and the mix in one view when the audience asks how the business is made up.
- **Service-level analysis.** Box plots or violins (Examples 4 and 5) compare delivery times across carriers: the median shows typical performance and the whiskers and outliers show reliability.
- **Data quality checks.** A histogram of an amount column (Example 4) reveals spikes at exactly zero or at a default value that a mean would hide.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Choose granularity first; use a rolling mean to show the trend over noisy daily data.
- Fill missing days deliberately: zero for sums, missing for averages. `resample("D").sum()` makes the gaps explicit.
- Stacked areas show a total and its mix, not how each band trends.
- Histograms show shape, box plots compare groups compactly, violins keep shape when there is enough data.
- Check every chart with the same numbers computed in pandas.

### Common mistakes

- Plotting a table with missing dates. Symptom: a line that smoothly connects across weeks without orders. Fix: resample to the full calendar and decide the fill.
- Reading trend from a stacked area band other than the bottom. Symptom: a category seems to be rising when only the one below it fell. Fix: use lines or facets for comparison.
- A rolling mean that starts too early. Symptom: a line that begins before the data has a full window, or `NaN` at the start. Fix: leave the first `window - 1` points empty and say so.
- Violins on tiny groups. Symptom: smooth shapes with tails beyond the data. Fix: use box plots, or show the points.

### Revision notes

- `resample("D").sum()`, `rolling(7).mean()`; first `window - 1` values are `NaN`.
- `ax.stackplot`, `px.area`; `ax.hist`, `ax.boxplot`, `ax.violinplot`; `px.box`, `px.violin`.
- Box: quartiles, median, whiskers, outlier points.
- Gaps: zero for sums, missing for averages.
