## Objectives

- Calculate recency, frequency and monetary value for every customer from order rows, in SQL and in pandas.
- Turn the three measures into scores with quantiles and combine the scores into named segments by explicit rules.
- Compare segments by size and share of revenue, and explain what action each segment suggests.
- Show how concentrated revenue is across customers with a cumulative share, and compute a simple customer value with its assumptions stated.
- Describe the limits of RFM: it describes the past, scores are relative, and segment names are not facts.

## Understand

### From orders to one row per customer

An order table has one row per order (or per order line). Customer analytics needs one row per **customer**, with measures that describe that customer's behaviour. This change of **grain** (what one row represents) is done with `GROUP BY customer_id`. The classic set of measures is **RFM**:

- **Recency**: how long ago the customer last bought. Formula: the as-of date minus the date of the latest order. Smaller is better, because a customer who bought last week is more likely to buy again than one who bought two years ago.
- **Frequency**: how many orders the customer placed in the period. Larger is better.
- **Monetary value**: how much the customer spent in total, from the order lines at the price at sale (Day 144). Larger is better.

RFM was developed in direct marketing and still works because it needs only three columns that every order system has. It describes what customers did, not why.

### The as-of date

Recency needs a reference date. Never use "today" inside a query that you want to reproduce, because next week the answer changes. Choose an **as-of date**, such as the day after the last order in the data or the end of the reporting period, write it into the query and report it with the result. In Northwind the data ends in December 1998, so the examples use 1999-01-01.

### Scores from quantiles

The three measures are on different scales (days, counts, money), so they are converted to **scores** that can be compared. The usual way is to cut the customers into equal-sized groups by rank, with 3, 4 or 5 groups, and give each group a number. SQL has `NTILE(n) OVER (ORDER BY ...)` and pandas has `pd.qcut`. For recency the order is reversed, so that a score of 4 always means "best". Two details cause bugs. First, **ties**: when many customers share a value (most customers placed exactly one order), a quantile cut cannot put equal values in the same group, so ranks are broken arbitrarily. In pandas the usual fix is `qcut(x.rank(method="first"), ...)`; in SQL, add a tie-break column to `ORDER BY` so the result is repeatable. For frequency, a rule with explicit thresholds (1 order, 2 orders, 3 or more) is often clearer than a quantile. Second, scores are **relative**: a "top" customer is top compared with the other customers in this dataset, not good in any absolute sense.

### Segments

A **segment** combines scores by a rule you can state. For example: recency in the top group and monetary in the top group is "champions"; old recency but high monetary is "at risk, high value"; recent but low value is "new or small". The names help communication, and the rules must be written down so another analyst gets the same result. Decide segments by rules before looking at outcomes, then compare segments by customer count, total revenue, **share of revenue** and average order value. The share of revenue per segment usually shows that a small group carries most of the money.

### Concentration and customer value

A cumulative share shows concentration: sort customers by revenue from largest to smallest, take the running sum, divide by the total, and find how many customers reach 80 percent. This is the Pareto view (the "80/20" idea): it is a measurement, not a law, and each business has its own number.

**Customer value** can be estimated in a simple way: `average order value * orders per year * expected years of relationship * margin`. Every term except the first is an assumption (you do not observe the future), so the result is only as good as those assumptions and should be shown with them. Historic value (what the customer already spent) is a fact; predicted value is a model.

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Segments let a team spend differently: protect the best customers, win back the lapsed high-value ones, and not waste discounts on customers who would buy anyway. The misconception is that RFM segments are fixed groups of people. A customer moves between segments as time passes, and a customer with one big order may be called a champion by monetary value yet have no loyalty at all. Always look at frequency beside monetary value, and re-run the segmentation on a schedule.

## Syntax and key functions

```sql-syntax
SELECT customer_id,
       DATE '1999-01-01' - MAX(order_date) AS recency_days,
       COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)            AS frequency,
       SUM(line_revenue)                   AS monetary
FROM ... GROUP BY customer_id;

NTILE(3) OVER (ORDER BY recency_days DESC, customer_id)
```

```python
rfm = tx.groupby("customer_id").agg(last=("order_date", "max"),
                                    frequency=("order_id", "size"),
                                    monetary=("amount", "sum"))
rfm["recency"] = (as_of - rfm["last"]).dt.days
rfm["m_score"] = pd.qcut(rfm["monetary"].rank(method="first"), 4, labels=[1, 2, 3, 4])
```

| Function | Returns |
|---|---|
| `date - date` (PostgreSQL) | Number of days between two dates, as an integer. |
| `NTILE(n) OVER (ORDER BY x)` | Group number 1 to n of equal-sized groups. |
| `pd.qcut(x, q, labels=...)` | Equal-count bins of `x`. |
| `(ts - ts).dt.days` | Whole days of a timedelta series. |
| `s.sort_values(ascending=False).cumsum() / s.sum()` | Cumulative share of the total. |
| `np.searchsorted(cum, 0.8)` | Position where the cumulative share first reaches 0.8. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: RFM table in SQL

One row per customer: days since the last order, the number of distinct orders and the total spent. The as-of date is written into the query.

```sql run
SELECT o.customer_id,
       DATE '1999-01-01' - MAX(o.order_date) AS recency_days,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS frequency,
       ROUND(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount))::numeric, 0) AS monetary
FROM orders o
JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
GROUP BY o.customer_id
ORDER BY monetary DESC, o.customer_id
LIMIT 8;
```

```output
customer_id | recency_days | frequency | monetary
FOLKO | 8 | 2 | 12905
GOURL | 912 | 1 | 9579
BOLID | 292 | 2 | 8095
ALFKI | 511 | 2 | 4704
BONAP | 114 | 3 | 4509
COMMI | 292 | 1 | 3536
FURIB | 978 | 1 | 3222
AROUT | 416 | 2 | 2988
```

The biggest spenders are not necessarily the most frequent: a single large order can top the list. This is why monetary value should be read together with frequency.

### Example 2: Scores, segments and share of revenue

Recency and monetary value are cut into three groups with `NTILE`, where the tie-break `customer_id` makes the grouping repeatable. A `CASE` expression turns the two scores into named segments, and a window `SUM() OVER ()` gives each segment's share of all revenue.

```sql run
WITH rfm AS (
  SELECT o.customer_id,
         DATE '1999-01-01' - MAX(o.order_date) AS recency_days,
         SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) AS monetary
  FROM orders o JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
  GROUP BY o.customer_id
), scored AS (
  SELECT *,
         NTILE(3) OVER (ORDER BY recency_days DESC, customer_id) AS r_score,
         NTILE(3) OVER (ORDER BY monetary, customer_id) AS m_score
  FROM rfm
), seg AS (
  SELECT *,
         CASE WHEN r_score = 3 AND m_score = 3 THEN '1 champion'
              WHEN r_score = 1 AND m_score = 3 THEN '2 at risk, high value'
              WHEN r_score = 3 THEN '3 recent, smaller'
              WHEN r_score = 1 THEN '4 lapsed, smaller'
              ELSE '5 middle' END AS segment
  FROM scored
)
SELECT segment,
       COUNT(*) AS customers,
       ROUND(SUM(monetary)::numeric, 0) AS revenue,
       ROUND((100 * SUM(monetary) / SUM(SUM(monetary)) OVER ())::numeric, 1) AS revenue_share_pct
FROM seg
GROUP BY segment
ORDER BY segment;
```

```output
segment | customers | revenue | revenue_share_pct
1 champion | 10 | 40767 | 29.5
2 at risk, high value | 9 | 27422 | 19.8
3 recent, smaller | 19 | 14418 | 10.4
4 lapsed, smaller | 21 | 15099 | 10.9
5 middle | 30 | 40614 | 29.4
```

The two groups of ten and nine champion and at-risk customers hold nearly half of all revenue between them, while the 40 customers in the two smaller segments hold about a fifth. Sizes and shares differ a lot, and this is the information that decides where effort goes.

### Example 3: RFM scoring in pandas

An order table is created in code (seeded), aggregated per customer, and scored with quartiles. The recency score is reversed so that 4 is always best. The summary joins the scores into a combined code and counts customers per segment rule.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 900
tx = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": np.arange(5001, 5001 + n),
    "customer_id": (rng.random(n) ** 2 * 200).astype(int) + 1,
    "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 360, n), unit="D"),
    "amount": rng.lognormal(3.6, 0.5, n).round(2),
})
as_of = pd.Timestamp("2026-01-01")
rfm = tx.groupby("customer_id").agg(last=("order_date", "max"), frequency=("order_id", "size"), monetary=("amount", "sum"))
rfm["recency"] = (as_of - rfm["last"]).dt.days
rfm["r"] = pd.qcut(rfm["recency"].rank(method="first", ascending=False), 4, labels=[1, 2, 3, 4]).astype(int)
rfm["f"] = pd.qcut(rfm["frequency"].rank(method="first"), 4, labels=[1, 2, 3, 4]).astype(int)
rfm["m"] = pd.qcut(rfm["monetary"].rank(method="first"), 4, labels=[1, 2, 3, 4]).astype(int)

def label(row):
    if row["r"] >= 3 and row["f"] >= 3:
        return "loyal and recent"
    if row["r"] <= 2 and row["m"] >= 3:
        return "at risk, high value"
    if row["r"] >= 3:
        return "recent, light"
    return "lapsed, light"

rfm["segment"] = rfm.apply(label, axis=1)
out = rfm.groupby("segment").agg(customers=("monetary", "size"), revenue=("monetary", "sum"), avg_frequency=("frequency", "mean"))
out["revenue_share_pct"] = out["revenue"] / out["revenue"].sum() * 100
print(len(rfm), "customers")
print(out.round(1).sort_values("revenue", ascending=False).to_string())
```

```output
189 customers
                     customers  revenue  avg_frequency  revenue_share_pct
segment                                                                  
loyal and recent            68  23359.9            8.1               62.1
at risk, high value         26   6549.7            5.3               17.4
lapsed, light               69   5221.6            2.1               13.9
recent, light               26   2455.6            2.6                6.5
```

Rules written as a function are easy to review and to change. The order of the `if` statements matters: the first matching rule wins.

### Example 4: Concentration and a simple customer value

The cumulative share of revenue shows how many customers produce 80 percent of it. The simple value formula then uses stated assumptions: an expected relationship of 3 years and a margin of 30 percent. The assumptions are variables at the top, so that they can be challenged.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 900
tx = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": np.arange(5001, 5001 + n),
    "customer_id": (rng.random(n) ** 2 * 200).astype(int) + 1,
    "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 360, n), unit="D"),
    "amount": rng.lognormal(3.6, 0.5, n).round(2),
})
cust = tx.groupby("customer_id")["amount"].agg(["sum", "size"]).sort_values("sum", ascending=False)
cum = cust["sum"].cumsum() / cust["sum"].sum()
k = int(np.searchsorted(cum.to_numpy(), 0.8)) + 1
print("customers:", len(cust), "| customers for 80% of revenue:", k, "(", round(k / len(cust) * 100, 1), "% of customers )")

years_observed, expected_years, margin = 1.0, 3.0, 0.30
aov = float(tx["amount"].mean())
orders_per_year = len(tx) / len(cust) / years_observed
value = aov * orders_per_year * expected_years * margin
print("AOV:", round(aov, 2), "| orders per customer per year:", round(orders_per_year, 2))
print("simple customer value:", round(value, 2))
```

```output
customers: 189 | customers for 80% of revenue: 95 ( 50.3 % of customers )
AOV: 41.76 | orders per customer per year: 4.76
simple customer value: 178.98
```

The concentration number is a fact about the past. The customer value is a model whose answer moves directly with `expected_years` and `margin`, so report it with those two numbers.

## Real-world applications

- **Retention marketing**: the "at risk, high value" segment from Example 2 is the target of a win-back message, because these customers were valuable and have gone quiet.
- **Loyalty programmes**: frequency and recency scores decide who gets early access or rewards, while "recent, light" customers get a second-purchase nudge.
- **Budget decisions**: the cumulative share from Example 4 tells a manager how much of the revenue depends on a small group of accounts, which is also a risk measure.
- **Sales prioritisation**: a B2B sales team ranks accounts by RFM-style scores to decide which customers a salesperson visits first.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- RFM describes each customer by days since the last order, number of orders and total spend, at one row per customer.
- Fix and report the as-of date; never use the current date inside a reproducible query.
- Quantile scores are relative and sensitive to ties: use a tie-break or an explicit threshold.
- Segment rules must be stated, ordered and applied before comparing outcomes.
- Concentration and customer value are different things: one is measured, the other rests on assumptions.

### Common mistakes

- Using `CURRENT_DATE` for recency. The result changes every day and cannot be checked later; use a fixed as-of date.
- Scoring with `qcut` on a column with many ties and getting `ValueError: Bin edges must be unique`; rank first with `method="first"`.
- Giving recency the same direction as the other scores. A large recency means a long time ago, so reverse it.
- Counting a customer's orders with `COUNT(*)` after joining to order lines; use `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)`.
- Treating segment names as findings. A name is a label for a rule; check the size and share before acting.

### Revision notes

- R = as-of date minus last order date; F = distinct orders; M = revenue from lines.
- `NTILE(n) OVER (ORDER BY x, tie_break)` and `qcut(rank(method="first"))` give quantile scores.
- Cumulative share: sort descending, `cumsum / total`.
- Value = AOV x orders per year x years x margin, with the assumptions visible.
