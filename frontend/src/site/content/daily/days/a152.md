## Understand

### What a forecast is for

A **forecast** is a statement about the future that a decision needs: how much stock to buy, how many staff to schedule, what revenue to put in a budget. A forecast is judged by how well it helps the decision, not by how sophisticated it is. In data work the first question is therefore "what is the simplest method that is hard to beat?", because a complex model that does not beat a simple one has no reason to exist. The methods in this lesson need nothing but the history of one series (the monthly revenue, for example) and they work for the first few months to a year ahead. Beyond that horizon the future depends on things the history does not contain, such as new products, price changes and the economy.

### Simple forecasting methods

- **Naive forecast**: the next value will be the last value. For a forecast several periods ahead, every future period gets the last observed value. It is the benchmark that every other method has to beat.
- **Moving-average forecast**: the next value will be the average of the last `k` values, for example `k = 3`. Averaging smooths out noise, but it lags behind a trend, since old values pull the average back.
- **Seasonal naive forecast**: the next value will be the value from the same period one season ago (the same month last year). It captures a repeating pattern with no calculation, and it is a strong benchmark for any series with clear seasonality (Day 144 measured seasonality with an index).
- **Seasonal naive with drift**: the seasonal naive value plus the growth the series showed between years. If the seasonal naive forecast uses last year's January, the drift adds the average year-on-year change, so that a growing business is not forecast at last year's level.

Which method is best depends on the series: a flat series without a seasonal pattern suits the naive or moving average; a seasonal series needs a seasonal method; a series with trend and seasonality needs both.

### Judging a forecast: the holdout

A forecast must be tested on data it has not seen. The standard test is the **holdout**: hide the last part of the history (for example the last 12 months), build the forecast from the earlier part (the **training** data), and compare it with what really happened (the **test** data). Measuring the error on the training data tells you only how well the method describes the past. Using a model that has seen the test data is the most common error in forecasting and gives optimistic results. Because a single holdout is one sample, a more careful analyst repeats it at several starting points, which is called a rolling origin; the single holdout is the first step.

### Error measures

Let `e = actual - forecast` for each period of the test data.

- **MAE** (mean absolute error) is the average of `|e|`. It is in the same units as the series ("on average we are off by 6 units"), and it is easy to explain.
- **MAPE** (mean absolute percentage error) is the average of `|e| / actual`, as a percentage. It lets you compare series of different size, but it breaks when the actual is zero or very small, and it penalises over-forecasts and under-forecasts unequally in some cases. Use it only for series that stay well away from zero.
- **RMSE** (root mean squared error) squares the errors before averaging and then takes the root, so it punishes large misses more. Choose between these by the cost of errors: MAE when all errors cost about the same per unit, RMSE when large errors are especially harmful.

A method is only useful if it beats the naive benchmark. The ratio of its error to the naive error makes this concrete: a ratio below 1 means it improves on the benchmark.

### Communicating uncertainty

A forecast is never exactly right, and a single number conveys a certainty that does not exist. Honest communication includes a range. A practical way to build one is to use the spread of past errors: if the standard deviation of the holdout errors is `s`, then roughly 80 percent of actual values would fall within the forecast plus or minus 1.28 `s` (and 95 percent within 1.96 `s`), provided that the errors are about symmetric and the future is like the past. The second condition is the weak one. A range built from past errors does not include the surprise of a change in conditions, so it is a minimum. When you quote a range, check how often past actuals fell inside such ranges (the coverage), and say what the range assumes: "assuming no price change and no new competitor".

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Budgets, stock levels and hiring depend on forecasts, and decision makers need the range to choose between a cautious and an aggressive plan. The misconception is that a better-fitting model is a better forecast. A model that fits the history perfectly can forecast badly because it has fitted the noise. Always evaluate on a holdout, compare with the naive benchmarks and prefer the simplest method whose error is close to the best.

| Function or formula | Returns |
|---|---|
| `s.iloc[-1]` | The last value of a series. |
| `s.iloc[-k:].mean()` | Moving average of the last `k` values. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: A moving average in SQL

Quarterly Northwind revenue and its three-quarter moving average. The orders in this data are dated in one month of each quarter, so the series is built by quarter, which has no gaps (a window over rows only means "the previous periods" when every period has a row). The window is computed over all quarters and the final query keeps 1998. The last moving average is the moving-average forecast for the next quarter.

```sql run
WITH m AS (
  SELECT date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date)::date AS quarter,
         SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) AS revenue
  FROM orders o JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
  GROUP BY 1
), w AS (
  SELECT quarter, revenue,
         AVG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY quarter ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) AS ma3
  FROM m
)
SELECT quarter, ROUND(revenue::numeric, 0) AS revenue, ROUND(ma3::numeric, 0) AS ma3
FROM w
WHERE quarter >= DATE '1998-01-01'
ORDER BY quarter;
```

```output
quarter | revenue | ma3
1998-01-01 | 15449 | 14866
1998-04-01 | 8687 | 11827
1998-07-01 | 3323 | 9153
1998-10-01 | 18477 | 10162
```

The moving average is much smoother than the quarterly revenue, which jumps between about 3,300 and 18,500. Its last value (the fourth quarter of 1998) would be the forecast for the first quarter of 1999 under this method. In a small, noisy series like this the average says little about the next quarter, a first sign that the method suits stable series.

### Example 2: Four methods on a holdout

A four-year monthly series is generated with trend, seasonality and noise (seeded). The first 36 months are the training data and the last 12 are the test data. Each method forecasts the 12 test months from the training data only, and the errors are measured against the actuals.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
season = np.array([0.88, 0.85, 0.95, 1.00, 1.02, 1.00, 0.96, 0.95, 1.00, 1.05, 1.15, 1.20])
t = np.arange(48)
y = pd.Series((100 + 1.0 * t) * np.tile(season, 4) * (1 + rng.normal(0, 0.04, 48)),
              index=pd.period_range("2022-01", periods=48, freq="M")).round(1)
train, test = y.iloc[:36], y.iloc[36:]
f = {}
f["naive"] = np.repeat(train.iloc[-1], 12)
f["moving avg 3"] = np.repeat(train.iloc[-3:].mean(), 12)
f["seasonal naive"] = train.iloc[-12:].to_numpy()
drift = train.iloc[-12:].mean() - train.iloc[-24:-12].mean()
f["seasonal naive + drift"] = train.iloc[-12:].to_numpy() + drift
a = test.to_numpy()
print("series sample:", [float(v) for v in y.iloc[:3]], "... test total:", round(float(a.sum()), 1))
for name, p in f.items():
    e = a - p
    print(f"{name:24} MAE {np.mean(np.abs(e)):6.2f}  MAPE {np.mean(np.abs(e) / a) * 100:5.2f}%")
```

```output
series sample: [88.0, 86.9, 95.8] ... test total: 1702.7
naive                    MAE  21.13  MAPE 15.64%
moving avg 3             MAE  17.31  MAPE 12.61%
seasonal naive           MAE  14.75  MAPE 10.43%
seasonal naive + drift   MAE   3.66  MAPE  2.55%
```

The naive and moving-average methods ignore the seasonal pattern and have the largest errors. Plain seasonal naive is better, but still off by about 15 on average because the series grows by about 12 a year, so last year's values are too low. Adding the drift cuts the MAE to under 4.

### Example 3: Beat the benchmark?

The error of each method is divided by the error of the naive method. A ratio below 1 means the method improves on the benchmark, and the ratio makes it plain that a method with a good-looking percentage error may still add little.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
season = np.array([0.88, 0.85, 0.95, 1.00, 1.02, 1.00, 0.96, 0.95, 1.00, 1.05, 1.15, 1.20])
t = np.arange(48)
y = pd.Series((100 + 1.0 * t) * np.tile(season, 4) * (1 + rng.normal(0, 0.04, 48)),
              index=pd.period_range("2022-01", periods=48, freq="M")).round(1)
train, test = y.iloc[:36], y.iloc[36:]
a = test.to_numpy()
def mae(p):
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(a - p)))
naive = mae(np.repeat(train.iloc[-1], 12))
drift = train.iloc[-12:].mean() - train.iloc[-24:-12].mean()
cands = {
    "moving avg 3": mae(np.repeat(train.iloc[-3:].mean(), 12)),
    "seasonal naive": mae(train.iloc[-12:].to_numpy()),
    "seasonal naive + drift": mae(train.iloc[-12:].to_numpy() + drift),
}
print("naive MAE:", round(naive, 2), "| drift per year:", round(float(drift), 1))
for k, v in cands.items():
    print(f"{k:24} MAE ratio to naive: {v / naive:.2f}")
```

```output
naive MAE: 21.13 | drift per year: 12.5
moving avg 3             MAE ratio to naive: 0.82
seasonal naive           MAE ratio to naive: 0.70
seasonal naive + drift   MAE ratio to naive: 0.17
```

The moving average improves on the naive error by under a fifth, seasonal naive by about 30 percent, and seasonal naive with drift by more than 80 percent. In practice this ratio decides whether the extra effort of a method is justified.

### Example 4: An honest range

The errors of the seasonal-naive-with-drift method on the training years give the spread. The 80 percent range is the forecast plus or minus 1.28 times that spread. It is then checked against the actual test months to see how often the actual fell inside, which is the coverage.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
season = np.array([0.88, 0.85, 0.95, 1.00, 1.02, 1.00, 0.96, 0.95, 1.00, 1.05, 1.15, 1.20])
t = np.arange(48)
y = pd.Series((100 + 1.0 * t) * np.tile(season, 4) * (1 + rng.normal(0, 0.04, 48)),
              index=pd.period_range("2022-01", periods=48, freq="M")).round(1)
train, test = y.iloc[:36], y.iloc[36:]
# errors of "same month last year + drift", measured on the second and third training year
errs = []
for i in range(24, 36):
    d = train.iloc[i - 12:i].mean() - train.iloc[i - 24:i - 12].mean()
    errs.append(float(train.iloc[i] - (train.iloc[i - 12] + d)))
s = float(np.std(errs, ddof=1))
drift = train.iloc[-12:].mean() - train.iloc[-24:-12].mean()
fc = train.iloc[-12:].to_numpy() + drift
low, high = fc - 1.28 * s, fc + 1.28 * s
inside = int(((test.to_numpy() >= low) & (test.to_numpy() <= high)).sum())
print("error spread (std):", round(s, 2))
print("next month forecast:", round(float(fc[0]), 1), "range:", round(float(low[0]), 1), "to", round(float(high[0]), 1))
print("actual months inside the 80% range:", inside, "of 12")
```

```output
error spread (std): 7.19
next month forecast: 122.3 range: 113.1 to 131.5
actual months inside the 80% range: 11 of 12
```

A range built from 12 past errors is rough; the coverage in the test period is the real check. A statement for a manager would read: "January is expected at about the forecast figure, most likely between the low and high values, assuming no change in prices or the market."

## Real-world applications

- **Budgeting**: a seasonal forecast with a range lets finance set a base-case budget and a downside case, instead of one number.
- **Stock planning**: seasonal forecasts at product level drive the purchase quantities before a peak, and the range sizes the safety stock (Day 150).
- **Staffing**: a forecast of orders by month tells operations when to hire temporary staff.
- **Target setting**: the error of the naive benchmark shows how much of a target miss is within normal variation, which helps leaders avoid reacting to noise.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- The naive and seasonal naive forecasts are benchmarks that every method must beat.
- Always evaluate on a holdout period that the method has not seen.
- MAE is in units, MAPE is relative and fails near zero; compare methods by a ratio to the naive error.
- A seasonal series needs a seasonal method, and growth needs a drift or trend.
- Give a range built from past errors, check its coverage, and state the assumptions.

### Common mistakes

- Evaluating on the training data. The error is far too optimistic; keep the last months for testing.
- Using MAPE on a series that includes zeros, which divides by zero. Use MAE or a different base.
- Forecasting a seasonal series with a moving average and being surprised by the December miss. Use a seasonal method.
- Reporting one number. The reader assumes certainty; add a range and the assumptions behind it.
- Believing that the best-fitting model is the best forecaster. Check the holdout error and the benchmark ratio.

### Revision notes

- Naive = last value; seasonal naive = same period last year; moving average = mean of last `k`.
- Drift = average year-on-year change added to the seasonal naive forecast.
- MAE = mean of absolute errors; MAPE = mean of absolute errors over actuals.
- Range = forecast plus or minus 1.28 x error std for about 80 percent, if past errors resemble future ones.
