## Objectives

- Define a cohort as the group of customers who share the period of their first order, and compute it with `MIN` and a `GROUP BY`.
- Build a retention table: the number and percentage of each cohort that is active in later periods.
- Turn a long result into a one-row-per-cohort table with conditional aggregation.
- Compute funnel steps and the conversion rate from the previous step and from the first step.

## Understand

### Why group customers by their start

An overall figure such as "5,000 active customers this year" mixes people who joined last week with people who joined five years ago. It cannot tell whether the business keeps customers. **Cohort analysis** compares groups of customers who began at the same time. A **cohort** is a set of entities that share a defining event in the same period, most often the period of their first purchase or sign-up. Following each cohort over time shows whether newer cohorts behave better or worse than older ones.

### Defining the cohort in SQL

Two steps define the cohorts. First find each customer's first event: `SELECT customer_id, MIN(order_date) AS first_order FROM orders GROUP BY customer_id`. Second, reduce that date to the cohort period, for example the month with `date_trunc('month', first_order)` or the year with `EXTRACT(YEAR FROM first_order)`. The result has exactly one row per customer, which is crucial: a customer must belong to one and only one cohort. The size of a cohort is the count of customers with that period.

Choose the period to fit the data. Monthly cohorts suit a busy shop. In this Northwind copy, customers order rarely (100 orders over three years and 89 customers who ordered), so monthly retention is mostly empty and yearly cohorts show a more readable picture. Say so in the report instead of forcing a period that hides the shape.

### Retention

**Retention** measures how many members of a cohort are still active in a later period. "Active" must be defined: here, placed at least one order in that period. For each customer find the distinct periods in which they were active, join those to the customer's cohort, and compute the **age** of each activity as periods since the cohort period (0 means the cohort period itself). Then count distinct customers per cohort and age. The **retention rate** is that count divided by the cohort size, which equals the count at age 0.

The table in long form has one row per cohort and age. Dividing by the cohort size can be done with a join to the cohort sizes or with `FIRST_VALUE(active) OVER (PARTITION BY cohort ORDER BY age)`, since the first row of each cohort is age 0 (day 53). Age 0 is always 100 percent, by definition.

### Pivoting into a retention grid

Dashboards show one row per cohort and one column per age. SQL builds this with **conditional aggregation**: `COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) FILTER (WHERE age = 1)` counts only the rows meeting the condition, so a column is created for each age you list. The `FILTER` clause is written right after an aggregate function. The set of columns is fixed in the query text, so new ages need a new column.

### Funnels

A **funnel** is an ordered series of steps that an entity can pass through, such as visited, signed up, ordered, ordered again. Each step contains the entities that reached it, and counts decrease from step to step. Two figures matter. The **step conversion** is the count at this step divided by the count at the previous step (what share continued). The **overall conversion** is the count divided by the count at the first step. Build a funnel by computing the count for each step, putting the counts as rows (with `UNION ALL`, or `VALUES`) and using `LAG` (day 51) to read the previous step.

Be careful that each step is a subset of the previous one. A customer with three orders must also be counted in "at least one order" and "at least two orders". Define steps as "reached at least this far", not "stopped exactly here".

### Counting distinct entities

Retention and funnels count customers, not orders. Use `COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)`, because a customer with five orders in one period is one active customer, not five.

### Common misconception

A common mistake is to think retention goes down only because customers leave. In this data, retention in later periods is also limited by how much time has passed. The 1998 cohort has no later year at all, so its absence in age 1 is not churn but missing data. Always check how far each cohort has been observed before comparing.

### Why it matters

Acquisition looks good if cohorts are big; the business is healthy only if customers return. Retention tables and funnels are the standard way to decide whether to spend on acquiring customers or on improving the experience.

## Syntax and key functions

```sql-syntax
-- cohort assignment: one row per customer
SELECT customer_id, MIN(order_date) AS first_order
FROM orders
GROUP BY customer_id;

-- cohort period
date_trunc('month', first_order)::date        -- monthly
EXTRACT(YEAR FROM first_order)::int            -- yearly
```

```sql-syntax
-- age of an activity in months
(EXTRACT(YEAR FROM activity_month) - EXTRACT(YEAR FROM cohort_month)) * 12
  + EXTRACT(MONTH FROM activity_month) - EXTRACT(MONTH FROM cohort_month)

-- one column of a retention grid
COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) FILTER (WHERE age = 1) AS age_1
```

| Piece | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `MIN(order_date)` per customer | First event, defines the cohort |
| `COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)` | Customers, not rows |
| `FILTER (WHERE ...)` | Conditional aggregate for pivoted columns |
| `FIRST_VALUE(n) OVER (PARTITION BY cohort ORDER BY age)` | Cohort size on every row |
| `LAG(n) OVER (ORDER BY step_no)` | Previous step count for step conversion |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: yearly cohorts and their sizes

The first order year of each customer defines the cohort. The three yearly cohorts have 34, 30 and 25 customers. Customers who never ordered have no first order and belong to no cohort.

```sql run
WITH first_order AS (
  SELECT customer_id, MIN(order_date) AS first_order_date
  FROM orders
  GROUP BY customer_id
)
SELECT EXTRACT(YEAR FROM first_order_date)::int AS cohort_year, COUNT(*) AS customers
FROM first_order
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
```

```output
cohort_year | customers
1996 | 34
1997 | 30
1998 | 25
```

### Example 2: monthly activity of the earliest cohorts

The same idea at month level, with the age in months. In January 1996, 9 customers started. Besides the 9 customers at age 0, one returned 19 months later and one 29 months later: monthly retention in this data is sparse, which is why yearly cohorts are used in the next examples. Only the first rows are shown.

```sql run
WITH first_order AS (
  SELECT customer_id, date_trunc('month', MIN(order_date))::date AS cohort_month
  FROM orders
  GROUP BY customer_id
), activity AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT customer_id, date_trunc('month', order_date)::date AS active_month
  FROM orders
)
SELECT f.cohort_month,
       ((EXTRACT(YEAR FROM a.active_month) - EXTRACT(YEAR FROM f.cohort_month)) * 12
        + EXTRACT(MONTH FROM a.active_month) - EXTRACT(MONTH FROM f.cohort_month))::int AS months_since,
       COUNT(*) AS customers
FROM first_order AS f
JOIN activity AS a ON a.customer_id = f.customer_id
GROUP BY f.cohort_month, months_since
ORDER BY f.cohort_month, months_since
LIMIT 6;
```

```output
cohort_month | months_since | customers
1996-01-01 | 0 | 9
1996-01-01 | 19 | 1
1996-01-01 | 29 | 1
1996-04-01 | 0 | 9
1996-04-01 | 19 | 1
1996-04-01 | 29 | 1
```

### Example 3: a retention table with percentages

Yearly activity: for each cohort and age in years, count the distinct customers who ordered in that year. `FIRST_VALUE` over the cohort's rows in age order picks the age 0 count, which is the cohort size, and the percentage is the count divided by it. The 1998 cohort has only age 0 because the data ends in 1998.

```sql run
WITH first_order AS (
  SELECT customer_id, EXTRACT(YEAR FROM MIN(order_date))::int AS cohort_year
  FROM orders
  GROUP BY customer_id
), activity AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT customer_id, EXTRACT(YEAR FROM order_date)::int AS active_year
  FROM orders
), counts AS (
  SELECT f.cohort_year, a.active_year - f.cohort_year AS years_since, COUNT(*) AS active_customers
  FROM first_order AS f
  JOIN activity AS a ON a.customer_id = f.customer_id
  GROUP BY f.cohort_year, years_since
)
SELECT cohort_year, years_since, active_customers,
       ROUND(100.0 * active_customers / FIRST_VALUE(active_customers) OVER (PARTITION BY cohort_year ORDER BY years_since), 1) AS retention_pct
FROM counts
ORDER BY cohort_year, years_since;
```

```output
cohort_year | years_since | active_customers | retention_pct
1996 | 0 | 34 | 100.0
1996 | 1 | 3 | 8.8
1996 | 2 | 4 | 11.8
1997 | 0 | 30 | 100.0
1997 | 1 | 3 | 10.0
1998 | 0 | 25 | 100.0
```

### Example 4: the retention grid, one row per cohort

Conditional aggregation turns the long table into the grid analysts read: one row per cohort, one column per age. A cohort that has not been observed long enough shows 0 in the later columns, which means "not applicable yet" for 1998; keep that in mind when reading the grid.

```sql run
WITH first_order AS (
  SELECT customer_id, EXTRACT(YEAR FROM MIN(order_date))::int AS cohort_year
  FROM orders
  GROUP BY customer_id
), activity AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT customer_id, EXTRACT(YEAR FROM order_date)::int AS active_year
  FROM orders
)
SELECT f.cohort_year,
       COUNT(DISTINCT f.customer_id) AS cohort_size,
       COUNT(DISTINCT a.customer_id) FILTER (WHERE a.active_year - f.cohort_year = 1) AS year_1,
       COUNT(DISTINCT a.customer_id) FILTER (WHERE a.active_year - f.cohort_year = 2) AS year_2
FROM first_order AS f
JOIN activity AS a ON a.customer_id = f.customer_id
GROUP BY f.cohort_year
ORDER BY f.cohort_year;
```

```output
cohort_year | cohort_size | year_1 | year_2
1996 | 34 | 3 | 4
1997 | 30 | 3 | 0
1998 | 25 | 0 | 0
```

### Example 5: a fulfilment funnel for orders

Funnel steps for orders: placed, shipped (a `shipped_date` exists) and shipped on time (shipped on or before the required date). `COUNT(column)` ignores NULL, so it counts shipped orders. A step is a subset of the previous one. The step conversion divides by the previous step through `LAG`, the overall conversion divides by the first step.

```sql run
WITH steps (step_no, step, orders) AS (
  SELECT 1, 'placed', COUNT(*) FROM orders
  UNION ALL
  SELECT 2, 'shipped', COUNT(shipped_date) FROM orders
  UNION ALL
  SELECT 3, 'shipped on time', COUNT(*) FROM orders WHERE shipped_date <= required_date
)
SELECT step, orders,
       ROUND(100.0 * orders / LAG(orders) OVER (ORDER BY step_no), 1) AS pct_of_previous,
       ROUND(100.0 * orders / FIRST_VALUE(orders) OVER (ORDER BY step_no), 1) AS pct_of_first
FROM steps
ORDER BY step_no;
```

```output
step | orders | pct_of_previous | pct_of_first
placed | 100 | NULL | 100.0
shipped | 85 | 85.0 | 85.0
shipped on time | 77 | 90.6 | 77.0
```

### Example 6: a customer funnel by order count

Customers move from registered (all 91 in `customers`) to ordered at least once, at least twice, at least three times. Each customer's order count comes from a `LEFT JOIN` so that customers without orders (FISSA and PARIS) stay in the first step. `COUNT(*) FILTER` builds the steps, and `UNION ALL` plus `LAG` computes the conversions.

```sql run
WITH per_customer AS (
  SELECT c.customer_id, COUNT(o.order_id) AS n_orders
  FROM customers AS c
  LEFT JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
  GROUP BY c.customer_id
), steps (step_no, step, customers) AS (
  SELECT 1, 'registered', COUNT(*) FROM per_customer
  UNION ALL SELECT 2, 'ordered once', COUNT(*) FROM per_customer WHERE n_orders >= 1
  UNION ALL SELECT 3, 'ordered twice', COUNT(*) FROM per_customer WHERE n_orders >= 2
  UNION ALL SELECT 4, 'ordered three times', COUNT(*) FROM per_customer WHERE n_orders >= 3
)
SELECT step, customers,
       ROUND(100.0 * customers / LAG(customers) OVER (ORDER BY step_no), 1) AS pct_of_previous,
       ROUND(100.0 * customers / FIRST_VALUE(customers) OVER (ORDER BY step_no), 1) AS pct_of_first
FROM steps
ORDER BY step_no;
```

```output
step | customers | pct_of_previous | pct_of_first
registered | 91 | NULL | 100.0
ordered once | 89 | 97.8 | 97.8
ordered twice | 10 | 11.2 | 11.0
ordered three times | 1 | 10.0 | 1.1
```

## Real-world applications

**Judging acquisition quality.** A retention grid (Examples 3 and 4) shows whether customers acquired in one period come back as often as earlier cohorts. Marketing compares cohorts from different campaigns this way.

**Finding where users drop out.** The funnel in Example 6 shows where customers stop: the biggest loss between the first and second order tells the team to work on the repeat-purchase experience.

**Operational funnels.** Example 5 follows orders through fulfilment; the same shape is used for lead to opportunity to deal, or signup to activation to payment.

**Forecasting revenue.** Retention percentages per age feed customer-lifetime-value models: expected orders in year 2 are the cohort size times the retention rate times the average order frequency.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- A cohort is a group of customers sharing the period of their first event; derive it with `MIN(date)` per customer, then truncate to a period.
- Each customer must be in exactly one cohort; count customers with `COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)`.
- Retention = active customers at age n divided by the cohort size (age 0).
- `FILTER` with conditional aggregation pivots ages into columns.
- A funnel's steps are nested subsets; conversion is shown both from the previous step and from the first.
- Check how long each cohort has been observed before comparing retention.

### Common mistakes

- Counting orders instead of customers: use `COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)`.
- Joining customers to all their orders and then taking the cohort from the order row, which puts one customer in several cohorts. Compute the first order per customer first.
- Defining funnel steps as "exactly" instead of "at least", so the steps are not nested and conversions exceed 100 percent.
- Comparing a young cohort's zero in a later column with an old cohort's real retention.
- Using an `INNER JOIN` from customers to orders in a funnel, which silently drops the customers who never ordered from step 1.
- Integer division in percentages: write `100.0 *` to avoid truncation.

### Revision notes

- Cohort: `MIN(order_date)` per customer, then `date_trunc` or `EXTRACT`.
- Age: later period minus cohort period; age 0 is the cohort size.
- Grid: `COUNT(DISTINCT ...) FILTER (WHERE age = n)`.
- Funnel: counts per step, `LAG` for step conversion, `FIRST_VALUE` for overall conversion.
