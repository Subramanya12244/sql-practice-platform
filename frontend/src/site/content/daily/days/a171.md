## Objectives

- Plan and run a timed mock interview of 60 minutes with sections for SQL, Excel and Power BI, statistics and a case, and a short Python task.
- Score a mock with a weighted rubric, record the evidence for each score, and turn the result into a ranked list of weak areas.
- Check a portfolio project against a checklist (question, data, cleaning log, checks, charts, caveats, reproducibility) and fix the gaps.
- Review the analyst skills of the programme so far and write a short plan for the next two weeks.

## Understand

### What a checkpoint day is for

This day builds no new technique. It checks whether the skills of the analyst path work together under time pressure and whether you can show them. A mock interview is the best test, because it exposes the habits that notebooks hide: going quiet, skipping the clarifying question, not testing an answer, or running out of time on the first of four tasks. A portfolio review is the second test: it asks whether a stranger could understand what you did, trust the numbers and reproduce them. Both use the same method as every earlier day: define what good looks like, measure against it with evidence, and write down what to change.

### The mock interview

Run it alone with a timer, or with a friend who reads the questions. Use 60 minutes: five minutes of introduction (who you are and one project in two sentences), 15 minutes of SQL (two questions from Day 167), 10 minutes of Excel and Power BI (Day 168), 10 minutes of statistics (Day 169), 15 minutes of Python and pandas (Day 170), and five minutes for your questions and close. Where possible, record yourself and listen back. For each question follow the routine from the SQL day (clarify, grain, plan, write, test, explain), and speak your reasoning. When time runs out, say what you would do next rather than stopping. Treat a wrong answer that you notice and repair as a good outcome: interviewers value self-correction.

### Scoring with a rubric

A **rubric** lists criteria and describes each score level, so a score is tied to evidence and not to a feeling. A useful set for analyst interviews has five criteria: correctness of the result, clarity of explanation, handling of edge cases and checks, structure and pacing, and communication of assumptions. Score each from 1 (weak) to 4 (strong) and write one line of evidence for every score ("did not test for NULLs in the NOT IN query"). Weights reflect what matters most: correctness and checks count more than style. A weighted score is the sum of weight times score divided by the maximum, expressed as a percentage. Compare mocks over time: the list of criteria that moved matters more than the total.

### The portfolio checklist

A portfolio project is a small piece of work that shows how you think. A reviewer should find, within a few minutes: the **question** the project answers and who would use it; the **data** source and how it was loaded; a **cleaning log** with row counts; the **checks** you ran (duplicates, keys, totals reconciled); the **analysis** with the method and its limits; **charts** with titles that state findings; **caveats** that say what the data cannot show; and **reproducibility**, meaning a script or notebook that runs from the raw data to the result, with a README that says how. The projects of this programme (the EDA project, the visualisation report, the analytics mini-project) already have these parts. The review is to confirm each is visible and to fix the gaps, not to build something new.

### Reviewing the skills so far

The analyst path so far has covered Excel and Power Query (Days 91 to 100), statistics and exploratory analysis (Days 101 to 113), visualisation (Days 114 to 123), Power BI and DAX (Days 124 onward), business analytics cases and the data-preparation workflow (including the mini-project on Day 166), and four days of interview practice. A simple self-assessment rates each area from 1 to 5, then picks the three lowest and assigns a concrete revision task to each. Honest ratings matter more than high ones: a rating is only useful if you could demonstrate the skill on a blank page.

### Common misconception

People often think the way to improve is to read more. For interviews and portfolios, improvement comes from timed practice with feedback and from rereading your own work with a checklist. One mock with a written rubric teaches more than three more hours of reading.

## Learn by example

### Example 1: The mock interview timetable

The sections and their minutes are listed, and a short function adds start and end times from a given start. The total must be 60 minutes, which the program checks, so the plan cannot silently overrun.

```python run
schedule = [("Intro", 5), ("SQL", 15), ("Excel and Power BI", 10), ("Statistics", 10), ("Python and pandas", 15), ("Questions and close", 5)]
assert sum(m for _, m in schedule) == 60


def timetable(schedule, start_minute=9 * 60):
    rows, t = [], start_minute
    for name, minutes in schedule:
        rows.append((f"{t // 60:02d}:{t % 60:02d}", f"{(t + minutes) // 60:02d}:{(t + minutes) % 60:02d}", name))
        t += minutes
    return rows


for begin, end, name in timetable(schedule):
    print(f"{begin}-{end}  {name}")
print("total minutes:", sum(m for _, m in schedule))
```

```output
09:00-09:05  Intro
09:05-09:20  SQL
09:20-09:30  Excel and Power BI
09:30-09:40  Statistics
09:40-09:55  Python and pandas
09:55-10:00  Questions and close
total minutes: 60
```

### Example 2: A weighted rubric

Five criteria have weights, and two mock rounds are scored from 1 to 4. The weighted percentage and the evidence for the weakest criterion are reported. Notice that round 2 improves on checks, which has the highest weight, so its total rises more than the plain average of the scores would suggest.

```python run
weights = {"correctness": 3, "explanation": 2, "checks": 3, "pacing": 1, "assumptions": 1}
rounds = {
    "mock 1": {"correctness": 3, "explanation": 3, "checks": 1, "pacing": 2, "assumptions": 2},
    "mock 2": {"correctness": 3, "explanation": 3, "checks": 3, "pacing": 2, "assumptions": 3},
}


def weighted_percent(scores):
    return sum(weights[c] * scores[c] for c in weights) / (4 * sum(weights.values())) * 100


for name, scores in rounds.items():
    plain = sum(scores.values()) / (4 * len(scores)) * 100
    print(f"{name}: weighted {weighted_percent(scores):.1f}% | plain average {plain:.1f}%")
weakest = min(rounds["mock 2"], key=lambda c: (rounds["mock 2"][c], c))
print("weakest criterion in mock 2:", weakest)
```

```output
mock 1: weighted 55.0% | plain average 55.0%
mock 2: weighted 72.5% | plain average 70.0%
weakest criterion in mock 2: pacing
```

### Example 3: Self-assessment and weak areas

Ratings for ten areas of the programme are sorted to find the three weakest, with ties resolved alphabetically so the result is stable. Each weak area maps to a revision lesson, and the plan lists them with the minutes to spend. The ratings are invented; use your own.

```python run
ratings = {
    "Excel lookups and pivots": 4, "Power Query": 3, "Statistics and tests": 2, "EDA": 4, "Chart choice": 4,
    "Matplotlib and seaborn": 3, "Star schema": 2, "DAX measures": 2, "SQL windows": 3, "pandas merge and groupby": 4,
}
lessons = {"Statistics and tests": "Days 101 to 111 and Day 169", "Star schema": "Days 126 and 127", "DAX measures": "Days 129 and 130",
           "Power Query": "Days 98, 99 and 125", "Matplotlib and seaborn": "Days 115 to 118", "SQL windows": "Day 167"}
weakest = sorted(ratings.items(), key=lambda kv: (kv[1], kv[0]))[:3]
for area, rating in weakest:
    print(f"{area} (rated {rating}): revise {lessons.get(area, 'earlier days of this area')}")
print("average rating:", round(sum(ratings.values()) / len(ratings), 2))
```

```output
DAX measures (rated 2): revise Days 129 and 130
Star schema (rated 2): revise Days 126 and 127
Statistics and tests (rated 2): revise Days 101 to 111 and Day 169
average rating: 3.1
```

### Example 4: Portfolio checklist validator

A project is described as a dictionary. The validator lists the checklist items that are missing or empty and gives a completeness score. The sample project has a good analysis but no caveats and no reproducibility note, the two gaps reviewers notice most.

```python run
CHECKLIST = ["question", "audience", "data_source", "cleaning_log", "checks", "analysis", "charts", "caveats", "reproducible", "readme"]
project = {
    "question": "Which sales channel earns the most paid revenue?",
    "audience": "Head of e-commerce",
    "data_source": "orders extract, March to August 2025",
    "cleaning_log": "505 raw rows, 500 after removing 5 duplicates, 3 orphans reported",
    "checks": ["unique order ids", "totals reconcile with the database"],
    "analysis": "SQL aggregation plus pandas growth table",
    "charts": ["revenue by channel", "monthly revenue by region"],
    "caveats": "",
    "reproducible": False,
    "readme": "How to run: python report.py",
}
missing = [item for item in CHECKLIST if not project.get(item)]
print("missing or empty:", missing)
print(f"completeness: {(len(CHECKLIST) - len(missing)) / len(CHECKLIST) * 100:.0f}%")
```

```output
missing or empty: ['caveats', 'reproducible']
completeness: 80%
```

### Example 5: A result-first answer

Behavioural questions ("tell me about a time...") are answered in four parts: situation, task, action, result, with a number in the result. This check counts the words in each part and flags an answer whose result has no number or whose situation is longer than the action, the two most common weaknesses.

```python run
import re

answer = {
    "situation": "Our weekly sales report showed two different totals for the same week.",
    "task": "I had to find the cause and fix it before the Monday meeting.",
    "action": "I compared row counts between the database and the report, found three orders with a customer id missing from the customer table, and corrected the join to keep them under an Unknown region.",
    "result": "The totals matched, 3 orders worth 1,025 pounds were no longer lost, and I added the check to the weekly script.",
}
words = {part: len(text.split()) for part, text in answer.items()}
print(words)
print("result has a number:", bool(re.search(r"\d", answer["result"])))
print("action longer than situation:", words["action"] > words["situation"])
print("total words:", sum(words.values()), "| spoken minutes at 130 words/min:", round(sum(words.values()) / 130, 1))
```

```output
{'situation': 12, 'task': 13, 'action': 33, 'result': 21}
result has a number: True
action longer than situation: True
total words: 79 | spoken minutes at 130 words/min: 0.6
```

## Real-world applications

- **Hiring preparation.** The timetable and rubric (Examples 1 and 2) turn practice into evidence, so you know which of your answers to improve before a real interview.
- **Career reviews.** A skills self-assessment (Example 3) is the same tool a manager uses in a development plan to choose training for the next quarter.
- **Portfolio reviews.** The checklist (Example 4) is what a hiring manager looks for in the first minutes of reading a repository or a report.
- **Stakeholder updates.** The result-first structure (Example 5) is how to report a finding in a meeting: the situation in one line, the action, and a result with a number.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- A mock interview exposes habits; run it timed, speak aloud and record it.
- Score with a weighted rubric and write the evidence for every score.
- A portfolio project should show question, data, cleaning log, checks, analysis, charts, caveats and reproducibility.
- Self-assess honestly, pick the three weakest areas and assign concrete tasks.
- Improvement comes from timed practice with feedback more than from more reading.

### Common mistakes

- Practising without a clock. Symptom: perfect answers that take twice the allowed time. Fix: a timetable and a visible timer.
- Scoring from a feeling. Symptom: a high score and no idea what to change. Fix: a rubric with evidence per criterion.
- A portfolio with code but no question or caveats. Symptom: a reviewer cannot tell what the project proves. Fix: a README with the question, result and limits.
- Rating every skill 4 or 5. Symptom: no weak areas to work on. Fix: rate by what you could do on a blank page.

### Revision notes

- Mock: 60 minutes, six sections, timer, recording.
- Rubric: criteria, weights, 1 to 4, evidence; weighted percentage.
- Checklist: question, audience, data, cleaning log, checks, analysis, charts, caveats, reproducibility, README.
- Gap finder: lowest three ratings, one revision task each.

## Revision task

Check yourself: at least one criterion that you named as weak must have improved, and your portfolio checklist must show no missing items for the project you present.
