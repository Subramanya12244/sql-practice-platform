## Objectives

- Define fact table, dimension table, grain, key and measure, and classify the columns of the sample model.
- Explain why one wide flat table causes redundancy, update anomalies and wrong totals, and show it with numbers.

- Describe the star schema, how it differs from a snowflake, and how the relationships look in Power BI Model view.

## Understand

### Facts and dimensions

Data for analysis falls into two kinds. A **fact** is something that happened and can be measured: an order line with a quantity and a price. A **dimension** describes the things the facts are about: which customer, which product, which day. Facts are numbers you add up (quantity, revenue); dimensions are the attributes you group and filter by (country, segment, category, month). A **fact table** has one row per event at a stated **grain**, the level of detail one row represents. In the sample model the grain of `Sales` is one row per order (`order_id` is unique), and each row carries the keys `customer_id` and `product_id`, the date `order_date`, and the measures `quantity` and `unit_price`. A **dimension table** has one row per thing, with a unique **key** and descriptive columns: `Customers(customer_id, customer_name, country, segment)` and `Products(product_id, product_name, category, unit_cost)`. The calendar is a dimension too (Day 128). Stating the grain is the first design decision, because every other choice must be consistent with it; mixing grains (say, adding monthly targets to an order-level table) is a common source of wrong totals.

### The star schema

In a **star schema** the fact table sits in the centre and each dimension table connects to it directly, with one relationship per dimension, which draws a star shape in Model view. Filters flow outward from the dimensions to the fact: choosing `country = UK` in a slicer on `Customers` filters `Sales` to the orders of UK customers, and every measure on `Sales` then responds. This is the structure Power BI is designed for: its engine compresses and filters tables most efficiently when facts are narrow and numeric and dimensions are small and descriptive. A **snowflake** schema normalises the dimensions further (for example, `Products` links to a separate `Categories` table). It saves a little storage but adds relationships and joins, and in Power BI the usual advice is to flatten such chains back into one dimension table so that one slicer filters directly.

### Why not one flat table

The alternative is a single wide table where each order row also carries the customer's name, country and segment, and the product's name, category and cost. It is easy to build (a few merges), which is why many analysts start there, but it has three problems. **Redundancy**: each customer's country is stored on every one of their orders, so the same facts are written many times. **Update anomalies**: if a customer moves from the UK to Spain, you must change every one of their rows; if you miss one, the table says the customer is in two countries, and totals by country are silently wrong. **Wrong counts**: with customers repeated on every row, counting rows counts orders, not customers, and a customer count needs a distinct count. Wide tables also grow large: 400 rows times twelve columns is more cells than 400 rows times six plus two small dimensions. A star schema stores each fact once, in the right place.

### What goes in each table

Place a column by asking what it describes. If it varies from order to order (quantity, unit price, order date), it belongs in the fact table. If it describes the customer, it belongs in `Customers`; if it describes the product, in `Products`. Columns that describe the same entity move together. Keep the key columns in the fact table for relationships, and in Power BI hide them from the report view so report authors use the dimension columns instead. Also beware of **non-additive** columns: `quantity` can be summed across orders, but `unit_price` cannot (the sum of prices means nothing). A price is averaged as a weighted average: total revenue divided by total quantity. Revenue is `quantity * unit_price`, computed per row and then summed.

### Relationships in Model view

In Power BI Model view you create a relationship by dragging `customer_id` from `Customers` onto `customer_id` in `Sales`, or through `Manage relationships`. A relationship connects one column in each table. In a star schema every relationship is **one-to-many**: the dimension (one) side has unique values; the fact (many) side repeats them. Power BI shows `1` and `*` at the line ends and an arrow showing the filter direction, from the one side to the many side. The next lesson covers cardinality and direction in detail.

### Misconceptions and why it matters

One misconception is that flat tables are simpler for the report author. They are simpler to create, but every question beyond the first becomes harder: counting customers, fixing a country, adding a second fact table. Another is that the star schema is only for big data; it matters most for correctness, even at 400 rows. In analyst work the star schema is what lets many different visuals share the same filters and the same measures, and it is the shape that DAX (Days 129 to 137) expects.

## Syntax and key functions

Splitting a flat table into a star in pandas:

```python
customers = flat[["customer_id", "customer_name", "country", "segment"]].drop_duplicates()
products = flat[["product_id", "product_name", "category", "unit_cost"]].drop_duplicates()
fact = flat[["order_id", "order_date", "customer_id", "product_id", "quantity", "unit_price"]]
rebuilt = fact.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
```

In Power BI, the same split is made in Power Query (Day 125) by referencing the loaded query, choosing columns, and removing duplicates; the relationship is then created in Model view:

```text
Model view: drag Customers[customer_id] onto Sales[customer_id]
            drag Products[product_id]   onto Sales[product_id]
Check: cardinality One to many (1:*), cross-filter direction Single, relationship active
```

| Term | Meaning | Example in the sample model |
|---|---|---|
| Fact table | Rows of events at one grain | `Sales`, one row per order |
| Dimension | Descriptive entities | `Customers`, `Products`, `Calendar` |
| Key | Unique id of a dimension row | `customer_id`, `product_id` |
| Foreign key | The key as stored in the fact | `Sales[customer_id]` |
| Measure | A number you aggregate | `quantity`, revenue |
| Grain | What one fact row is | one order |
| Star / snowflake | Dimensions connect directly / through other dimensions | star: `Sales` to `Customers` |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: The cost of the flat table

The flat table joins every attribute onto every order. Counting cells (rows times columns) for the flat table and for the star shows how much the flat table repeats. The same customer attributes appear once per order instead of once per customer.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_model():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    customers = pd.DataFrame({
        "customer_id": np.arange(1, 41),
        "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
        "country": rng.choice(["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"], size=40),
        "segment": rng.choice(["Consumer", "Corporate", "Small Business"], size=40, p=[0.5, 0.3, 0.2]),
    })
    names = ["Desk", "Chair", "Bookcase", "Laptop", "Monitor", "Phone", "Notebook", "Pen Set", "Stapler", "Printer", "Lamp", "Cabinet"]
    categories = ["Furniture", "Furniture", "Furniture", "Technology", "Technology", "Technology",
                  "Office Supplies", "Office Supplies", "Office Supplies", "Technology", "Furniture", "Furniture"]
    products = pd.DataFrame({
        "product_id": np.arange(1, 13),
        "product_name": names,
        "category": categories,
        "unit_cost": rng.integers(5, 120, size=12),
    })
    sales = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1401),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, 400), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, 400),
        "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, 400),
        "quantity": rng.integers(1, 8, 400),
    })
    cost = products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]
    sales["unit_price"] = (cost[sales["product_id"]].to_numpy() * (1.3 + 0.5 * rng.random(400))).round(2)
    sales = sales.sort_values(["order_date", "order_id"]).reset_index(drop=True)
    dates = pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31", freq="D")
    calendar = pd.DataFrame({
        "date": dates,
        "year": dates.year,
        "quarter": dates.quarter,
        "month_number": dates.month,
        "month_name": dates.strftime("%B"),
    })
    return sales, customers, products, calendar


def flat_table():
    sales, customers, products, calendar = make_model()
    return sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")


sales, customers, products, calendar = make_model()
flat = flat_table()
star_cells = sales.size + customers.size + products.size
print("flat:", flat.shape, "->", flat.size, "cells")
print("star:", sales.size, "+", customers.size, "+", products.size, "=", star_cells, "cells")
print("flat is", round(flat.size / star_cells, 2), "times larger")
print("country stored", flat["country"].notna().sum(), "times for", flat["customer_id"].nunique(), "customers")
```

```output
flat: (400, 12) -> 4800 cells
star: 2400 + 160 + 48 = 2608 cells
flat is 1.84 times larger
country stored 400 times for 40 customers
```

### Example 2: Split the flat table and prove nothing was lost

Dimensions come from selecting their columns and dropping duplicate rows; the fact table keeps the keys and measures. Merging them back must give the flat table exactly. This is the test for a correct split: if it fails, a dimension table has a key that does not describe one entity.

### Example 3: The update anomaly

Customer 7 moves to a different country (Spain, or the UK if they are already in Spain). In the flat table every one of their order rows must change, and a partial update leaves the customer in two countries. In the star, one row of `Customers` changes and every order follows through the relationship.

### Example 4: Additive and non-additive columns

Quantity can be summed. A unit price cannot: summing prices is meaningless, and the simple average of the prices gives each order equal weight regardless of its quantity. The right average price is total revenue divided by total quantity. The two averages differ because large orders can have different prices.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_model():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

        "customer_id": np.arange(1, 41),
        "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
        "country": rng.choice(["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"], size=40),
        "segment": rng.choice(["Consumer", "Corporate", "Small Business"], size=40, p=[0.5, 0.3, 0.2]),
    })
    names = ["Desk", "Chair", "Bookcase", "Laptop", "Monitor", "Phone", "Notebook", "Pen Set", "Stapler", "Printer", "Lamp", "Cabinet"]
    categories = ["Furniture", "Furniture", "Furniture", "Technology", "Technology", "Technology",
                  "Office Supplies", "Office Supplies", "Office Supplies", "Technology", "Furniture", "Furniture"]
    products = pd.DataFrame({
        "product_id": np.arange(1, 13),
        "product_name": names,
        "category": categories,
        "unit_cost": rng.integers(5, 120, size=12),
```

### Example 5: A star join gives the same answer

Revenue by country and category is the typical star query: the fact joined to two dimensions, grouped by dimension attributes. rows = con.execute("""

## Real-world applications

- **Retail and finance reporting.** Sales, stock and budget are separate fact tables that share `Customers`, `Products` and `Calendar` dimensions, so one slicer filters all of them.
- **Fixing data in one place.** A correction to a customer's segment (Example 3) is one change in the dimension, and every existing and future order follows.
- **Correct averages.** Distinguishing additive measures from prices (Example 4) is what stops a dashboard from reporting a meaningless sum of prices or an unweighted average.
- **Performance.** Models with narrow fact tables and small dimensions (Example 1) load faster, use less memory and refresh more quickly than wide flat tables.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Facts are events with measures at a stated grain; dimensions describe entities with unique keys.
- A star has the fact in the centre and one-to-many relationships from each dimension.
- A flat table repeats attributes, allows update anomalies and invites wrong counts.
- Split by asking what each column describes; prove the split by merging back.
- Quantities add; prices need weighted averages.

### Common mistakes

- Starting from one flat table for everything. Symptom: customer counts that are order counts, countries that disagree. Fix: split into fact and dimensions.
- A dimension key that is not unique. Symptom: the merge adds rows, revenue inflates. Fix: deduplicate, or choose the right key.
- Summing unit prices. Symptom: an absurd total, or a simple average that ignores quantities. Fix: weighted average, revenue divided by quantity.
- Mixed grains in one fact table. Symptom: monthly targets repeated on every order. Fix: separate fact tables joined through shared dimensions.

### Revision notes

- Grain first: Sales is one row per order.
- Star: fact in the middle, dimensions around it, `1:*` relationships.
- Split test: merge back and compare with the flat table.
- Weighted average price = revenue / quantity.
