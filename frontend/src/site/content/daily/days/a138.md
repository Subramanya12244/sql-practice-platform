## Objectives

- Describe an audience by the decision it must make, and choose the amount of detail to match.
- Structure a data story as situation, complication and resolution, with the answer first.
- Write action titles that state the finding, and apply the rule of one message per slide.
- Compute the facts of a story in pandas so that every number in a headline is traceable.
- Check a slide outline against written rules with a Python function.

## Understand

### Analysis is not yet a story

An analysis answers a question; a **data story** gets a particular person to understand a finding and do something about it. The two are different pieces of work. Analysis is complete when the numbers are right. A story is complete when a reader who spent two minutes with it can say what happened, why it matters and what to do. The tools in Power BI or pandas are the same for both, but the output is organised differently: an analysis follows the order in which you found things, and a story follows the order in which the reader needs to hear them. Many strong analyses are ignored because the reader had to work out the point for themselves.

### Start with the audience

The first question is not "what did I find?" but "who is reading and what must they decide?". Describe the audience in one line: the **role** (commercial director, finance analyst, regional manager), the **decision** they own (shift budget, approve a hire, change a price) and the **time** they have (a two-minute email, a ten-minute meeting, a read-at-leisure report). That description fixes three things. The amount of detail: an executive needs the three findings that change the decision, an analyst wants the method and the table. The vocabulary: use their terms, such as "orders" or "revenue", and not the names of columns or DAX measures. And the evidence: a sceptical finance reader needs a reconciliation; a sales manager needs the customers behind the number. A common misconception is that more detail makes a story more credible. Beyond what the decision needs, detail hides the point and invites questions about the wrong things.

### Situation, complication, resolution

A reliable structure comes from consulting practice: **situation, complication, resolution (SCR)**. The **situation** is the shared, uncontroversial background that the reader agrees with: revenue grew by 65.7% this year. The **complication** is what has changed or what is wrong, the reason the story needs telling: growth was uneven, and August fell by 15.9% from July. The **resolution** is what you recommend: investigate August and rebalance effort towards the slowest market. SCR works because it mirrors how people think: they accept the context, feel the tension, and are then ready for the answer. A story with a situation and no complication is a report; one with a complication and no resolution leaves the reader worried and without a next step.

Put the answer first. This is sometimes called the **pyramid principle**: the top of the story is the conclusion, with supporting points underneath and the data under those. A busy reader who stops after the first slide or sentence has still got the recommendation. The chronological order of your analysis (how you got there) goes in the appendix.

### Action titles and one message per slide

A topic title names the subject: "Revenue by country". An **action title** states the finding in a sentence: "Spain grew 62.6%, the slowest of four markets". The reader gets the point from the titles alone, and the chart under the title is the evidence for it. A good test is to read only the titles in order: they should tell the story as a short paragraph. A working rule for the course: an action title has at least six words, contains a number, and contains a verb that says what moved (grew, fell, rose, dropped, accounts for).

**One message per slide** (or per page of a report, or per paragraph of an email) means that each slide proves a single claim with a single chart. When two claims share a slide, the reader cannot tell which one the chart supports, and the presenter loses the room while explaining both. If you have two messages, make two slides. The rule also keeps the chart honest: a chart that was built to show one comparison, with the relevant bar highlighted, is clearer than one that tries to show everything.

### Numbers must be traceable

Every number in a headline should come from a computation you can repeat. The best practice is to compute the facts in code first, store them in a dictionary, and build the titles from it with formatted strings. That way a number cannot drift from the data, a reviewer can rerun the code, and when the data refreshes the headline updates with it. In this lesson the data is the sample sales model with a 2024 year added, as on Day 131.

### Why it matters

Analysts are judged by the decisions they change. A finding that is not heard has the same effect as a finding that was never made. Structure is the cheapest way of increasing the impact of work that has already been done, and the habits (audience first, answer first, action titles, one message) apply to a dashboard, an email and a presentation alike.

## Syntax and key functions

There is no new tool syntax. The reference lists the story elements and the course rule for each.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Audience line | Role, decision, time available. |
| Situation | A fact the reader already accepts. |
| Complication | What changed or what is wrong, with a number. |
| Resolution | A recommendation the reader can act on. |
| Action title | At least six words, a number and a verb of movement. |
| Slide | One message, one visual, one action title. |

A fact dictionary and a formatted headline:

```python
facts = {"growth": 65.7, "worst_month": "August", "worst_drop": 15.9}
title = f"Revenue grew {facts['growth']:.1f}% but {facts['worst_month']} fell {facts['worst_drop']:.1f}%"
```

## Learn by example

### Example 1: The audience decides the detail

Compute the findings once, then show how much of them each audience receives.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
countries = ["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"]
cats = ["Hardware", "Software", "Services"]
customers = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": range(1, 41),
    "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
    "country": [countries[i % 4] for i in range(40)],
    "segment": [["Retail", "Corporate"][(i // 4) % 2] for i in range(40)],
})
products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, 13),
    "product_name": [f"Product {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)],
    "category": [cats[i % 3] for i in range(12)],
    "unit_cost": [float(10 + 5 * i) for i in range(12)],
})
n = 400
sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n + 1),
    "order_date": pd.to_datetime("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, n),
    "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, n),
    "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
})
sales["unit_price"] = (sales["product_id"].map(products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]) * 1.5).round(2)
calendar = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31")})
calendar["year"] = calendar["date"].dt.year
calendar["quarter"] = calendar["date"].dt.quarter
calendar["month_number"] = calendar["date"].dt.month
calendar["month_name"] = calendar["date"].dt.strftime("%B")
sales["revenue"] = sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"]
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
s24 = sales.copy()
s24["order_date"] = s24["order_date"] - pd.DateOffset(years=1)
s24["quantity"] = (s24["quantity"] + 1) // 2
s24["revenue"] = s24["quantity"] * s24["unit_price"]
both = pd.concat([s24, sales], ignore_index=True)
monthly = both.groupby(both["order_date"].dt.to_period("M"))["revenue"].sum()
t24 = s24.merge(customers, on="customer_id")
c24 = t24.groupby("country")["revenue"].sum()
c25 = fact.groupby("country")["revenue"].sum()
growth = ((c25 / c24 - 1) * 100).round(1).sort_values()
print(growth.to_string())

audiences = {
    "commercial director": {"findings": 2, "table": False},
    "finance analyst": {"findings": 4, "table": True},
}
for name, spec in audiences.items():
    shown = list(growth.index[: spec["findings"]])
    print(name, shown, "with table" if spec["table"] else "no table")
```

```output
country
Spain      62.6
Germany    65.8
France     66.7
UK         68.1
commercial director ['Spain', 'Germany'] no table
finance analyst ['Spain', 'Germany', 'France', 'UK'] with table
```

Spain grew slowest at 62.6% and the UK fastest at 68.1%. The director gets the two slowest markets and no table; the analyst gets all four markets and a table behind them. The data is identical: the audience decides how much to show.

### Example 2: Compute the facts, then write SCR

Build a dictionary of facts and assemble situation, complication and resolution from it.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
countries = ["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"]
cats = ["Hardware", "Software", "Services"]
customers = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": range(1, 41),
    "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
    "country": [countries[i % 4] for i in range(40)],
    "segment": [["Retail", "Corporate"][(i // 4) % 2] for i in range(40)],
})
products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, 13),
    "product_name": [f"Product {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)],
    "category": [cats[i % 3] for i in range(12)],
    "unit_cost": [float(10 + 5 * i) for i in range(12)],
})
n = 400
sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n + 1),
    "order_date": pd.to_datetime("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, n),
    "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, n),
    "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
})
sales["unit_price"] = (sales["product_id"].map(products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]) * 1.5).round(2)
calendar = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31")})
calendar["year"] = calendar["date"].dt.year
calendar["quarter"] = calendar["date"].dt.quarter
calendar["month_number"] = calendar["date"].dt.month
calendar["month_name"] = calendar["date"].dt.strftime("%B")
sales["revenue"] = sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"]
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
s24 = sales.copy()
s24["order_date"] = s24["order_date"] - pd.DateOffset(years=1)
s24["quantity"] = (s24["quantity"] + 1) // 2
s24["revenue"] = s24["quantity"] * s24["unit_price"]
both = pd.concat([s24, sales], ignore_index=True)
monthly = both.groupby(both["order_date"].dt.to_period("M"))["revenue"].sum()
rev25 = float(monthly["2025"].sum())
rev24 = float(monthly["2024"].sum())
mom = monthly["2025"].pct_change() * 100
worst = mom.idxmin()
facts = {
    "revenue": rev25,
    "growth": (rev25 / rev24 - 1) * 100,
    "worst_month": worst.strftime("%B"),
    "worst_drop": -float(mom.min()),
    "slowest_country": "Spain",
}
print(f"Situation: revenue grew {facts['growth']:.1f}% to {facts['revenue']:,.2f}.")
print(f"Complication: {facts['worst_month']} fell {facts['worst_drop']:.1f}% from the month before.")
print(f"Resolution: find out what happened in {facts['worst_month']} and review the plan in {facts['slowest_country']}.")
```

```output
Situation: revenue grew 65.7% to 67,132.50.
Complication: August fell 15.9% from the month before.
Resolution: find out what happened in August and review the plan in Spain.
```

Every number is produced by the code: 65.7% growth to 67,132.50 and a fall of 15.9% in August. The situation is something the reader agrees with, the complication names the tension with a number, and the resolution is an action with an object. If the data is refreshed and a different month is the worst, the text follows.

### Example 3: Topic titles against action titles

Apply the course rule for an action title to a list of candidates.

```python run
import re

VERBS = {"grew", "fell", "rose", "dropped", "accounts", "slowed", "peaked"}

def is_action_title(title):
    words = title.split()
    has_number = bool(re.search(r"\d", title))
    has_verb = any(w.lower().strip(",.") in VERBS for w in words)
    return len(words) >= 6 and has_number and has_verb

titles = [
    "Revenue by country",
    "Spain grew 62.6%, the slowest of four markets",
    "August fell 15.9% from July",
    "Germany accounts for 29.0% of revenue, the largest share",
]
for t in titles:
    print("action" if is_action_title(t) else "topic ", t)
```

```output
topic  Revenue by country
action Spain grew 62.6%, the slowest of four markets
topic  August fell 15.9% from July
action Germany accounts for 29.0% of revenue, the largest share
```

The first title only names a subject. The second and fourth pass the rule. The third has a number and a verb but only five words, so the rule rejects it; a rule is a screening tool, and you might choose to accept this short title or add the context ("August fell 15.9% from July, the largest monthly drop"). The point of the exercise is to notice what a title commits to.

### Example 4: One message per slide

A slide specification has one title, a list of messages and a count of charts. Check the outline.

```python run
slides = [
    {"title": "Revenue grew 65.7% to 67,132.50 in 2025", "messages": ["growth"], "charts": 1},
    {"title": "Spain grew 62.6%, the slowest market, and August fell 15.9%", "messages": ["spain slow", "august dip"], "charts": 2},
    {"title": "Germany accounts for 29.0% of revenue", "messages": ["germany share"], "charts": 1},
]

def check_slides(slides):
    problems = []
    for i, s in enumerate(slides, start=1):
        if len(s["messages"]) != 1:
            problems.append(f"slide {i}: {len(s['messages'])} messages")
        if s["charts"] > 1:
            problems.append(f"slide {i}: {s['charts']} charts")
    return problems

print(check_slides(slides))
split = [{"title": "Spain grew 62.6%, the slowest market", "messages": ["spain slow"], "charts": 1},
         {"title": "August fell 15.9% from July", "messages": ["august dip"], "charts": 1}]
print(check_slides(split))
```

```output
['slide 2: 2 messages', 'slide 2: 2 charts']
[]
```

Slide 2 carries two messages and two charts, so the checker flags both. Splitting it into two slides, each with one claim and one chart, passes. The cost is one more slide; the gain is that each chart supports exactly one sentence.

### Example 5: Assemble the outline, answer first

Order the slides: the resolution first, then the situation and the complication as support, and the data in the appendix.

```python run
outline = [
    ("Resolution", "Investigate August and review Spain before the next planning round"),
    ("Situation", "Revenue grew 65.7% to 67,132.50 in 2025"),
    ("Complication", "August fell 15.9% from July and Spain grew slowest at 62.6%"),
    ("Appendix", "Revenue by country and month, with the method"),
]
for number, (role, title) in enumerate(outline, start=1):
    print(f"{number}. [{role}] {title}")
titles_only = " ".join(t for r, t in outline if r != "Appendix")
print(len(titles_only.split()))
```

```output
1. [Resolution] Investigate August and review Spain before the next planning round
2. [Situation] Revenue grew 65.7% to 67,132.50 in 2025
3. [Complication] August fell 15.9% from July and Spain grew slowest at 62.6%
4. [Appendix] Revenue by country and month, with the method
28
```

Reading only the first three titles gives the whole story in 28 words: what to do, why, and what changed. That is the test of a good outline. The appendix holds what a sceptical reader may want to check, so the method is available without being in the way.

## Real-world applications

- **Monthly business review.** A one-page email with a recommendation first, a growth fact and a complication, built from the computed facts (Examples 2 and 5).
- **Dashboard captions.** Replacing topic titles on a dashboard with action titles that update from a measure tells readers what to look at (Example 3).
- **Presentations to executives.** Applying one message per slide keeps a ten-minute slot on the decision (Example 4).
- **Hand-offs between analysts.** A stored fact dictionary makes the story reproducible when the data is refreshed (Example 2).

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- A story is organised for the reader's decision, not for the order of discovery.
- Describe the audience by role, decision and time, and size the detail to it.
- Situation, complication, resolution, with the answer first.
- Action titles state the finding; one message and one visual per slide.
- Compute facts in code and build headlines from them.

### Common mistakes

- Starting with the method and the data and putting the conclusion on the last slide. Put the recommendation first.
- Using topic titles ("Revenue by country") so that the reader has to find the point in the chart.
- Putting two findings on one slide, which confuses which one the chart shows.
- Typing numbers into slides by hand, so a refresh makes the headline wrong.
- A complication with no number, which sounds like an opinion.

### Revision notes

- Audience: role, decision, time.
- SCR; answer first (pyramid).
- Action title: sentence with a number and a verb.
- One message, one chart per slide.
- Facts dictionary, formatted headlines.
