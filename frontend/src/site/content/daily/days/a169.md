## Objectives

- Answer probability interview questions by enumerating outcomes, applying conditional probability and Bayes' rule, and checking the result by simulation.
- Run and interpret an A/B test on two conversion rates, and a two-sample t-test, and say what a p-value does and does not mean.
- Decompose a change in a business metric into its factors and name the factor that explains most of it.
- Structure an open metric case (a number moved: why?) in a repeatable order, and state assumptions and next steps.

## Understand

### The three kinds of question

Analyst interviews mix three kinds of statistical and analytical questions. **Probability puzzles** ("two dice, what is the chance the sum is 8?", "a test is 90 percent accurate and a person tests positive, how likely are they to be ill?") check that you can reason from first principles. **Statistics questions** ("how would you test whether the new checkout is better?", "what is a p-value?") check that you know the standard tools and their limits. **Case questions** ("daily active users fell 8 percent last week, what do you do?") check how you structure an open problem and whether you reach a decision. The best preparation is the same for all three: work a small number of problems carefully, verify the answers by computing them, and practise saying the reasoning aloud in a fixed order.

### Probability by counting

Most interview probability questions are solved by listing equally likely outcomes and counting. With two fair dice there are 36 equally likely pairs, and five of them sum to 8, so the probability is 5/36. **Conditional probability**, written P(A | B), restricts attention to the outcomes where B happens: P(at least one six | the sum is 8) counts the pairs with sum 8 that contain a six and divides by the five pairs that sum to 8. Two events are **independent** if knowing one tells you nothing about the other, which means P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B); never assume independence without a reason. The **expected value** of a game is the sum of each outcome's value times its probability, the long-run average per play. **Bayes' rule** reverses a conditional probability: P(ill | positive) = P(positive | ill) × P(ill) / P(positive), where P(positive) adds the true positives and the false positives. Its famous lesson is that when the condition is rare, most positives can be false: with a prevalence of 1 percent, a sensitivity of 90 percent and a false positive rate of 5 percent, only about 15 percent of positives are truly ill. Always check an answer by simulation or by enumeration when you can.

### A/B tests and p-values

An **A/B test** randomly assigns users to the current version (A) and the new one (B) and compares an outcome, usually a conversion rate. The **null hypothesis** says there is no real difference. The **p-value** is the probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of seeing a difference at least as large as the one observed. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and it says nothing about whether the difference matters in business terms. A small p-value (conventionally below 0.05) means the data would be surprising under no effect. For two conversion rates the standard test is the **two-proportion z-test**: the difference in rates divided by its standard error under the pooled rate. A **confidence interval** for the difference shows the plausible range of the effect, which is more useful than the p-value alone. For a numeric outcome such as order value, the two-sample **t-test** compares means (use the Welch version, which does not assume equal variances). Good answers also mention what makes the test valid: random assignment, a sample size decided in advance, one planned primary metric, and a fixed duration rather than stopping when the p-value dips below 0.05.

### Multiple comparisons

If you run 20 independent tests at the 0.05 level when nothing is going on, the chance that at least one comes out significant is 1 − 0.95^20, about 64 percent. This is why slicing an A/B result into many segments and reporting the one that "worked" is a classic error. Corrections such as dividing the threshold by the number of tests (Bonferroni) reduce the risk, and the better habit is to state the hypotheses before looking.

### Metric cases

In a case question the number moved and you must find out why. Use the same sequence each time. **Define and check the metric**: what exactly is measured, over what period, has the definition or the tracking changed, and is the movement larger than normal noise? **Decompose** it into factors: revenue is visitors × conversion rate × average order value, so a fall in revenue must come from one or more of them. **Segment** to find where it happened (device, country, channel, new versus returning customers). **Generate hypotheses** (an outage, a price change, a campaign ending, seasonality, a data problem) and say what evidence would confirm each. **Recommend** the next action and the metric that will show whether it worked. Say your assumptions aloud, and ask for numbers instead of guessing them.

### Misconceptions and why it matters

The common misconceptions are that a p-value of 0.03 means a 97 percent chance the effect is real, that a non-significant result proves no effect (it may only mean the sample was small), and that a significant result is automatically important (a tiny effect is significant in a huge sample). In analyst work, the same ideas decide whether a launch is called a success, so being exact about them is part of the job.

## Syntax and key functions

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

p = Fraction(5, 36)                                    # exact probability
ppv = sens * prev / (sens * prev + fpr * (1 - prev))   # Bayes' rule
p_pool = (x_a + x_b) / (n_a + n_b)
se = np.sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (1 / n_a + 1 / n_b))
z = (x_b / n_b - x_a / n_a) / se
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z)))             # two-sided
stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)                 # Welch t-test
```

| Tool | Returns or answers |
|---|---|
| Enumeration with `itertools.product` | Exact probability by counting outcomes |
| `Fraction` | Exact rational probability |
| Bayes' rule | P(condition given positive) |
| `stats.norm.cdf` | Normal cumulative probability (for z-test p-values) |
| `stats.chi2_contingency(table, correction=False)` | Chi-square test; equals the z-test for two proportions |
| `stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)` | Welch t statistic and p-value |
| Decomposition | Revenue = visitors × conversion × order value |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Counting outcomes and conditional probability

Two dice have 36 equally likely outcomes. The program lists them, counts the ones that sum to 8, and then restricts to those to find the chance that at least one die shows a six. A simulation with a seeded generator agrees with the exact answer to two decimals, the check you can propose in an interview.

```python run
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product
import numpy as np

rolls = list(product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))
sum8 = [r for r in rolls if sum(r) == 8]
print("P(sum is 8):", Fraction(len(sum8), len(rolls)))
with_six = [r for r in sum8 if 6 in r]
print("P(a six | sum is 8):", Fraction(len(with_six), len(sum8)))

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
a = rng.integers(1, 7, 200000)
b = rng.integers(1, 7, 200000)
hit = (a + b) == 8
print("simulated P(sum is 8):", round(float(hit.mean()), 2), "| exact:", round(5 / 36, 2))
print("simulated P(a six | sum is 8):", round(float(((a == 6) | (b == 6))[hit].mean()), 2))
```

```output
P(sum is 8): 5/36
P(a six | sum is 8): 2/5
simulated P(sum is 8): 0.14 | exact: 0.14
simulated P(a six | sum is 8): 0.4
```

### Example 2: Bayes' rule with a rare condition

A test has sensitivity 90 percent (it finds 90 percent of ill people) and a false positive rate of 5 percent, and 1 percent of people are ill. Of 10,000 people, 90 ill people test positive and 495 healthy people also test positive, so most positives are false. The counting and the formula give the same answer, and counting is easier to explain aloud.

```python run
sens, fpr, prev = 0.90, 0.05, 0.01
people = 10000
ill = people * prev
true_pos = ill * sens
false_pos = (people - ill) * fpr
print("true positives:", round(true_pos), "| false positives:", round(false_pos))
print("P(ill | positive) by counting:", round(true_pos / (true_pos + false_pos), 4))
print("P(ill | positive) by Bayes:", round(sens * prev / (sens * prev + fpr * (1 - prev)), 4))
```

```output
true positives: 90 | false positives: 495
P(ill | positive) by counting: 0.1538
P(ill | positive) by Bayes: 0.1538
```

### Example 3: An A/B test on conversion

The old page converted 120 of 2,400 visitors (5.0 percent) and the new page 156 of 2,400 (6.5 percent). The z-test uses the pooled rate for the standard error. A chi-square test on the 2-by-2 table without continuity correction gives the same p-value, which is a cross-check. The interval for the difference uses the unpooled standard error.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

n_a, x_a, n_b, x_b = 2400, 120, 2400, 156
p_a, p_b = x_a / n_a, x_b / n_b
pool = (x_a + x_b) / (n_a + n_b)
se = np.sqrt(pool * (1 - pool) * (1 / n_a + 1 / n_b))
z = (p_b - p_a) / se
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z)))
print("rates:", round(p_a, 4), round(p_b, 4), "| lift:", round(p_b - p_a, 4))
print("z:", round(float(z), 3), "| p-value:", round(float(p_value), 4))
chi2 = stats.chi2_contingency([[x_a, n_a - x_a], [x_b, n_b - x_b]], correction=False)
print("chi-square p-value:", round(float(chi2[1]), 4))
se_diff = np.sqrt(p_a * (1 - p_a) / n_a + p_b * (1 - p_b) / n_b)
print("95% interval for the lift:", round(float(p_b - p_a - 1.96 * se_diff), 4), "to", round(float(p_b - p_a + 1.96 * se_diff), 4))
```

```output
rates: 0.05 0.065 | lift: 0.015
z: 2.232 | p-value: 0.0256
chi-square p-value: 0.0256
95% interval for the lift: 0.0018 to 0.0282
```

### Example 4: A t-test and the multiple-testing risk

Order values for two groups come from seeded normal data. Welch's t-test compares the means, and the effect is stated in money so a reader can judge whether it matters. The last lines compute the chance of at least one false positive among 20 independent tests at the 0.05 level, and the Bonferroni threshold.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
a = rng.normal(50, 12, 300)
b = rng.normal(52, 12, 300)
t, p = stats.ttest_ind(b, a, equal_var=False)
print("mean A:", round(float(a.mean()), 2), "| mean B:", round(float(b.mean()), 2), "| difference:", round(float(b.mean() - a.mean()), 2))
print("t:", round(float(t), 3), "| p-value:", round(float(p), 4))
print("chance of at least one false positive in 20 tests:", round(1 - 0.95 ** 20, 3))
print("Bonferroni threshold for 20 tests:", 0.05 / 20)
```

```output
mean A: 48.42 | mean B: 50.37 | difference: 1.96
t: 2.163 | p-value: 0.031
chance of at least one false positive in 20 tests: 0.642
Bonferroni threshold for 20 tests: 0.0025
```

### Example 5: Decompose a revenue drop

A case question says revenue fell. Revenue is visitors × conversion rate × average order value. Substituting one factor at a time from last week's value to this week's shows how much of the change each explains. The three effects sum to the total change only in this sequential order, so say which order you used.

```python run
last = {"visitors": 50000, "conversion": 0.030, "aov": 62.0}
this = {"visitors": 49000, "conversion": 0.026, "aov": 63.0}
revenue = lambda d: d["visitors"] * d["conversion"] * d["aov"]
print("revenue last:", round(revenue(last)), "| this:", round(revenue(this)), "| change:", round(revenue(this) - revenue(last)))
step = dict(last)
effects = {}
for factor in ["visitors", "conversion", "aov"]:
    before = revenue(step)
    step[factor] = this[factor]
    effects[factor] = round(revenue(step) - before)
print("effects:", effects, "| sum:", sum(effects.values()))
print("largest driver:", min(effects, key=effects.get))
```

```output
revenue last: 93000 | this: 80262 | change: -12738
effects: {'visitors': -1860, 'conversion': -12152, 'aov': 1274} | sum: -12738
largest driver: conversion
```

## Real-world applications

- **Experiment readouts.** The z-test, interval and the check against chi-square (Example 3) are what an analyst reports to a product team deciding whether to ship a change.
- **Alert triage.** The decomposition (Example 5) is the first step when a dashboard metric drops, because it points to traffic, conversion or basket size before anyone looks at segments.
- **Screening and fraud models.** Bayes' rule (Example 2) explains why a rare event with an imperfect detector produces mostly false alarms, which drives how alerts are reviewed.
- **Interview preparation.** The routines here (count, condition, test, decompose, recommend) are the structure of strong answers.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Solve probability questions by counting equally likely outcomes, then check by simulation.
- Bayes' rule needs the false positives: with rare conditions most positives can be false.
- A p-value is the chance of data this extreme if the null were true; it is not the chance the null is true, and it says nothing about importance.
- Report the effect size and an interval, not only significance, and fix the plan before looking.
- For metric cases: define, decompose, segment, hypothesise, recommend.

### Common mistakes

- Treating independent events as dependent or the reverse. Symptom: a probability that is too large or too small. Fix: state the independence assumption and test it with a small enumeration.
- Reading p = 0.03 as a 97 percent chance the effect is real. Symptom: overconfident claims. Fix: describe it as the surprise under no effect.
- Stopping an A/B test when it first looks significant. Symptom: far more false positives than 5 percent. Fix: decide the duration and sample size in advance.
- Jumping to causes in a case question. Symptom: an answer with a story but no check of the metric. Fix: define the metric and rule out a data problem first.

### Revision notes

- Counting: favourable over total outcomes; conditional restricts the sample space.
- Bayes: true positives over all positives.
- z-test: pooled standard error; p from the normal; interval from the unpooled standard error.
- Decomposition: revenue = visitors × conversion × order value; state the order.
