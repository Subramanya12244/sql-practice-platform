## Objectives

- Explain the difference between a fact table and a dimension table, and say what question each answers.
- State the grain of a fact table in one sentence, and explain why choosing it comes before choosing any column.
- Classify measures as additive, semi-additive or non-additive, and compute a non-additive measure (an average price) correctly from additive parts.
- Run a star-join query that slices a measure by dimension attributes, and recognise a join that changes the grain and inflates a sum.
- Describe the four design steps of dimensional modelling: business process, grain, dimensions, facts.

## Understand

### Why a second kind of model

Days 142 and 143 contrasted OLTP systems with analytical ones and showed how normalisation removes redundancy so that each fact is stored once. That design suits an application that writes small, frequent changes. An analyst asks different questions: revenue by country and month, units by product category, the ten best customers of a quarter. Answering them from a normalised schema means joining many small tables every time, and the join paths are different for every analyst. **Dimensional modelling** is a way of organising a warehouse so that those questions are easy and fast: a few large tables of events surrounded by small descriptive tables, joined in one standard pattern. It is a design for reading, so it accepts some redundancy that normalisation would remove.

### Facts and dimensions

A **fact table** records events or measurements: one row each time something happens that the business wants to measure. A sale, a click, a shipment, a payment. Its columns are mostly numbers that can be summed or averaged (the **measures**) and **keys** that point to the descriptive tables. A **dimension table** holds the descriptive context of the events: who, what, where, when. A customer dimension has the name and country; a product dimension has the name and category; a date dimension has the full date, the year and the month. The rule of thumb: facts answer "how much" and "how many", dimensions answer "by what" and "for whom". In the warehouse used in this block, `fact_sales` has `quantity` and `amount_cents` and the keys `date_key`, `customer_key` and `product_key`; `dim_customer`, `dim_product` and `dim_date` describe them.

### The grain comes first

The **grain** of a fact table is the meaning of one row, stated in a sentence. For `fact_sales` the grain is **one row per order line**: one product, bought by one customer, on one date, in one quantity. Every design decision follows from the grain: which dimensions apply (an order line has a product, an order header does not), which measures make sense (the quantity of this line, the amount of this line) and how rows can be counted. Designers write the grain first, before listing any column, because most modelling errors are grain errors: mixing rows at different grains in one table (an order's shipping cost repeated on each line, then summed), or leaving the grain vague so that nobody knows whether a row can appear twice. A useful test is whether the grain is **atomic**, the lowest level the source can provide. An atomic fact table can be summarised to any higher level (by day, by month), but a table loaded at monthly grain can never be broken down by day.

### Measures: additive, semi-additive and non-additive

A **measure** is a numeric fact. How it can be aggregated depends on its kind. An **additive** measure can be summed across every dimension: `quantity` and `amount_cents` can be added across customers, products and dates, and the totals mean something. A **semi-additive** measure can be summed across some dimensions but not across time: an account balance can be added across customers on one day, but adding one customer's balances across days is meaningless (average or take the last value instead). A **non-additive** measure cannot be summed at all: a unit price, a percentage, a ratio. The standard repair is to store the additive parts and compute the ratio at query time: the average unit price is `sum(amount_cents) / sum(quantity)`, not the average of the per-row prices, because the second gives a small order the same weight as a large one. Store `amount_cents` (an integer, as on Day 101) so that sums are exact.

### Dimension attributes

Dimension columns are the things analysts **group and filter by**: country, category, month. They are usually text, and a dimension is wide and short compared with a fact table (a dimension of a few thousand rows with 20 columns, a fact table of millions of rows with 6). A good dimension contains the labels people actually use, spelled consistently, including derived ones such as `year` and `month` in `dim_date`, so that queries do not have to compute them. Each dimension row has a **surrogate key**, a meaningless integer that the warehouse assigns (`customer_key`), in addition to the **business key** that comes from the source (`customer_id`). Day 145 explains why.

### The four design steps

Kimball-style design follows four steps. First, **choose the business process**: sales, shipments, support tickets. Second, **declare the grain**. Third, **identify the dimensions** that describe each row at that grain. Fourth, **identify the facts**, the measures at that grain. If a candidate dimension does not make sense at the grain (a "supplier" for a fact whose rows do not involve one supplier), the grain or the dimension is wrong.

### Common misconception

Learners think that the star shape is only about speed. Its first benefit is **consistent meaning**: "revenue by category" is one fact table joined to one product dimension, with no ambiguity about which rows are counted. The second misconception is that joining more tables only adds columns. A join to a table with several matching rows per key multiplies the fact rows and inflates every sum; the symptom is a total that is a multiple of the true one. Always check a total before and after adding a join.

### Why it matters

The model decides which questions are easy, which are slow and which are wrong. A clear grain and additive measures prevent the most expensive errors in reporting: totals that are quietly doubled.

## Syntax and key functions

The standard shape of a star-join query: the fact table in the middle, dimensions joined by their surrogate keys, descriptive columns in `GROUP BY`, measures aggregated.

```sql-syntax
SELECT p.category, d.year, d.month,
       sum(f.amount_cents) AS amount_cents,
       sum(f.quantity)     AS units
FROM fact_sales f
JOIN dim_product p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
JOIN dim_date    d ON d.date_key    = f.date_key
GROUP BY p.category, d.year, d.month
ORDER BY p.category, d.year, d.month;
```

The ER structure of the sample warehouse:

```text
dim_date (date_key PK, full_date, year, month)
        |
        | 1:N
dim_customer (customer_key PK, customer_id, customer_name, country, ...) --1:N-- fact_sales
        (sale_id PK, date_key FK, customer_key FK, product_key FK, quantity, amount_cents)
dim_product (product_key PK, product_id, product_name, category) --1:N--/
```

| Item | What it means |
|---|---|
| Grain | One row per order line in `fact_sales`. |
| Measure | `quantity`, `amount_cents` (additive). |
| Dimension key | `date_key`, `customer_key`, `product_key` (surrogate integers). |
| Business key | `customer_id`, `product_id` from the source. |
| Average price | `sum(amount_cents) / sum(quantity)`, not `avg` of a per-row price. |
| Star join | Fact joined to dimensions on surrogate keys. |

### Example 2: Additive and non-additive measures

`amount_cents` and `quantity` are summed freely. The average unit price per month is a ratio: the correct figure is the sum of amounts over the sum of units. The last column averages the per-row prices instead and gives a different number, because each order line counts equally regardless of its quantity. Both are cast to `numeric` before rounding.

```sql run
CREATE TABLE dim_date (date_key integer PRIMARY KEY, full_date date NOT NULL, year integer NOT NULL, month integer NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE dim_customer (customer_key integer PRIMARY KEY, customer_id text NOT NULL, customer_name text NOT NULL, country text NOT NULL,
    valid_from date NOT NULL, valid_to date, is_current boolean NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE dim_product (product_key integer PRIMARY KEY, product_id text NOT NULL, product_name text NOT NULL, category text NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE fact_sales (sale_id integer PRIMARY KEY, date_key integer NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_date, customer_key integer NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_customer,
    product_key integer NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_product, quantity integer NOT NULL, amount_cents integer NOT NULL);
INSERT INTO dim_date SELECT to_char(d, 'YYYYMMDD')::int, d::date, extract(year FROM d)::int, extract(month FROM d)::int
FROM generate_series(date '2026-01-01', date '2026-02-28', interval '1 day') AS d;
INSERT INTO dim_customer SELECT i, 'C' || lpad(i::text, 3, '0'), 'Customer ' || lpad(i::text, 2, '0'),
    (ARRAY['PT', 'ES', 'FR', 'DE'])[i % 4 + 1], date '2026-01-01', NULL, true FROM generate_series(1, 12) AS i;
INSERT INTO dim_product SELECT i, 'P' || lpad(i::text, 3, '0'), 'Product ' || i,
    (ARRAY['Stationery', 'Books', 'Tools', 'Toys'])[i % 4 + 1] FROM generate_series(1, 8) AS i;
INSERT INTO fact_sales SELECT i, to_char(date '2026-01-01' + i * 3 % 59, 'YYYYMMDD')::int, i * 5 % 12 + 1, i * 3 % 8 + 1, i % 4 + 1,
    (i % 4 + 1) * (500 + (i * 3 % 8 + 1) * 250) FROM generate_series(1, 60) AS i;
SELECT d.month, sum(f.quantity) AS units, sum(f.amount_cents) AS amount_cents,
       round(sum(f.amount_cents)::numeric / sum(f.quantity), 1) AS price_correct,
       round(avg(f.amount_cents::numeric / f.quantity), 1) AS price_avg_of_rows
FROM fact_sales f JOIN dim_date d ON d.date_key = f.date_key
GROUP BY d.month ORDER BY d.month;
```

```output
month | units | amount_cents | price_correct | price_avg_of_rows
1 | 76 | 125000 | 1644.7 | 1625.0
2 | 74 | 121500 | 1641.9 | 1625.0
```

### Example 3: A star-join query

Revenue by category and month: the fact table is joined to two dimensions by surrogate keys, and the descriptive columns appear in `GROUP BY`. January and February are the only months in `dim_date`.

```sql run

    (ARRAY['PT', 'ES', 'FR', 'DE'])[i % 4 + 1], date '2026-01-01', NULL, true FROM generate_series(1, 12) AS i;
INSERT INTO dim_product SELECT i, 'P' || lpad(i::text, 3, '0'), 'Product ' || i,
    (ARRAY['Stationery', 'Books', 'Tools', 'Toys'])[i % 4 + 1] FROM generate_series(1, 8) AS i;
INSERT INTO fact_sales SELECT i, to_char(date '2026-01-01' + i * 3 % 59, 'YYYYMMDD')::int, i * 5 % 12 + 1, i * 3 % 8 + 1, i % 4 + 1,

SELECT d.year, d.month, p.category, count(*) AS lines, sum(f.amount_cents) AS amount_cents
FROM fact_sales f
JOIN dim_product p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
```

### Example 4: A join that changes the grain

A promotion table lists product 1 twice (two campaigns ran). Joining the fact table to it repeats every sale of product 1 once per promotion, so a sum over the joined rows is too large. The first column is the true total for product 1, the second the inflated one. The fix is to aggregate the promotions to the fact's grain before joining, or to leave them out of a revenue query.

```sql run
CREATE TABLE dim_date (date_key integer PRIMARY KEY, full_date date NOT NULL, year integer NOT NULL, month integer NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE dim_customer (customer_key integer PRIMARY KEY, customer_id text NOT NULL, customer_name text NOT NULL, country text NOT NULL,
    valid_from date NOT NULL, valid_to date, is_current boolean NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE dim_product (product_key integer PRIMARY KEY, product_id text NOT NULL, product_name text NOT NULL, category text NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE fact_sales (sale_id integer PRIMARY KEY, date_key integer NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_date, customer_key integer NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_customer,
    product_key integer NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_product, quantity integer NOT NULL, amount_cents integer NOT NULL);
INSERT INTO dim_date SELECT to_char(d, 'YYYYMMDD')::int, d::date, extract(year FROM d)::int, extract(month FROM d)::int
FROM generate_series(date '2026-01-01', date '2026-02-28', interval '1 day') AS d;
INSERT INTO dim_customer SELECT i, 'C' || lpad(i::text, 3, '0'), 'Customer ' || lpad(i::text, 2, '0'),
    (ARRAY['PT', 'ES', 'FR', 'DE'])[i % 4 + 1], date '2026-01-01', NULL, true FROM generate_series(1, 12) AS i;
INSERT INTO dim_product SELECT i, 'P' || lpad(i::text, 3, '0'), 'Product ' || i,
    (ARRAY['Stationery', 'Books', 'Tools', 'Toys'])[i % 4 + 1] FROM generate_series(1, 8) AS i;
INSERT INTO fact_sales SELECT i, to_char(date '2026-01-01' + i * 3 % 59, 'YYYYMMDD')::int, i * 5 % 12 + 1, i * 3 % 8 + 1, i % 4 + 1,
    (i % 4 + 1) * (500 + (i * 3 % 8 + 1) * 250) FROM generate_series(1, 60) AS i;
CREATE TABLE promo (product_key integer, promo_name text);
INSERT INTO promo VALUES (1, 'spring'), (1, 'clearance'), (2, 'spring');
SELECT (SELECT sum(amount_cents) FROM fact_sales WHERE product_key = 1) AS true_total,
       (SELECT sum(f.amount_cents) FROM fact_sales f JOIN promo r ON r.product_key = f.product_key WHERE f.product_key = 1) AS inflated_total,
       (SELECT count(*) FROM fact_sales WHERE product_key = 1) AS lines,
       (SELECT count(*) FROM fact_sales f JOIN promo r ON r.product_key = f.product_key WHERE f.product_key = 1) AS joined_rows;
```

```output
true_total | inflated_total | lines | joined_rows
5250 | 10500 | 7 | 14
```

## Real-world applications

- **Retail reporting.** Sales by store, product and day are a classic star: one fact table at order-line grain and conformed dimensions that finance, marketing and operations all use.
- **Self-service BI.** Analysts drag `category` and `month` into a tool; the star shape gives the tool one obvious join path and consistent results.
- **Auditable totals.** Stating the grain lets a reviewer check that a number is a sum of order lines and nothing else.
- **Pipeline design.** The measures chosen (cents as integers, additive parts of ratios) determine which aggregates can be precomputed later (Day 150).

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Facts record events with measures and keys; dimensions describe who, what, where, when.
- Declare the grain in one sentence before choosing columns; prefer the atomic grain.
- Additive measures sum everywhere; ratios are computed from additive parts.
- A star join uses surrogate keys and groups by dimension attributes.
- A join to a many-per-key table changes the grain and inflates sums.

### Common mistakes

- **Vague grain.** Rows at mixed levels get double counted. Write the grain sentence.
- **Averaging a ratio.** `avg(price)` weights rows equally. Use `sum(amount) / sum(quantity)`.
- **Totals multiplied by a join.** Compare the total before and after each join.
- **Putting descriptions in the fact table.** Country text repeated in 10 million rows. Keep it in the dimension.
- **Storing money as floats.** Sums drift. Use integer cents.

### Revision notes

- Four steps: process, grain, dimensions, facts.
- Additive, semi-additive (not across time), non-additive.
- Business key vs surrogate key.
- Check totals around every join.
