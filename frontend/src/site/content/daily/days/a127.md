## Objectives

- Read a relationship as a pair of columns with a cardinality (one-to-many, many-to-one, one-to-one, many-to-many) and a filter direction, and infer the cardinality from the data.
- Predict what a slicer filters under a single-direction and a both-directions relationship.
- Explain why a duplicate dimension key inflates totals and why orphan fact rows show as a blank group.
- Explain active and inactive relationships and find ambiguous paths between tables in a relationship graph.

## Understand

### What a relationship does

A relationship tells the model that a column in one table matches a column in another. In the sample model, `Customers[customer_id]` matches `Sales[customer_id]`. The relationship has two jobs. It lets the model **propagate filters**: when a report user picks `country = UK` in a slicer, the filter selects the UK rows of `Customers`, and the relationship passes it on to `Sales`, which keeps only the rows whose `customer_id` is among those customers. Every visual that sums sales then shows UK sales only, without any join written by hand. It also lets visuals combine columns from different tables, such as `Customers[country]` on an axis and the sum of `Sales[quantity]` as the value. A relationship is not a join that creates a merged table. It is a rule about how filters travel, evaluated when a visual is drawn, which is why the model stays small.

### Cardinality

**Cardinality** describes how many rows can match on each side. In a **one-to-many** relationship (written `1:*`) one side has unique values and the other repeats them: one customer, many sales. Power BI also lists it as **many-to-one** (`*:1`) when the tables are chosen in the opposite order; it is the same relationship seen from the other end. In a **one-to-one** relationship (`1:1`) both columns are unique, as with a table of extra customer attributes split in two. In a **many-to-many** relationship (`*:*`) neither column is unique. That type exists for special cases, but it is rarely the right answer for a fact and a dimension: it usually means a dimension is not truly a dimension (it has duplicate keys), and it makes totals harder to reason about. Power BI usually detects cardinality from the data when the relationship is created, and you can reproduce that detection with `is_unique` on each column. The default and safe choice in a star schema is one-to-many from each dimension to the fact table.

### Filter direction

**Cross-filter direction** says which way filters travel. The default is **single**: from the one side to the many side, from the dimension to the fact. Selecting a country filters sales; selecting a sale (in a visual) does not filter the customers table. With **both** directions, filters travel either way: filtering `Sales` to one product also filters `Customers` to the customers who bought it. That sounds useful, and sometimes is (for instance, a slicer that should list only customers with sales), but bidirectional relationships bring risks: they can create **ambiguity** when there is more than one route between two tables, they can slow down the model because filters spread across more rows, and they can produce results that surprise report authors. The standard advice is to keep relationships single-direction and to use a calculation or a different structure when a filter must go the other way.

### Data problems that relationships expose

Two problems appear in almost every real model. First, a dimension with **duplicate keys**: if `customer_id = 1` appears twice in `Customers`, the relationship can no longer be one-to-many, and in a join every order of customer 1 appears twice, inflating revenue. This is the same effect as the merge of Day 125, and the same cure: make the key unique before relating. Second, **orphan** fact rows: a sale whose `customer_id` is missing from `Customers`. The relationship cannot match it to any customer, so Power BI adds a blank member to the dimension: those sales show up under `(Blank)` in a visual by country. The total is right, but the label shows that the data has an integrity problem to fix at the source or in Power Query.

### Active and inactive relationships, and ambiguity

Between two tables, at most one relationship can be **active**; the others are **inactive** and shown as dashed lines in Model view. Suppose `Sales` has both `order_date` and `ship_date` and both relate to the `Calendar` table. Only one can filter by default (usually the order date, since that is how sales are normally reported). The inactive one is kept so that a specific calculation can switch it on for that calculation only, using the DAX function `USERELATIONSHIP`, and you will use that on a later day. This limit exists to avoid **ambiguity**: if there were two active routes from `Calendar` to `Sales`, the model could not decide which filter path to follow, and the results would depend on an arbitrary choice. The same problem arises indirectly, for example when `Calendar` relates to `Sales` and also to `Customers` (through a sign-up date) while `Customers` relates to `Sales`: then there are two paths from `Calendar` to `Sales`. Power BI refuses to activate a relationship that would create such a path. The practical test is to draw the tables as a graph and count the routes between each pair.

### Misconceptions and why it matters

A common misconception is that a relationship copies columns across, so that you can use `Customers[country]` in `Sales`. You cannot see it in the Sales table; the model only uses it when a visual combines the two tables. Another is that turning on both directions is a free fix for a slicer that does not filter another table. Most wrong totals in Power BI trace back to relationships: duplicate keys, wrong direction, orphans, or the wrong active date. The checks in this lesson are the ones to run first.

Detecting cardinality from the two key columns, as the examples do:

```python
left_unique = left[col_a].is_unique
right_unique = right[col_b].is_unique
# (True, False) -> one-to-many; (False, True) -> many-to-one; (True, True) -> one-to-one; (False, False) -> many-to-many
```

| Concept | Meaning | Check in pandas |
|---|---|---|
| One-to-many | Unique key on one side, repeated on the other | `dim[key].is_unique`, `fact[key].is_unique` is False |
| Single direction | Filters flow dimension to fact | `fact[fact[key].isin(dim_selected[key])]` |
| Both directions | Filters also flow fact to dimension | `dim[dim[key].isin(fact_selected[key])]` |
| Orphan rows | Fact keys with no dimension row | `~fact[key].isin(dim[key])` |
| Inactive relationship | Second relationship between the same tables | a second key column, such as `ship_date` |
| Ambiguity | More than one active path between two tables | count paths in the relationship graph |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: A filter flows from dimension to fact

The slicer `country = UK` selects customers; the relationship passes the selection to Sales by matching `customer_id`. The filtered rows add up to the UK revenue. A filter on `Products` works the same way through `product_id`. With single direction, the reverse does not happen: the Customers table still has all its rows even when a product is selected.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_model():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    customers = pd.DataFrame({
        "customer_id": np.arange(1, 41),
        "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
        "country": rng.choice(["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"], size=40),
        "segment": rng.choice(["Consumer", "Corporate", "Small Business"], size=40, p=[0.5, 0.3, 0.2]),
    })
    names = ["Desk", "Chair", "Bookcase", "Laptop", "Monitor", "Phone", "Notebook", "Pen Set", "Stapler", "Printer", "Lamp", "Cabinet"]
    categories = ["Furniture", "Furniture", "Furniture", "Technology", "Technology", "Technology",
                  "Office Supplies", "Office Supplies", "Office Supplies", "Technology", "Furniture", "Furniture"]
    products = pd.DataFrame({
        "product_id": np.arange(1, 13),
        "product_name": names,
        "category": categories,
        "unit_cost": rng.integers(5, 120, size=12),
    })
    sales = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1401),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, 400), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, 400),
        "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, 400),
        "quantity": rng.integers(1, 8, 400),
    })
    cost = products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]
    sales["unit_price"] = (cost[sales["product_id"]].to_numpy() * (1.3 + 0.5 * rng.random(400))).round(2)
    sales = sales.sort_values(["order_date", "order_id"]).reset_index(drop=True)
    dates = pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31", freq="D")
    calendar = pd.DataFrame({
        "date": dates,
        "year": dates.year,
        "quarter": dates.quarter,
        "month_number": dates.month,
        "month_name": dates.strftime("%B"),
    })
    return sales, customers, products, calendar


def with_ship_date():
    sales, customers, products, calendar = make_model()
    rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
    sales = sales.assign(ship_date=sales["order_date"] + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(2, 10, len(sales)), unit="D"))
    sales = sales.assign(revenue=sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"])
    return sales, customers, products, calendar


sales, customers, products, calendar = with_ship_date()
uk = customers.loc[customers["country"] == "UK", "customer_id"]
uk_sales = sales[sales["customer_id"].isin(uk)]
print("UK customers:", len(uk), "| UK sales rows:", len(uk_sales), "| UK revenue:", round(float(uk_sales["revenue"].sum()), 2))

printers = products.loc[products["product_name"] == "Printer", "product_id"]
printer_sales = sales[sales["product_id"].isin(printers)]
print("Printer sales rows:", len(printer_sales), "| revenue:", round(float(printer_sales["revenue"].sum()), 2))
print("customers table rows under single direction:", len(customers))
```

```output
UK customers: 5 | UK sales rows: 57 | UK revenue: 20141.37
Printer sales rows: 42 | revenue: 15498.47
customers table rows under single direction: 40
```

### Example 3: Cardinality from the data

The function looks at the uniqueness of each key column and names the cardinality, the check Power BI makes when it proposes a relationship. It is run on the real pairs of the model and on a dimension that has been given a duplicate key.

```python run

    sales, customers, products, calendar = make_model()
    rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
    sales = sales.assign(ship_date=sales["order_date"] + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(2, 10, len(sales)), unit="D"))

def cardinality(left, left_col, right, right_col):
    kind = {(True, False): "one-to-many", (False, True): "many-to-one", (True, True): "one-to-one", (False, False): "many-to-many"}
    return kind[(bool(left[left_col].is_unique), bool(right[right_col].is_unique))]


sales, customers, products, calendar = with_ship_date()
print("Customers -> Sales:", cardinality(customers, "customer_id", sales, "customer_id"))
print("Products -> Sales:", cardinality(products, "product_id", sales, "product_id"))
print("Calendar -> Sales:", cardinality(calendar, "date", sales, "order_date"))
print("Sales -> Customers:", cardinality(sales, "customer_id", customers, "customer_id"))
dup = pd.concat([customers, customers.iloc[[0]]], ignore_index=True)
print("duplicate key:", cardinality(dup, "customer_id", sales, "customer_id"))
```

### Example 5: Inactive relationships and ambiguous paths

`Sales` now has an extra `ship_date` column (added for this lesson). Both dates could relate to `Calendar`: the active one filters by `order_date`, and the inactive one would group the same revenue by shipping month, as the pandas calculation shows. Ship dates run past the end of the 2025 calendar, so some rows would have no calendar date, another sign of why coverage matters. The path counter then checks relationship graphs: one route between two tables is fine, and two active routes are ambiguous.

```python run
def paths(edges, start, end, seen=()):
    if start == end:
        return 1
    return sum(paths(edges, nxt, end, seen + (start,)) for a, b in edges for nxt in ((b,) if a == start else (a,) if b == start else ()) if nxt not in seen)


star = [("Calendar", "Sales"), ("Customers", "Sales"), ("Products", "Sales")]
```

## Real-world applications

- **Debugging a wrong total.** When a regional total is too high, check the dimension key for duplicates (Example 4) and the relationship cardinality (Example 3) before touching any calculation.
- **Finding data quality issues.** A `(Blank)` group in a visual (Example 4) flags orphan keys that the source team needs to fix.
- **Reporting by more than one date.** Orders by order date and by ship date (Example 5) both come from one fact table with one active and one inactive relationship to `Calendar`.
- **Reviewing a model.** A reviewer lists each relationship with its cardinality, direction and active state and checks the graph for ambiguous paths before sign-off.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- A relationship passes filters between tables; it does not copy columns.
- Dimensions to fact are one-to-many with single direction by default.
- Both directions filter the dimension from the fact, but risk ambiguity and slowness.
- Duplicate dimension keys inflate totals; orphan fact keys show as `(Blank)`.
- Only one relationship between two tables is active; the others are inactive; ambiguous paths are refused.

### Common mistakes

- Turning on both directions to make a slicer work. Symptom: slow visuals, ambiguity errors, surprising totals. Fix: keep single direction and restructure.
- Relating on a column with duplicates. Symptom: inflated revenue or a many-to-many relationship. Fix: clean the dimension to unique keys.
- Ignoring `(Blank)`. Symptom: a country called `(Blank)` with real revenue. Fix: find and fix orphan keys.
- Expecting the inactive date relationship to filter automatically. Symptom: ship-date charts that match order dates. Fix: a calculation must activate it explicitly.

### Revision notes

- Cardinality from uniqueness of both columns.
- Single direction: dimension to fact. Both: also fact to dimension.
- Duplicate key means inflated totals; orphan key means `(Blank)`.
- One active relationship per pair of tables; ambiguity means more than one active path.
