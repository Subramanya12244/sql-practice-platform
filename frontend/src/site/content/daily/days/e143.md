## Objectives

- Describe the update, insert and delete anomalies that come from storing the same fact in many rows.
- Apply first, second and third normal form by identifying functional dependencies and splitting a table.
- Read and draw an entity-relationship structure with one-to-many and many-to-many relationships and a junction table.
- Verify that a decomposition loses nothing by rebuilding the original table with joins and comparing.
- Weigh the trade-off between a normalised schema for transactions and a denormalised one for analysis.

## Understand

### The problem: one fact stored many times

Day 142 said that OLTP systems use normalised schemas. **Normalisation** is the process of organising tables so that each fact is stored exactly once. To see why that matters, look at a table that does not do it. Imagine every order line written into one wide table: `flat_sale(order_id, product, customer_name, customer_city, product_price, qty, order_date)`. Ana's city appears on every line of every order she places, and the price of a pen appears on every line that sells a pen. This duplication causes three kinds of trouble, called **anomalies**.

- **Update anomaly.** Ana moves from Berlin to Munich. You must change her city on every one of her rows. If one row is missed, the table now says Ana lives in two places, and a report grouped by city gives a wrong answer without any error message.
- **Insert anomaly.** You want to record a new product, a stapler at 5.00, before anyone has bought it. There is no order line to put it in, so you cannot store it, or you must invent a fake order.
- **Delete anomaly.** Cy's only order is cancelled and its row deleted. Cy, his city and the price of the desk that appeared only on his row disappear along with it, though they are still true.

All three come from the same cause: facts about different things (a customer, a product, an order) are mixed in one row. Normalisation separates them.

### Functional dependencies: the tool behind the normal forms

A **functional dependency** `A -> B` means that if you know the value of A, there is exactly one possible value of B. `customer_name -> customer_city` holds in a clean table: knowing the customer determines the city. `product -> product_price` holds. `order_id -> customer_name, order_date` holds, because one order belongs to one customer and has one date. But `order_id -> product` does not hold, because an order can contain many products. A **key** is a set of columns that determines every other column; in `flat_sale` the key is `(order_id, product)`, one row per product per order. You can test a dependency in data by grouping: if any value of A has more than one value of B, `A -> B` is false.

### The normal forms

A **normal form** is a rule about dependencies. Each form includes the one before.

**First normal form (1NF):** every column holds a single, atomic value, and rows are unique. A column holding `'Pen, Lamp'` or repeating columns `product1, product2, product3` break it. The fix is one row per value: put each product of an order on its own row.

**Second normal form (2NF):** the table is in 1NF and no column depends on only part of a composite key. In `flat_sale` with the key `(order_id, product)`, `product_price` depends only on `product`, and `customer_name` and `order_date` depend only on `order_id`. These are partial dependencies. The fix is to move them to tables of their own: `prod(product, product_price)` and `sale_order(order_id, customer_name, order_date)`, leaving `sale_line(order_id, product, qty)`. A table with a single-column key is automatically in 2NF if it is in 1NF.

**Third normal form (3NF):** the table is in 2NF and no non-key column depends on another non-key column (a **transitive dependency**). In `sale_order`, `customer_city` depends on `customer_name`, which depends on `order_id`. The city is really a fact about the customer. The fix: `cust(customer_name, customer_city)`, and `sale_order` keeps only the customer reference. A memorable summary: every column depends on the key, the whole key and nothing but the key.

There are stricter forms (Boyce-Codd and beyond) that matter in design theory; most operational schemas stop at 3NF.

### Entities and relationships

An **entity** is a thing the business tracks (customer, product, order) and becomes a table. A **relationship** says how entities connect. In a **one-to-many** relationship one customer has many orders, and each order has exactly one customer: the "many" side stores a **foreign key** pointing at the "one" side's primary key (`sale_order.customer_name`). In a **many-to-many** relationship an order contains many products and a product appears in many orders. A relational table cannot hold a list in a column (that would break 1NF), so a **junction table** (`sale_line`) holds one row per pair, with a composite primary key made of both foreign keys, and any attributes of the pair (the quantity).

```text
 cust 1 ----< sale_order 1 ----< sale_line >---- 1 prod
 (customer_name PK)  (order_id PK)   (order_id, product PK)   (product PK)
 "one" ----< "many": the many side holds the foreign key
```

A foreign key constraint makes the database refuse a `sale_order` whose customer does not exist (no orphans), and refuse to delete a customer who still has orders, unless you chose a cascade rule.

### Trade-offs: when to stop normalising, when to reverse it

Normalised schemas protect correctness: each fact changes in one place, constraints are simple, and writes are small and fast. The price is joins: to print an invoice you join four tables. For OLTP, where each query is short and keyed, that price is small. For OLAP (Day 142) the price is high, because analytical queries join and aggregate millions of rows, so analytical schemas deliberately **denormalise**: they copy the descriptive attributes into wide tables to avoid joins. They accept duplication because nobody edits a warehouse by hand: the pipeline rebuilds it. The rule of thumb is to normalise the system that records facts and denormalise the system that reports on them, and never to denormalise before you know which query is slow.

### Common misconception

Learners often believe that more tables always means a better design. Splitting a table that has no real dependency problem only adds joins. Another is that a surrogate key (an artificial id) is required for normal form; keys are about dependencies, not about ids (this lesson uses names as keys for readability, and real designs usually prefer ids). A third is that normalisation changes the data: a correct decomposition is **lossless**, meaning that joining the pieces gives back exactly the original rows. You can and should check this.

### Why it matters

Most of the bad numbers a data engineer meets in source systems come from a missing or violated dependency: two spellings of one customer, two prices for one product. Knowing the normal forms lets you diagnose the cause, decide whether the source or your pipeline should fix it, and design a clean landing for the data.

## Syntax and key functions

Splitting a table and enforcing the relationships:

```sql-syntax
CREATE TABLE parent (id int PRIMARY KEY, attr text NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE child  (id int PRIMARY KEY, parent_id int NOT NULL REFERENCES parent(id));
CREATE TABLE junction (a_id int REFERENCES a(id), b_id int REFERENCES b(id), PRIMARY KEY (a_id, b_id));

INSERT INTO parent SELECT DISTINCT col_a, col_b FROM flat;

-- test a dependency A -> B: any rows returned break it
SELECT a FROM flat GROUP BY a HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT b) > 1;

-- lossless check: both queries return no rows
SELECT * FROM flat EXCEPT SELECT ... FROM <joined pieces>;
```

| Item | What it does or returns |
|---|---|
| `PRIMARY KEY (a, b)` | Composite key: the pair is unique. |
| `REFERENCES t` / `FOREIGN KEY` | Rejects values that have no parent row. |
| `SELECT DISTINCT` | The unique combinations; the usual way to fill a parent table from a flat one. |
| `COUNT(DISTINCT b) ... HAVING > 1` | Finds values of A that map to more than one B, so `A -> B` fails. |
| `EXCEPT` | Rows in the first result and not in the second; two empty results prove equality. |
| `regexp_split_to_table(text, pattern)` | Turns a delimited string into one row per piece (the 1NF fix). |
| `information_schema.key_column_usage` | Lists columns that belong to keys, with their constraint names. |
| `string_agg(x, ', ' ORDER BY x)` | Joins values of a group into one text, in order. |

## Learn by example

The tables in these examples are small and use names as keys for readability. They use their own table names so they do not collide with other tables.

### Example 1: The anomalies in a flat table

Ana's later order says Munich, her earlier orders say Berlin, and the pen was sold at two prices. Nothing in the table forbids it. The two queries test the dependencies `customer_name -> customer_city` and `product -> product_price`: any row returned is a violation.

```sql run
CREATE TABLE flat_sale (order_id int, product text, customer_name text, customer_city text, product_price numeric(8,2), qty int, order_date date);
INSERT INTO flat_sale VALUES
  (1, 'Pen', 'Ana', 'Berlin', 2.00, 10, '2026-01-05'),
  (1, 'Lamp', 'Ana', 'Berlin', 40.00, 1, '2026-01-05'),
  (2, 'Desk', 'Ben', 'Paris', 150.00, 1, '2026-01-12'),
  (3, 'Pen', 'Ana', 'Munich', 2.50, 5, '2026-02-03'),
  (4, 'Desk', 'Cy', 'Berlin', 150.00, 2, '2026-02-10');

SELECT 'customer -> city' AS dependency, customer_name AS value, COUNT(DISTINCT customer_city) AS distinct_values
FROM flat_sale GROUP BY customer_name HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT customer_city) > 1
UNION ALL
SELECT 'product -> price', product, COUNT(DISTINCT product_price)
FROM flat_sale GROUP BY product HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT product_price) > 1
ORDER BY dependency, value
LIMIT 10;
```

```output
dependency | value | distinct_values
customer -> city | Ana | 2
product -> price | Pen | 2
```

The table is internally contradictory, and it is the update anomaly in action: someone changed some rows and not others. Storing each fact once would make this state impossible.

### Example 2: First normal form, one value per cell

A column `products` holds a comma-separated list, which breaks 1NF: you cannot count, join or filter the products reliably. `regexp_split_to_table` produces one row per product, and the count of products per order becomes a simple `COUNT`.

```sql run
CREATE TABLE order_list (order_id int, products text);
INSERT INTO order_list VALUES (1, 'Pen,Lamp'), (2, 'Desk'), (3, 'Pen,Pen,Desk');

SELECT order_id, regexp_split_to_table(products, ',') AS product
FROM order_list
ORDER BY order_id, product
LIMIT 10;
```

```output
order_id | product
1 | Lamp
1 | Pen
2 | Desk
3 | Desk
3 | Pen
3 | Pen
```

Order 3 lists Pen twice, a hint that the model needs a quantity column: one row per product per order with `qty`. Atomic values make that and every other query possible.

### Example 3: Testing a functional dependency in Python

The function `determines(rows, a, b)` returns True when every value of column `a` has exactly one value of `b`. It is the same GROUP BY test in Python, and it lets you list which dependencies hold in a small dataset before deciding how to split it. The data is the clean version of the flat table.

```python run
rows = [
    {"order_id": 1, "product": "Pen", "customer_name": "Ana", "customer_city": "Berlin", "product_price": 2.0, "order_date": "2026-01-05"},
    {"order_id": 1, "product": "Lamp", "customer_name": "Ana", "customer_city": "Berlin", "product_price": 40.0, "order_date": "2026-01-05"},
    {"order_id": 2, "product": "Desk", "customer_name": "Ben", "customer_city": "Paris", "product_price": 150.0, "order_date": "2026-01-12"},
    {"order_id": 3, "product": "Pen", "customer_name": "Ana", "customer_city": "Berlin", "product_price": 2.0, "order_date": "2026-02-03"},
]

def determines(rows, a, b):
    seen = {}
    for r in rows:
        key = tuple(r[c] for c in a)
        if seen.setdefault(key, r[b]) != r[b]:
            return False
    return True

for a, b in [(("customer_name",), "customer_city"), (("product",), "product_price"), (("order_id",), "customer_name"),
             (("order_id",), "product"), (("order_id",), "customer_city"), (("order_id", "product"), "order_date")]:
    print(f"{', '.join(a)} -> {b}: {determines(rows, a, b)}")
```

```output
customer_name -> customer_city: True
product -> product_price: True
order_id -> customer_name: True
order_id -> product: False
order_id -> customer_city: True
order_id, product -> order_date: True
```

`order_id -> product` is False because an order has several products. `order_id -> customer_city` is True, but only through the customer: a transitive dependency, the one 3NF removes. The data cannot prove a dependency (more rows might break it); it can only disprove one, so the business rule decides.

### Example 4: Decompose to third normal form and check that nothing was lost

The flat table is split into four tables, each filled with `SELECT DISTINCT`, with primary and foreign keys. Joining them back gives the original rows. The final query counts the rows that are in one version and not the other, in both directions: zero and zero mean the decomposition is lossless.

```sql run
CREATE TABLE flat_sale (order_id int, product text, customer_name text, customer_city text, product_price numeric(8,2), qty int, order_date date);
INSERT INTO flat_sale VALUES
  (1, 'Pen', 'Ana', 'Berlin', 2.00, 10, '2026-01-05'),
  (1, 'Lamp', 'Ana', 'Berlin', 40.00, 1, '2026-01-05'),
  (2, 'Desk', 'Ben', 'Paris', 150.00, 1, '2026-01-12'),
  (3, 'Pen', 'Ana', 'Berlin', 2.00, 5, '2026-02-03'),
  (4, 'Desk', 'Cy', 'Berlin', 150.00, 2, '2026-02-10');
CREATE TABLE cust (customer_name text PRIMARY KEY, customer_city text NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE prod (product text PRIMARY KEY, product_price numeric(8,2) NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE sale_order (order_id int PRIMARY KEY, customer_name text NOT NULL REFERENCES cust, order_date date NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE sale_line (order_id int REFERENCES sale_order, product text REFERENCES prod, qty int NOT NULL, PRIMARY KEY (order_id, product));
INSERT INTO cust SELECT DISTINCT customer_name, customer_city FROM flat_sale;
INSERT INTO prod SELECT DISTINCT product, product_price FROM flat_sale;
INSERT INTO sale_order SELECT DISTINCT order_id, customer_name, order_date FROM flat_sale;
INSERT INTO sale_line SELECT order_id, product, qty FROM flat_sale;
SELECT (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM (
          SELECT order_id, product, customer_name, customer_city, product_price, qty, order_date FROM flat_sale
          EXCEPT
          SELECT l.order_id, l.product, o.customer_name, c.customer_city, p.product_price, l.qty, o.order_date
          FROM sale_line l JOIN sale_order o ON o.order_id = l.order_id
          JOIN cust c ON c.customer_name = o.customer_name JOIN prod p ON p.product = l.product) a) AS lost_rows,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM (
          SELECT l.order_id, l.product, o.customer_name, c.customer_city, p.product_price, l.qty, o.order_date
          FROM sale_line l JOIN sale_order o ON o.order_id = l.order_id
          JOIN cust c ON c.customer_name = o.customer_name JOIN prod p ON p.product = l.product
          EXCEPT
          SELECT order_id, product, customer_name, customer_city, product_price, qty, order_date FROM flat_sale) b) AS invented_rows,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM flat_sale) AS flat_rows,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM cust) AS customers,
       (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM prod) AS products;
```

```output
lost_rows | invented_rows | flat_rows | customers | products
0 | 0 | 5 | 3 | 3
```

Five flat rows repeated three customers and three products; now each customer's city exists once. Moving Ana would be one `UPDATE` on `cust`, and the update anomaly is gone.

### Example 5: Reading the relationships from the catalogue

The schema describes itself in `information_schema`. This query lists each foreign key: the child table and column, and the parent table it references. It is how you document or audit a database you did not design, and it is the textual form of the ER diagram.

```sql run
CREATE TABLE flat_sale (order_id int, product text, customer_name text, customer_city text, product_price numeric(8,2), qty int, order_date date);
INSERT INTO flat_sale VALUES
  (1, 'Pen', 'Ana', 'Berlin', 2.00, 10, '2026-01-05'),
  (1, 'Lamp', 'Ana', 'Berlin', 40.00, 1, '2026-01-05'),
  (2, 'Desk', 'Ben', 'Paris', 150.00, 1, '2026-01-12'),
  (3, 'Pen', 'Ana', 'Berlin', 2.00, 5, '2026-02-03'),
  (4, 'Desk', 'Cy', 'Berlin', 150.00, 2, '2026-02-10');
CREATE TABLE cust (customer_name text PRIMARY KEY, customer_city text NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE prod (product text PRIMARY KEY, product_price numeric(8,2) NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE sale_order (order_id int PRIMARY KEY, customer_name text NOT NULL REFERENCES cust, order_date date NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE sale_line (order_id int REFERENCES sale_order, product text REFERENCES prod, qty int NOT NULL, PRIMARY KEY (order_id, product));
INSERT INTO cust SELECT DISTINCT customer_name, customer_city FROM flat_sale;
INSERT INTO prod SELECT DISTINCT product, product_price FROM flat_sale;
INSERT INTO sale_order SELECT DISTINCT order_id, customer_name, order_date FROM flat_sale;
INSERT INTO sale_line SELECT order_id, product, qty FROM flat_sale;
SELECT kcu.table_name AS child_table, kcu.column_name AS child_column, ccu.table_name AS parent_table
FROM information_schema.table_constraints tc
JOIN information_schema.key_column_usage kcu ON kcu.constraint_name = tc.constraint_name AND kcu.table_schema = tc.table_schema
JOIN information_schema.constraint_column_usage ccu ON ccu.constraint_name = tc.constraint_name AND ccu.table_schema = tc.table_schema
WHERE tc.constraint_type = 'FOREIGN KEY' AND kcu.table_name IN ('sale_order', 'sale_line')
ORDER BY child_table, child_column
LIMIT 10;
```

```output
child_table | child_column | parent_table
sale_line | order_id | sale_order
sale_line | product | prod
sale_order | customer_name | cust
```

`sale_line` has two foreign keys and a composite primary key: the signature of a junction table that implements a many-to-many relationship between orders and products.

### Example 6: A many-to-many relationship and a read model

Students and courses are many-to-many, so an `enrolment` junction table holds the pairs. The first query reads through the junction table. The second builds the denormalised, read-friendly shape that a report or warehouse would use: one row per student with the list of courses, produced from the normalised tables rather than stored by hand.

```sql run
CREATE TABLE student (student_id int PRIMARY KEY, student_name text NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE course (course_id int PRIMARY KEY, title text NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE enrolment (student_id int REFERENCES student, course_id int REFERENCES course, PRIMARY KEY (student_id, course_id));
INSERT INTO student VALUES (1, 'Ana'), (2, 'Ben'), (3, 'Cy');
INSERT INTO course VALUES (10, 'SQL'), (11, 'Python'), (12, 'Statistics');
INSERT INTO enrolment VALUES (1, 10), (1, 11), (2, 10), (3, 10), (3, 11), (3, 12);

SELECT s.student_name, COUNT(*) AS courses, string_agg(c.title, ', ' ORDER BY c.title) AS titles
FROM student s
JOIN enrolment e ON e.student_id = s.student_id
JOIN course c ON c.course_id = e.course_id
GROUP BY s.student_name
ORDER BY s.student_name
LIMIT 10;
```

```output
student_name | courses | titles
Ana | 2 | Python, SQL
Ben | 1 | SQL
Cy | 3 | Python, SQL, Statistics
```

The titles column is a list in one cell, acceptable here because it is a computed result and not stored data. Storing it would break 1NF and create the update anomaly again.

## Real-world applications

- **Diagnosing source data.** When two customers appear with different cities, a dependency check names the broken rule (`customer -> city`) and tells you whether to fix the source or add a rule to the pipeline.
- **Designing a landing area.** Raw feeds often arrive flat, like `flat_sale`. A cleaning step decomposes them into entities with keys, and lossless checks (Day 136's comparison) prove that no row was lost.
- **Documenting an unfamiliar database.** Reading primary and foreign keys from `information_schema` gives the entity-relationship picture without a diagram.
- **Preparing a warehouse model.** The normalised tables are the source; analysts need a wide, joined shape, which is built with the joins of Example 6 and is the topic of the next lessons.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Normalisation stores each fact once, removing update, insert and delete anomalies.
- Functional dependency `A -> B`: knowing A fixes B. Test it by grouping; data can disprove it but not prove it.
- 1NF atomic values; 2NF no partial dependency on a composite key; 3NF no transitive dependency. "The key, the whole key, nothing but the key."
- One-to-many uses a foreign key on the many side; many-to-many needs a junction table.
- A good decomposition is lossless. Normalise for OLTP, denormalise deliberately for reporting.

### Common mistakes

- **Storing lists in a cell.** Counts and joins break. Use one row per value and a junction table.
- **Repeating descriptive attributes on every row.** One edit then changes some rows and not others. Move them to their own table.
- **Splitting without keys and constraints.** Orphan rows appear. Add primary keys and foreign keys.
- **Assuming a dependency from sample data.** Three rows can look functional by chance. Confirm with the business rule.
- **Denormalising before measuring.** You pay duplication and gain nothing. Normalise first, denormalise for a known slow query.

### Revision notes

- Anomalies: update, insert, delete.
- Forms: 1NF atomic; 2NF whole key; 3NF nothing but the key.
- Relationship patterns: foreign key on the "many" side; junction table for many-to-many.
- Checks: `GROUP BY a HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT b) > 1`; `EXCEPT` both ways for lossless.
