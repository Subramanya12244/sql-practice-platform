## Understand

### The question marketing analytics answers

A company spends money on advertising, email, discounts and partnerships. Marketing analytics asks whether the spending is worth it and where the next unit of money should go. Two different questions hide in this: **attribution** asks which of the touches a customer experienced deserves credit for a sale, and **incrementality** asks whether the sale would have happened anyway. Attribution is an accounting rule. Incrementality is a causal question. A report can be perfect on the first and wrong on the second.

### Attribution basics

A customer's **journey** is the ordered list of marketing touches before a purchase, for example: search advert, then email, then direct visit. An **attribution rule** splits the credit for the sale among those touches:

- **First-touch** gives all credit to the first channel. It favours channels that create awareness.
- **Last-touch** gives all credit to the last channel before purchase. It favours channels that close, such as brand search or a retargeting advert, and is the default in many tools because it is easy to measure.
- **Linear** divides the credit equally among all touches. It is fairer to the middle of the journey but gives credit to touches that may have had no effect.

None of these is the truth. They are conventions, each with a bias, and the same sales produce different channel rankings under each. A good report shows two or three rules side by side: if a channel is strong under all of them, the conclusion is robust; if it appears strong only under one, the rule is doing the work. Attribution also depends on what can be observed. Touches that are not tracked (a billboard, a conversation) get no credit and the tracked channels get too much.

### Return measures

- **ROAS** (return on advertising spend) is `revenue / spend`. A ROAS of 4 means 4 of revenue for each 1 spent. It ignores costs of goods.
- **ROI** (return on investment) is `(gross profit - spend) / spend`, where gross profit is revenue times the gross margin (gross profit is revenue minus the cost of the goods sold). An ROI of 0.5 means that each unit spent returned its cost plus 50 percent. A channel with a high ROAS and a thin margin can have a negative ROI, so ROAS should not be used alone to decide where to put money.
- **CAC** (customer acquisition cost) is `spend / new customers acquired`. Compare it with the value a customer brings (Day 145): if a customer yields 90 of gross profit over their life and CAC is 120, the channel loses money.

These measures use a revenue figure that comes from an attribution rule, so they inherit its bias.

### Incrementality and uplift

Many customers who see an advert or receive a discount email would have bought anyway. A report that credits the campaign with all of their purchases overstates its effect. **Incrementality** measures the extra sales caused by the campaign. The standard method is an experiment: randomly assign customers to a **treatment** group that gets the campaign and a **control** (holdout) group that does not, then compare outcome rates. **Uplift** is `conversion rate of treatment - conversion rate of control`, and **incremental conversions** are `uplift * number treated`. Random assignment is what makes the comparison fair: both groups have the same mix of eager and reluctant buyers on average, so the difference can be attributed to the campaign. Comparing customers who chose to click with those who did not is not an experiment, because the clickers were different people before the campaign.

### Uncertainty

A difference between two rates measured on samples has noise. For a rate `p` on `n` customers, the standard error is `sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)`. For the difference of two independent rates the standard error is `sqrt(se_t^2 + se_c^2)`, and an approximate 95 percent interval is the difference plus or minus 1.96 standard errors (this approximation is reasonable when each group has at least a few hundred customers and the rates are not extremely close to 0 or 1). If the interval includes zero, the data does not rule out that the campaign had no effect. A wide interval is also useful information: it tells you the test was too small to answer the question, and the honest conclusion is "not enough evidence", which differs from "no effect".

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Budgets move on these numbers. The misconception is that a channel with the highest attributed ROAS is the best place for more money. Last-touch ROAS of retargeting is often very high because it targets people who were already about to buy, so the incremental return can be a small fraction of the attributed one. The reverse also happens: upper-funnel channels look weak under last-touch yet raise later conversions. The way to settle it is a holdout test, not a better attribution formula.

## Syntax and key functions

```python
roas = revenue / spend
roi = (revenue * margin - spend) / spend
cac = spend / new_customers

uplift = rate_t - rate_c
se = (rate_t * (1 - rate_t) / n_t + rate_c * (1 - rate_c) / n_c) ** 0.5
low, high = uplift - 1.96 * se, uplift + 1.96 * se
```

| Function or formula | Returns |
|---|---|
| `revenue / spend` | ROAS. |
| `(revenue * margin - spend) / spend` | ROI on gross profit. |
| `spend / new_customers` | CAC. |
| `df.groupby("treated")["bought"].mean()` | Conversion rate per group. |
| `rate_t - rate_c` | Uplift in conversion rate. |
| `1.96 * se` | Half-width of an approximate 95% interval. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Three attribution rules

Six journeys are listed with the revenue of the resulting order. Each rule splits the revenue between the channels touched. The totals under every rule equal the total revenue, which is the check.

```python run
from collections import defaultdict

journeys = [
    (["search", "email", "direct"], 90.0),
    (["social", "search"], 60.0),
    (["email"], 40.0),
    (["social", "email", "search", "direct"], 120.0),
    (["search"], 50.0),
    (["social", "direct"], 80.0),
]

def credit(rule):
    out = defaultdict(float)
    for touches, revenue in journeys:
        if rule == "first":
            out[touches[0]] += revenue
        elif rule == "last":
            out[touches[-1]] += revenue
        else:
            for t in touches:
                out[t] += revenue / len(touches)
    return out

channels = ["direct", "email", "search", "social"]
print("channel   first    last  linear")
for c in channels:
    print(f"{c:8}{credit('first')[c]:7.1f}{credit('last')[c]:8.1f}{credit('linear')[c]:8.1f}")
total = sum(r for _, r in journeys)
print("total", total, [round(sum(credit(r).values()), 1) for r in ("first", "last", "linear")])
```

```output
channel   first    last  linear
direct      0.0   290.0   100.0
email      40.0    40.0   100.0
search    140.0   110.0   140.0
social    260.0     0.0   100.0
total 440.0 [440.0, 440.0, 440.0]
```

Social is the entry point for three of the six journeys and gets large credit under first-touch, while direct gets credit under last-touch. The ranking of channels changes with the rule, which is the point of showing more than one.

### Example 2: ROAS, ROI and CAC by channel

Spend, revenue, gross margin and new customers per channel are in a table. ROAS ignores margin, ROI uses each channel's gross margin. The two rankings are different.

```python run
import pandas as pd

d = pd.DataFrame({
    "channel": ["search", "social", "email", "display"],
    "spend": [10000.0, 8000.0, 1000.0, 6000.0],
    "revenue": [32000.0, 14000.0, 6000.0, 9000.0],
    "margin": [0.40, 0.50, 0.15, 0.40],
    "new_customers": [200, 100, 25, 40],
}).set_index("channel")
d["roas"] = d["revenue"] / d["spend"]
d["roi"] = (d["revenue"] * d["margin"] - d["spend"]) / d["spend"]
d["break_even_roas"] = 1 / d["margin"]
d["cac"] = d["spend"] / d["new_customers"]
print(d[["roas", "break_even_roas", "roi", "cac"]].round(2).to_string())
print("rank by roas:", list(d["roas"].sort_values(ascending=False).index))
print("rank by roi: ", list(d["roi"].sort_values(ascending=False).index))
```

```output
         roas  break_even_roas   roi    cac
channel                                    
search   3.20             2.50  0.28   50.0
social   1.75             2.00 -0.12   80.0
email    6.00             6.67 -0.10   40.0
display  1.50             2.50 -0.40  150.0
rank by roas: ['email', 'search', 'social', 'display']
rank by roi:  ['search', 'email', 'social', 'display']
```

Email has by far the best ROAS, but its margin is only 15 percent, so its break-even ROAS is 6.7 and it loses a little money, while search wins on ROI. A channel needs a ROAS above `1 / margin` to break even. Social and display lose money on gross profit.

### Example 3: Uplift from a holdout test

Twenty thousand customers are randomly split; half receive the campaign. The conversion rate per group, the uplift, and a 95 percent interval from the normal approximation are computed. A seed makes the result stable.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 20000
g = pd.DataFrame({"treated": rng.random(n) < 0.5})
p = np.where(g["treated"], 0.050, 0.040)
g["bought"] = rng.random(n) < p
g["amount"] = np.where(g["bought"], rng.normal(60, 15, n).round(2), 0.0)
t = g[g["treated"]]
c = g[~g["treated"]]
pt, pc = float(t["bought"].mean()), float(c["bought"].mean())
nt, nc = len(t), len(c)
uplift = pt - pc
se = (pt * (1 - pt) / nt + pc * (1 - pc) / nc) ** 0.5
print("treated:", nt, "rate:", round(pt * 100, 2), "% | control:", nc, "rate:", round(pc * 100, 2), "%")
print("uplift (points):", round(uplift * 100, 2))
print("95% interval (points):", round((uplift - 1.96 * se) * 100, 2), "to", round((uplift + 1.96 * se) * 100, 2))
```

```output
treated: 9916 rate: 4.88 % | control: 10084 rate: 4.15 %
uplift (points): 0.74
95% interval (points): 0.16 to 1.31
```

The interval is wide relative to the estimate, because conversion rates of a few percent on groups of ten thousand are measured with a noticeable error. The data suggests a positive uplift, and the lower end of the interval shows how small the effect could be.

### Example 4: Attributed against incremental return

The campaign cost 3,000. If all purchases by treated customers are credited to the campaign, the return looks large. Only the extra purchases beyond the control rate are incremental. Margin is again 40 percent.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 20000
g = pd.DataFrame({"treated": rng.random(n) < 0.5})
p = np.where(g["treated"], 0.050, 0.040)
g["bought"] = rng.random(n) < p
g["amount"] = np.where(g["bought"], rng.normal(60, 15, n).round(2), 0.0)
t = g[g["treated"]]
c = g[~g["treated"]]
cost, margin = 3000.0, 0.40
attributed_rev = float(t["amount"].sum())
aov = float(t.loc[t["bought"], "amount"].mean())
extra_orders = (float(t["bought"].mean()) - float(c["bought"].mean())) * len(t)
incremental_rev = extra_orders * aov
print("attributed revenue:", round(attributed_rev), "| ROAS:", round(attributed_rev / cost, 2))
print("incremental orders:", round(extra_orders), "| incremental revenue:", round(incremental_rev))
print("incremental ROAS:", round(incremental_rev / cost, 2), "| incremental ROI on margin:", round((incremental_rev * margin - cost) / cost, 2))
```

```output
attributed revenue: 28897 | ROAS: 9.63
incremental orders: 73 | incremental revenue: 4356
incremental ROAS: 1.45 | incremental ROI on margin: -0.42
```

The attributed ROAS is many times larger than the incremental one, because most buyers in the treatment group would have bought without the campaign. The incremental ROI is the figure that answers whether the campaign was worth its cost, and here it is negative: the campaign cost more than the margin on its extra orders.

## Real-world applications

- **Budget allocation**: the ROI ranking from Example 2 shows where to move money, and a holdout test decides whether the move is justified when channels overlap.
- **Promotion design**: before sending a discount to everyone, a holdout group measures how many of the extra sales are incremental and whether the discount cost exceeds the margin gained.
- **Reporting to executives**: showing first-touch, last-touch and linear credit together prevents one rule from deciding the story.
- **Test planning**: the interval width in Example 3 shows how large a test must be to detect a small uplift, so teams do not run tests that cannot answer the question.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Attribution rules (first, last, linear) are conventions with different biases; show more than one.
- ROAS ignores margin; ROI uses gross profit; CAC compares with customer value.
- Incrementality needs a random holdout; uplift is the difference of conversion rates.
- Report uncertainty: an interval that includes zero is "not enough evidence", not "no effect".
- Attributed return can be far above incremental return.

### Common mistakes

- Ranking channels by ROAS alone and funding a channel that loses money after margin. Compute ROI too.
- Crediting a campaign with every sale to people who saw it. Compare with a control group.
- Comparing people who clicked with people who did not and calling it uplift. The groups differ before the campaign; use random assignment.
- Reporting an uplift without an interval or the group sizes. The reader cannot judge whether it is noise.
- Treating last-touch credit as the cause of a sale. It records the final touch, not the effect.

### Revision notes

- `ROAS = revenue / spend`; `ROI = (revenue x margin - spend) / spend`; `CAC = spend / new customers`.
- Break-even ROAS = 1 / margin.
- `uplift = rate_t - rate_c`; `se = sqrt(se_t^2 + se_c^2)`; interval = uplift plus or minus 1.96 se.
- Incremental orders = uplift x treated customers.
