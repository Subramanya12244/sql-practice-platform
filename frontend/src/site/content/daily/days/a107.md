## Objectives

- Classify each column as numeric (continuous or discrete), categorical, binary, date or identifier, and choose the right summary for each kind.
- Summarise a categorical variable with counts and shares, and a numeric variable with centre, spread, shape and missing values.
- Build histograms as bin counts with `np.histogram`, and explain how the number of bins changes the story.
- Read a boxplot as numbers: the five-number summary, the interquartile range and the whisker limits.
- Combine the findings into one summary table for a dataset and state what each variable looks like.

## Understand

### One variable at a time

**Univariate analysis** examines a single variable on its own: what values it takes, how often, where its centre is, how spread out it is and what shape it has. "Uni" means one. It is the second step of an exploration, after the profiling of Day 106, and it comes before comparing variables (Day 108). You cannot interpret a relationship between two columns until you know what each column looks like alone. A correlation between `order_total` and `quantity` means something different if one of them has a long tail of huge values.

### Choosing the right summary by variable type

The kind of column decides which summary makes sense. Averaging an order id is meaningless, and counting distinct values of a price is rarely useful.

- **Numeric continuous** columns can take almost any value in a range: `order_total`, `unit_price`, `shipping_days` if measured exactly. Use centre (mean, median), spread (standard deviation, IQR), range and shape.
- **Numeric discrete** columns take a few whole values: `quantity`, `rating`. Counting how often each value occurs is often clearer than a mean.
- **Categorical** columns hold labels: `channel`, `device`, `category`. Use counts and shares, ordered from most to least common.
- **Binary** columns have two values, such as `returned`. The share of `True` is the key number, and its mean is exactly that share.
- **Date** columns are summarised by earliest, latest and counts per period.
- **Identifier** columns such as `order_id` have a unique value per row. Check that they are unique; do not average them.

### Centre, spread and shape

From Days 86 and 87 you know the pieces. The **mean** is the arithmetic average and is pulled by extreme values. The **median** is the middle value and resists them. When the mean sits well above the median, the distribution has a long tail to the right, called **right skew** (positive skew). Order values, incomes and waiting times are usually right-skewed because there is a floor at zero but no ceiling. The **standard deviation** measures typical distance from the mean, and the **interquartile range (IQR)**, the 75th minus the 25th percentile, measures the spread of the middle half of the data and is not affected by extremes. **Skewness** is a single number for asymmetry; positive means a right tail.

A good habit is to compare mean and median every time. If they are close, the distribution is roughly symmetric and the mean is a fair description. If they differ a lot, report the median as the typical value and mention the tail.

### Histograms

A **histogram** shows the shape of a numeric variable. The number line is cut into equal-width intervals called **bins**, and each bin's bar height is the count of values that fall inside it. A bar is not a category: the x-axis is a continuous scale and the bars touch. Read a histogram for its peaks (are there one or two?), its symmetry, the length of its tails and any gaps.

The bin width is a choice, and it changes the picture. Too few bins hide structure; too many bins make the picture ragged with noise. Try two or three widths before concluding anything. In this lesson you build a histogram as the numbers behind it, with `np.histogram`, and print them as rows of text bars. Drawing the same thing as a graphic is the subject of Day 115, and the numbers are the same either way.

### Boxplots as numbers

A **boxplot** draws five numbers: the minimum and maximum of the typical range (the whiskers), the first quartile Q1, the median and the third quartile Q3. The box spans Q1 to Q3, so its length is the IQR. The conventional whisker rule extends each whisker to the furthest data point within 1.5 x IQR of the box, so the limits are Q1 - 1.5 x IQR and Q3 + 1.5 x IQR, and points beyond them are drawn individually as dots. You met that rule on Day 86. A boxplot is compact, so it is excellent for comparing many groups (Day 108), but it hides multiple peaks; a histogram shows them. Use both.

### Transformations: a first look

When a variable has a long right tail, taking the logarithm (`np.log1p` for values that may be zero) compresses large values and often produces a more symmetric shape. It is a way of viewing the data, not a correction of it: always report the original values in the final summary.

### Missing values and the denominator

Every univariate summary must state its denominator. A mean rating of 3.4 is computed only over rated orders. If a quarter of the ratings are missing, say so beside the number. `value_counts(dropna=False)` and `count()` against `len()` make the base visible.

### Common misconception

"The mean is the typical value." For skewed variables it is not: a few very large orders can raise the mean above what most customers spend. Another misconception is that a histogram with more bins is more accurate. More bins show more detail but also more noise; no setting is correct for every dataset.

### Why it matters

A univariate pass finds the facts that every later number depends on: that most orders are small, that discounts are mostly zero, that ratings pile up at the high end. It also reveals problems that a profile missed, such as a second peak caused by two currencies mixed in one column.

## Syntax and key functions

Summaries:

```python
s.describe()
s.value_counts(normalize=True)
s.quantile([0.25, 0.5, 0.75])
s.skew()
```

Histogram counts:

```python
counts, edges = np.histogram(s.dropna(), bins=edges_or_number)
```

`counts[i]` is how many values fall in the interval from `edges[i]` up to `edges[i + 1]` (the last interval also includes its upper edge).

Boxplot limits:

```python
q1, q3 = s.quantile([0.25, 0.75])
iqr = q3 - q1
low, high = q1 - 1.5 * iqr, q3 + 1.5 * iqr
```

| Function | Returns |
|---|---|
| `s.describe()` | Count, mean, std, min, quartiles and max. |
| `s.value_counts(normalize=True)` | Share of each distinct value, most common first. |
| `s.quantile(q)` | Value at fraction q of the sorted data. |
| `s.skew()` | Skewness; positive for a right tail. |
| `np.histogram(x, bins=...)` | Tuple of bin counts and bin edges. |
| `np.log1p(x)` | log(1 + x), used to compress long right tails. |
| `s.mean()`, `s.median()` | Centre; compare the two. |
| `s.mean()` on a boolean column | Share of `True` values. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Classify the columns

Before summarising anything, decide each column's kind. This helper uses simple rules: unique per row is an identifier, two values is binary, dates are dates, text is categorical, few whole numbers are discrete and the rest are continuous. The rules are a starting point, and you should check each result: here `customer_id` is a code with repeated values, so treat it as an identifier, and `shipping_days` holds whole days, so it behaves like a discrete number.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

pd.set_option("display.width", 200)
pd.set_option("display.max_columns", 20)


def make_orders(messy=False):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 600
    category = rng.choice(["home", "beauty", "sports", "electronics", "toys"], size=n, p=[0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10])
    base = pd.Series({"home": 40, "beauty": 25, "sports": 55, "electronics": 180, "toys": 20})
    discount = rng.choice([0, 5, 10, 20], size=n, p=[0.6, 0.15, 0.15, 0.1])
    ship = np.clip(rng.poisson(3.5, n) + 1, 1, 12).astype(float)
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1001 + n),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 180, n), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 260, n),
        "channel": rng.choice(["organic", "paid", "email", "referral"], size=n, p=[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]),
        "device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop", "tablet"], size=n, p=[0.55, 0.35, 0.10]),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": np.clip(rng.poisson(0.8 + discount / 20, n) + 1, 1, 6),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * rng.lognormal(0, 0.25, n)).round(2),
        "discount_pct": discount,
        "shipping_days": ship,
    })
    df.loc[rng.choice(n, 4, replace=False), "unit_price"] *= 8
    df["order_total"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"] * (1 - df["discount_pct"] / 100)).round(2)
    df["rating"] = np.clip(np.round(4.3 - 0.2 * ship + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)), 1, 5)
    df["returned"] = rng.random(n) < 0.06 + 0.06 * (category == "electronics") + 0.03 * (ship > 6)
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.05, "shipping_days"] = np.nan
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.25, "rating"] = np.nan
    if messy:
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 25, replace=False), "channel"] = "Paid "
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 3, replace=False), "quantity"] = -1
        df = pd.concat([df, df.sample(6, random_state=1)], ignore_index=True)
    return df
orders = make_orders()

def kind(s):
    if s.nunique() == len(s):
        return "identifier"
    if s.nunique() == 2:
        return "binary"
    if pd.api.types.is_datetime64_any_dtype(s):
        return "date"
    if s.dtype == object:
        return "categorical"
    if s.nunique() <= 10:
        return "numeric discrete"
    return "numeric continuous"

print(pd.Series({c: kind(orders[c]) for c in orders.columns}).to_string())
```

```output
order_id                 identifier
order_date                     date
customer_id      numeric continuous
channel                 categorical
device                  categorical
category                categorical
quantity           numeric discrete
unit_price       numeric continuous
discount_pct       numeric discrete
shipping_days    numeric continuous
order_total      numeric continuous
rating             numeric discrete
returned                     binary
```

### Example 5: A boxplot as numbers, and a log view

The five numbers and the whisker limits come straight from quantiles. Orders above the upper limit are the points a boxplot would draw as dots. Taking `np.log1p` compresses the tail: skewness falls from above 3 to about 0.5 and far fewer values lie beyond the whiskers. This is only a different view of the same orders.

### Example 6: One summary table for the dataset

The last example puts the numeric variables into a single table with the denominator, centre, spread and shape, and adds the share of missing values. This is the kind of table that goes into an EDA report: each row can be read in one line, and the mean-versus-median gap flags the skewed variables.

## Real-world applications

- **Pricing and promotions.** The shape of `unit_price` and `order_total` (Examples 3 to 5) tells a pricing analyst whether a typical order is small or whether a few large orders drive revenue, which changes which discount rules are worth testing.
- **Survey and rating analysis.** Rating distributions, as in Example 3, are usually piled at the high end; reporting the share of each rating is more honest than a single average, and the count of rated orders gives the denominator.
- **Operations and service levels.** Shipping time is summarised with the median and a high percentile rather than the mean, because late deliveries create a right tail.
- **Reporting.** A table like Example 6 sits at the top of an EDA report, so that readers see centre, spread, shape and missing values for each variable before any conclusion.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Choose the summary by variable type: counts and shares for categories, centre, spread and shape for numbers, share of `True` for binary columns.
- Always compare mean and median; a mean above the median means a right tail.
- A histogram is bin counts: the bin width is a choice, so try more than one.
- A boxplot is Q1, median, Q3 and whiskers at 1.5 x IQR beyond the box; points beyond the whiskers are shown individually.
- State the denominator for every summary, and report original values even after viewing the data on a log scale.

### Common mistakes

- Averaging a skewed variable and calling it typical. The mean is above the median and describes few real orders. Fix: report the median and the spread.
- Treating a histogram like a bar chart of categories and reading bar gaps or order as meaningful. Fix: remember the x-axis is a number line.
- Judging the shape from a single bin width. A coarse width hides detail and a fine width shows noise. Fix: try two or three widths.
- Computing a mean rating without noting that a quarter of orders are unrated. Fix: print `count()` beside the mean.
- Averaging an identifier or a code stored as a number, such as `customer_id`. The result is meaningless. Fix: classify columns first.
- Using `value_counts()` on a continuous column, which produces hundreds of rows of ones. Fix: use `describe` or bins.

### Revision notes

- Types: continuous, discrete, categorical, binary, date, identifier.
- `describe()`, `skew()`, mean versus median; `value_counts(normalize=True)`.
- `np.histogram(x, bins=edges)` gives `counts, edges`.
- Whiskers: Q1 - 1.5 x IQR and Q3 + 1.5 x IQR.
- `np.log1p` compresses a right tail; the denominator is `count()`.
