## Understand

### Looking at two variables together

Until now the statistics in this course described one variable at a time: its centre (mean, median), its spread (variance, standard deviation) and its distribution. Analysts nearly always ask a second kind of question: do two variables move together? Do orders with higher discounts have larger quantities? Do days with more visits have more sales? Each row of the data is a pair of values `(x, y)`, and the question is whether the pairs follow a pattern.

### Covariance

**Covariance** measures how two variables vary together. For each row, take how far `x` is above or below its mean and how far `y` is above or below its mean, and multiply the two distances. When both are above their means, or both below, the product is positive. When one is above and the other below, the product is negative. The **sample covariance** adds up the products and divides by `n - 1`, the same divisor as the sample variance:

`cov(x, y) = sum((x - mean_x) * (y - mean_y)) / (n - 1)`

A positive covariance means that high values of `x` tend to go with high values of `y`, a negative one means the opposite, and a value near zero means no consistent linear pattern. The covariance of a variable with itself is its variance. The weakness of covariance is its **units**: it is measured in the units of `x` times the units of `y`, so its size changes if you change units. Measure a length in centimetres instead of metres and the covariance becomes 100 times larger although the relationship has not changed. A covariance of 4.3 therefore cannot be called strong or weak by itself.

### The Pearson correlation coefficient

The **Pearson correlation coefficient**, written `r`, removes the units by dividing the covariance by the product of the two standard deviations:

`r = cov(x, y) / (std_x * std_y)`

The result always lies between -1 and +1. A value of +1 means that all points lie exactly on an upward sloping straight line, -1 that they lie on a downward sloping line, and 0 that there is no *linear* relationship. Values around 0.1 to 0.3 are usually called weak, around 0.5 moderate and above 0.7 strong, though what counts as strong depends on the field. Because the units cancel, `r` does not change when you rescale or shift either variable: centimetres and metres give the same `r`. Swapping `x` and `y` also gives the same value, so correlation has no direction.

Squaring gives `r²`, the share of the variation in one variable that a straight line of the other accounts for. An `r` of 0.5 therefore accounts for a quarter of the variation, not half.

### What Pearson does not see

`r` measures only straight-line association, and it has two well-known blind spots. First, it can be near zero for a strong **curved** relationship: if `y = x²` for `x` running from -5 to 5, `y` is completely determined by `x` yet `r` is exactly 0, because the left half of the curve cancels the right half. Second, it is sensitive to **outliers**. One extreme point can create a correlation that is not in the rest of the data, or destroy one that is. Always look at a scatter plot before trusting a coefficient.

### Spearman correlation

**Spearman's rank correlation** replaces each value by its rank (1 for the smallest, 2 for the next, and so on, with tied values sharing the average rank) and then calculates the Pearson correlation of the ranks. It measures whether the relationship is **monotonic**, meaning that `y` keeps rising (or keeps falling) as `x` rises, even if it is not a straight line. For `y = exp(x)`, which is curved but always rising, Spearman is exactly 1 while Pearson is lower. Because ranks ignore how big the gaps are, Spearman is far less affected by a single outlier. Use Pearson for roughly linear relationships in data without extreme points, and Spearman for ranked data, curved but steadily rising or falling relationships, or data with outliers. Reporting both is a good habit.

### Reading a scatter plot

A **scatter plot** draws one point per row, with `x` across and `y` up. Describe it in four words. **Direction**: do the points rise (positive) or fall (negative) from left to right? **Form**: do they follow a line, a curve, or separate clusters? **Strength**: how tightly do they hug that pattern? **Outliers**: are there points far from the rest? A cloud with no visible pattern means a correlation near 0. Plots cannot be drawn in the browser exercises here, so the examples describe the picture in numbers, but you should draw one in a real analysis.

### Correlation is not causation

Two variables can be strongly correlated for reasons other than one causing the other. There are three common explanations beyond causation. **Reverse causation**: you assume `x` causes `y` when `y` causes `x`. **Coincidence**: with many variables and few rows, some pairs will correlate by chance. **Confounding**: a third variable drives both. Summer temperature raises ice cream sales and also raises the number of people swimming, and so drownings; ice cream sales and drownings correlate even though neither causes the other. To investigate, you can compare rows with similar values of the suspected confounder, or remove the part of each variable explained by it. A designed experiment, is the strongest way to establish causation. A correlation is evidence of a pattern worth explaining, not an explanation.

### Why it matters

Correlation is the first tool for choosing which variables to look at more closely, for spotting redundant columns before modelling and for checking whether a metric moves with the business outcome you care about. Used carelessly it produces confident but wrong stories.

## Syntax and key functions

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

np.cov(x, y)                      # 2x2 covariance matrix (sample, divisor n - 1)
np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]           # Pearson r
df["a"].corr(df["b"])             # Pearson by default
df["a"].corr(df["b"], method="spearman")
df.corr(numeric_only=True)        # correlation matrix of all numeric columns
stats.pearsonr(x, y)              # (r, p-value)
stats.spearmanr(x, y)             # (rho, p-value)
```

| Function | Returns |
|---|---|
| `np.cov(x, y)[0, 1]` | Sample covariance of `x` and `y`. |
| `np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]` | Pearson correlation. |
| `Series.corr(other, method=...)` | Correlation; method `pearson` or `spearman`; missing pairs are skipped. |
| `DataFrame.corr(numeric_only=True)` | Matrix of pairwise correlations. |
| `scipy.stats.pearsonr` / `spearmanr` | Coefficient and a p-value for the null hypothesis of no association. |
| `Series.rank()` | Ranks, with ties given the average rank. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Covariance by hand, and the effect of units

Six pairs: advertising spend `x` (in thousands) and sales `y`.

| x | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | 2 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 7 | 9 |

```python run
import numpy as np

x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], dtype=float)
y = np.array([2, 4, 5, 4, 7, 9], dtype=float)
by_hand = ((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())).sum() / (len(x) - 1)
print(round(by_hand, 3), round(np.cov(x, y)[0, 1], 3))
print(round(np.cov(x, x)[0, 1], 3), round(x.var(ddof=1), 3))
print(round(np.cov(x / 100, y)[0, 1], 3))
```

```output
4.3 4.3
3.5 3.5
0.043
```

The covariance is 4.3, positive because high spend goes with high sales. The covariance of `x` with itself, 3.5, is the variance of `x`. If `x` is rescaled to hundreds (divided by 100) the covariance becomes 0.043, a hundred times smaller, with no change in the relationship. That is why we standardise.

### Example 2: Pearson r from its definition

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], dtype=float)
y = np.array([2, 4, 5, 4, 7, 9], dtype=float)
r = np.cov(x, y)[0, 1] / (x.std(ddof=1) * y.std(ddof=1))
print(round(r, 4), round(np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1], 4))
r_s, p = stats.pearsonr(x, y)
print(round(r_s, 4), round(p, 4))
print(round(np.corrcoef(x / 100, y)[0, 1], 4), round(np.corrcoef(y, x)[0, 1], 4))
df = pd.DataFrame({"x": x, "y": y, "z": -y})
print(df.corr().round(2).to_string())
```

```output
0.9256 0.9256
0.9256 0.0081
0.9256 0.9256
      x     y     z
x  1.00  0.93 -0.93
y  0.93  1.00 -1.00
z -0.93 -1.00  1.00
```

`r` is 0.9256, strong and positive, and every route gives the same number. The p-value of 0.0081 tests the null hypothesis that the true correlation is 0 (Day 90's idea): with only six points, such a strong pattern would be unusual if there were no association. Rescaling `x` or swapping the variables leaves `r` unchanged. The matrix shows `z = -y` has `r` of -1 with `y`: a perfect negative line.

### Example 3: An outlier, Pearson and Spearman

Add one extra week with very high spend (20) and low sales (1) to the data of Example 1.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], dtype=float)
y = np.array([2, 4, 5, 4, 7, 9], dtype=float)
for label, a, b in [("without", x, y), ("with", np.append(x, 20), np.append(y, 1))]:
    print(label, round(stats.pearsonr(a, b)[0], 3), round(stats.spearmanr(a, b)[0], 3))
```

```output
without 0.926 0.899
with -0.35 0.18
```

Without the extra week Pearson is 0.926 and Spearman 0.899. With it, Pearson drops to -0.35: a single point turned a strong positive correlation into a moderate negative one. Spearman falls to 0.18 but stays positive, because the outlier is only the largest value of `x` and the smallest of `y` in rank terms and moves just one point in the ranking. Neither number is "right"; the right action is to look at the point and decide whether it is an error or a real event.

### Example 4: Curves and monotone relationships

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

xs = np.arange(-5, 6, dtype=float)
print(round(np.corrcoef(xs, xs ** 2)[0, 1], 3))
xe = np.arange(1, 9, dtype=float)
ye = np.exp(xe)
print(round(stats.pearsonr(xe, ye)[0], 3), round(stats.spearmanr(xe, ye)[0], 3))
```

```output
0.0
0.776 1.0
```

For `y = x²` on -5 to 5 the correlation is exactly 0.0 although `y` is a function of `x`: Pearson only detects straight lines. For `y = exp(x)` the curve always rises, so Spearman is 1.0, while Pearson is 0.776 because the points do not lie on a line. A coefficient close to 0 therefore means "no linear relationship", not "no relationship".

### Example 5: A confounder

Simulated daily data: temperature drives ice cream sales and drownings. Neither affects the other.

```python run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
temp = rng.normal(25, 5, 200)
ice = 2 * temp + rng.normal(0, 6, 200)
drown = 0.3 * temp + rng.normal(0, 2, 200)
print(round(np.corrcoef(ice, drown)[0, 1], 2))

order = np.argsort(temp)
bands = np.array_split(order, 4)
print([round(float(np.corrcoef(ice[b], drown[b])[0, 1]), 2) for b in bands])

def remove_linear(v, control):
    slope, intercept = np.polyfit(control, v, 1)
    return v - (slope * control + intercept)

print(round(np.corrcoef(remove_linear(ice, temp), remove_linear(drown, temp))[0, 1], 2))
```

```output
0.44
[0.0, 0.11, -0.09, 0.07]
-0.13
```

Over all days, ice cream sales and drownings correlate at 0.44, which looks like a finding. Within each quarter of the temperature range (days of similar temperature) the correlations are near zero (between -0.09 and 0.11). After removing the straight-line effect of temperature from both variables the correlation is -0.13. The relationship came from temperature, not from ice cream. The seed makes this reproducible, and since the sample is random, small values such as -0.13 are the sort of noise to expect from 200 rows.

## Real-world applications

- **Choosing metrics.** Correlating weekly marketing spend with weekly sales shows whether the relationship is worth modelling, and Spearman guards against one unusual week (Examples 2 and 3).
- **Removing redundant columns.** A correlation matrix over numeric features flags pairs with `|r|` near 1, such as price in two currencies, so that one can be dropped (Example 2).
- **Interpreting dashboards.** When a report shows two metrics moving together, the confounder check (Example 5) prevents a story such as "discounts cause seasonality".
- **Data quality.** An unexpected correlation of exactly 1 or -1 between two columns often means one was derived from the other.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Covariance has units; Pearson `r = cov / (std_x * std_y)` is unit-free, between -1 and 1, symmetric in `x` and `y`.
- `r` measures straight-line association only; `r²` is the share of variation a line accounts for.
- Spearman is Pearson on ranks; it detects monotone relationships and resists outliers.
- Read a scatter plot for direction, form, strength and outliers before trusting a number.
- Correlation can come from reverse causation, coincidence or a confounder; it does not prove causation.

### Common mistakes

- Reading `r = 0` as "no relationship", when a curve can give 0.
- Reporting a coefficient without a plot and missing an outlier that created or hid it.
- Using the covariance size to judge strength; it depends on units.
- Describing `r = 0.5` as "50% related" instead of `r² = 0.25`.
- Using `np.cov` and forgetting that it returns a 2 x 2 matrix, so the covariance is at `[0, 1]`.
- Concluding causation from a strong coefficient in observational data.

### Revision notes

- `cov = sum((x - mx)(y - my)) / (n - 1)`; `r = cov / (sx * sy)`.
- `np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]`, `df.corr()`, `stats.pearsonr`, `stats.spearmanr`.
- Outliers move Pearson; curves hide from Pearson; monotone curves give Spearman 1.
- Check for a confounder: compare within bands of the third variable.
