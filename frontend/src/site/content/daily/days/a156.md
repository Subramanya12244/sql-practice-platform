## Objectives

- Turn a business request into a question, a set of metrics and a plan of evidence before running any query.
- Gather evidence with SQL and a small pandas model, and keep every number traceable to a query or an assumption.
- Build a simple business case with scenarios, a break-even point and a statement of what it depends on.
- Write a one-page business case with the situation, evidence, options, recommendation, risks and next step.
- Check the write-up against its numbers, so that every figure in the text matches the analysis.

## Understand

### The project

The request: management of the Northwind trading company noticed that revenue moves a lot from quarter to quarter and asks whether a **win-back campaign** aimed at customers who have stopped ordering is worth running. You will deliver a one-page business case. The project combines what the business-analytics phase taught: the revenue picture (Day 144), customer segments and recency (Days 145 and 154), a campaign return with assumptions and a test (Days 148 and 155), and a written recommendation (Days 141, 153 and 155). Nothing here needs a new technique. The skill being practised is the order of work and the discipline of linking each claim to evidence.

### The order of work

1. **Question and decision.** Write it in one sentence: "Should we spend about 1,400 on a personal win-back campaign for customers who ordered last more than a year ago?" Name the decision-maker and what they can choose: go, do not go, or test first.
2. **Metrics and definitions.** Define lapsed (no order in the 365 days before the as-of date 1999-01-01), revenue (net of discounts, from the order lines), and success (incremental gross profit above cost).
3. **Situation.** The revenue picture over time and the customer base split into active and lapsed, so that the reader sees the size of the group in question.
4. **Evidence and model.** The value of a lapsed customer in the past, and a simple model of the campaign with three scenarios for the response rate. The response rate is the key unknown, so present it as a range, together with the **break-even response rate**: the response at which the campaign earns exactly its cost.
5. **Recommendation.** Choose one of the options and say why, using the model, in a form the manager can act on.
6. **Risks, assumptions and next step.** What could make you wrong and what would you check or test next.

### Traceability

Every number in the write-up must come from a query or a stated assumption. A practical habit is to keep a short **evidence table** while working: the figure, where it came from (query name), and whether it is a fact (measured in the data) or an assumption (chosen). In the finished case the facts and assumptions are visibly different, for example "57 customers (measured)" and "response rate 5 to 15 percent (assumed)". This is also what protects the analyst in the review meeting: when someone challenges a number, the source is at hand, and when someone challenges an assumption, it is easy to change it and show the result. When you finish, **re-read the text against the outputs**: a number that was updated in the model but not in the text is a very common error in business cases.

### Structure of the write-up

A one-page business case has a fixed skeleton that readers learn to scan: a **headline** that states the recommendation, **situation** (two or three sentences with the key figures), **evidence** (three or four bullet points with numbers), **options** (go, do not go, test first, with the expected outcome of each), **recommendation** (one option and the reason), **risks and assumptions** and the **next step** with an owner and a date. Write the headline last, and keep each part short: a manager who reads only the headline and the recommendation should reach the right decision. Use plain words. Instead of "the incremental ROI is negative in the pessimistic scenario" write "if only one customer in twenty responds, the campaign loses money".

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Most analysis work ends in a document that someone else reads and acts on. A correct analysis with a vague write-up is rarely used, and a clear write-up with unsupported numbers can mislead. The misconception to avoid is that the model is the deliverable. The deliverable is the decision it supports, with the evidence, the uncertainty and the next step visible. Another is that a business case must claim certainty. In this project the honest result is an expected profit that depends on a response rate nobody knows yet, and the sensible recommendation is to test.


```python
break_even_response = cost_per_contact / (order_value * margin)
```

| Formula | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `last_order < DATE '1998-01-01'` | Lapsed: last order more than 365 days before 1999-01-01. |
| `revenue / orders` | Average order value of the group. |
| `cost / (value x margin)` | Break-even response rate. |
| `n x response x value x margin - n x cost` | Expected profit of the campaign. |
| `f"{x:,.0f}"` | A number with thousands separators for the text. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: The situation

Revenue, orders, customers and average order value for each year. This is the situation paragraph of the case: the business has about the same number of orders each year, and revenue fluctuates with the order value.

```sql run
SELECT EXTRACT(year FROM o.order_date)::int AS yr,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS orders,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.customer_id) AS customers,
       ROUND(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount))::numeric, 0) AS revenue,
       ROUND((SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) / COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id))::numeric, 0) AS aov
FROM orders o
JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
```

```output
yr | orders | customers | revenue | aov
1996 | 34 | 34 | 52325 | 1539
1997 | 33 | 33 | 40059 | 1214
1998 | 33 | 32 | 45936 | 1392
```

Orders per year are almost constant (33 or 34) and almost every order comes from a different customer, so repeat buying is rare. Revenue is therefore driven by the average order value and by who orders.

### Example 2: Who is lapsed, and what were they worth?

With the as-of date of 1999-01-01, customers whose last order was before 1998-01-01 are lapsed. The query counts the customers of each group, their past orders and revenue, and the average order value. These are the facts that feed the model.

```sql run
WITH c AS (
  SELECT o.customer_id,
         MAX(o.order_date) AS last_order,
         COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS n,
         SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) AS revenue
  FROM orders o JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
  GROUP BY o.customer_id
)
SELECT CASE WHEN last_order >= DATE '1998-01-01' THEN 'active' ELSE 'lapsed' END AS status,
       COUNT(*) AS customers,
       SUM(n) AS orders,
       ROUND(SUM(revenue)::numeric, 0) AS past_revenue,
       ROUND((SUM(revenue) / SUM(n))::numeric, 0) AS aov
FROM c
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
```

```output
status | customers | orders | past_revenue | aov
active | 32 | 40 | 59025 | 1476
lapsed | 57 | 60 | 79295 | 1322
```

The lapsed group has 57 customers, about two thirds of the customers, and a past average order value of 1,322. Fifty-seven customers is a small group for any campaign, which matters for how a test can be designed.

### Example 3: The campaign model

The inputs are the facts of Example 2 (57 customers, average order value 1,322) and assumptions (gross margin 25 percent, a cost of 25 per customer for a personal call). Three response scenarios give the expected profit, and the break-even response rate is computed.

```python run
n, aov = 57, 1322.0                 # facts from Example 2
margin, cost_per_contact = 0.25, 25.0   # assumptions
total_cost = n * cost_per_contact
print("campaign cost:", round(total_cost))
for name, response in (("low", 0.05), ("base", 0.10), ("high", 0.15)):
    orders = n * response
    profit = orders * aov * margin - total_cost
    print(f"{name:5} response {response:.0%}: extra orders {orders:4.1f}, expected profit {profit:7.0f}")
print("break-even response rate:", round(cost_per_contact / (aov * margin) * 100, 1), "%")
```

```output
campaign cost: 1425
low   response 5%: extra orders  2.9, expected profit    -483
base  response 10%: extra orders  5.7, expected profit     459
high  response 15%: extra orders  8.5, expected profit    1401
break-even response rate: 7.6 %
```

The base case earns a profit and the low case loses money. The break-even response rate is about 7.6 percent, between the low and base assumptions, so the decision depends on a number that is not known.

### Example 4: The write-up, assembled from the numbers

A template fills the text from the same variables as the model, so that the text and the numbers cannot disagree. The assumptions are listed beside the facts. The output is the one-page case.

```python run
n, aov = 57, 1322.0
margin, cost_per_contact = 0.25, 25.0
cost = n * cost_per_contact
be = cost_per_contact / (aov * margin)
profit = {r: n * r * aov * margin - cost for r in (0.05, 0.10, 0.15)}
print("HEADLINE: Test a win-back campaign on a pilot before committing to it.")
print()
print(f"SITUATION: {n} of Northwind's 89 customers have not ordered for a year. Each past order was worth about {aov:,.0f} (fact).")
print(f"EVIDENCE: a personal call costs {cost_per_contact:.0f} per customer, {cost:,.0f} for the group (assumption). At a {margin:.0%} margin the campaign breaks even at a {be:.1%} response rate.")
print(f"OPTIONS: go (profit {profit[0.10]:,.0f} at 10% response, {profit[0.05]:,.0f} at 5%); do nothing (0); pilot on a part of the group first.")
print("RECOMMENDATION: run a pilot on 20 customers, because the profit depends on a response rate that we do not know.")
print("RISKS: response rate and margin are assumptions; the group is small, so one reply changes the result.")
print("NEXT STEP: sales manager calls 20 customers and reports orders within 8 weeks.")
```

```output
HEADLINE: Test a win-back campaign on a pilot before committing to it.

SITUATION: 57 of Northwind's 89 customers have not ordered for a year. Each past order was worth about 1,322 (fact).
EVIDENCE: a personal call costs 25 per customer, 1,425 for the group (assumption). At a 25% margin the campaign breaks even at a 7.6% response rate.
OPTIONS: go (profit 459 at 10% response, -483 at 5%); do nothing (0); pilot on a part of the group first.
RECOMMENDATION: run a pilot on 20 customers, because the profit depends on a response rate that we do not know.
RISKS: response rate and margin are assumptions; the group is small, so one reply changes the result.
NEXT STEP: sales manager calls 20 customers and reports orders within 8 weeks.
```

The recommendation follows from the model: the case is positive only above a response rate that cannot be verified yet, and the cost of testing is lower than the cost of being wrong. A reader who reads only the headline and the recommendation gets the right decision.

## Real-world applications

- **Business cases for new initiatives**: the same structure of situation, evidence, options, recommendation and risks is used when asking for budget for a campaign, a tool or a hire.
- **Consulting-style deliverables**: an evidence table linking each figure to a query lets a reviewer check the analysis quickly.
- **Decision logs**: the stated assumptions and next step make it possible to check later whether the assumptions were right and learn from the outcome.
- **Portfolio work**: a one-page case with its code is a typical piece shown in an analyst's portfolio or interview.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Start with the question and the decision; define the metrics before the queries.
- Separate facts (measured) from assumptions (chosen), and present the key assumption as a range with its break-even.
- Build the text from the same variables as the model, and re-read it against the outputs.
- A one-page case has headline, situation, evidence, options, recommendation, risks and next step.
- When the decisive number is unknown, recommend a cheap test.

### Common mistakes

- Starting with queries and no question, which produces many numbers and no decision.
- Presenting an assumption as a fact, such as a response rate with no basis. Label it and give a range.
- Updating the model but not the text, so that the write-up quotes old numbers. Generate the text from the variables.
- Hiding the uncertainty to sound confident. State the break-even point and what the result depends on.
- Writing for other analysts instead of the decision-maker. Lead with the recommendation in plain words.

### Revision notes

- Order of work: question, definitions, situation, evidence and model, recommendation, risks and next step.
- Break-even response = `cost per contact / (order value x margin)`.
- Evidence table: figure, source, fact or assumption.
- A write-up that is generated from variables cannot disagree with its model.
