## Objectives

- Reconcile a total between two systems and state the difference in absolute and relative terms.
- Apply tolerance rules (absolute, relative and rounding-aware) and explain when each is appropriate.
- Break a difference into explained categories (timing, exclusions, rounding) and an unexplained remainder.
- Reconcile by group, so that one large error cannot hide behind many small agreeing groups.
- Write a reconciliation result as a short, factual explanation that someone else can act on.

## Understand

### Reconciliation versus validation

Day 136 compared a source with a target to find which rows differ. **Reconciliation** answers a business question at a higher level: do the totals of two systems agree, and if they do not, why not? A finance team asks "the warehouse says January revenue is 41,200.00 and the ledger says 41,350.00: which is right, and what accounts for the 150?" The goal is not a perfect match at any price. The goal is a difference that is either zero, or fully explained and small enough that everybody accepts it.

The word comes from accounting, where a **reconciliation** is a statement that starts from one balance, lists every item that explains the gap and ends at the other balance. Data teams borrow that structure because it is auditable: each line has a cause, an amount and an owner.

### Differences are normal, unexplained differences are not

Two systems that measure the same thing rarely agree to the cent at any single moment. The usual causes of a legitimate difference are these.

- **Timing.** The warehouse loads at midnight; the ledger kept accepting orders afterwards. The same order exists on both sides but on different days, or only on one side until the next run.
- **Scope rules.** The pipeline drops failed orders or test accounts by design, so the warehouse total is lower on purpose.
- **Rounding.** Converting 3 prices of 0.333 into 2-decimal currency and summing gives a different total from summing first and rounding once.
- **Late or corrected records.** An order was refunded after the extract.

Everything left after these causes is an **unexplained difference**, and it is the number that decides whether you have a bug. A good reconciliation never ends with "about 150 off": it ends with "148 explained by 2 late orders and failed orders, 2 unexplained, within tolerance".

### Tolerance rules

A **tolerance** is the largest difference you accept as "equal". Choose it from the data, not from convenience.

- An **absolute tolerance** accepts a fixed amount: `abs(a - b) <= 0.01`. It suits sums of money with a known rounding rule, where the difference can only be a few cents.
- A **relative tolerance** accepts a fraction of the size: `abs(a - b) <= 0.001 * max(abs(a), abs(b))`. It suits large and small totals alike, because a 5 unit difference matters on a total of 50 and not on a total of 5 million.
- **Zero tolerance** is correct for counts and for integer amounts such as cents. A count that is off by one is a lost row, never rounding.

A tolerance must be written down next to the check, with the reason. Quiet widening ("make the test pass") is the main way reconciliation checks stop meaning anything. Python's `math.isclose(a, b, rel_tol=..., abs_tol=...)` implements both at once, but it needs care: with only `rel_tol`, comparing with 0 never passes unless the values are exactly equal, so give an `abs_tol` too.

### Why floating point needs care

Binary floating point cannot represent most decimal fractions exactly. Adding `0.1` to a running total ten times in a loop gives `0.9999999999999999`, not `1.0`. A reconciliation that compares float sums with `==` then fails on a correct load. There are two safe choices: store money in integer cents (the pipeline does), or use `decimal.Decimal` with an explicit rounding. In SQL, use `numeric` rather than `real` or `double precision` for money. Then the difference you compute is exact and the tolerance only has to cover real rounding policy.

### Reconciling by group

A single grand total can hide problems: one day 500 too high and another day 500 too low cancel to zero. **Reconcile by group**: per day, per customer, per currency, per partition. Each group gets its own difference and its own tolerance verdict. Groups are also how you narrow a failure down. A mismatch in one date partition tells the team which rerun to schedule. The cost is volume, so print only the groups that fail and keep the overall verdict.

### Explaining a difference: the waterfall

The accounting structure is a **waterfall** (also called a bridge): start from the source total, subtract or add each explained item, and arrive at the target total. In code: compute the total difference, then classify rows into reasons, sum each reason and subtract. What remains is the unexplained part. Classify with rules that mirror the pipeline: rows after the load cutoff are timing, rows with status `failed` are an exclusion. If you classify by guessing from the numbers, you will "explain" a bug.

### Common misconception

Learners often think the aim of reconciliation is to make the difference zero, and so they patch the target. The aim is to understand it. A target forced to equal the source by an adjustment row hides the defect that caused the gap and breaks the audit trail. Another misconception is that a small difference is harmless: 2 cents on one run is fine, but 2 cents on every one of 1,000 orders is a systematic rounding rule you should fix once, and it is only visible when you look at the per-group differences.

### Why it matters

Reconciliation is how data engineers earn trust. Finance, operations and auditors compare your numbers with systems of record; a team that can say "here is the bridge, here is what remains and why we accept it" ends the argument in minutes. A team that cannot spends days defending a total.

## Syntax and key functions

Tolerance checks:

```python
import math
from decimal import Decimal, ROUND_HALF_UP

abs(a - b) <= 0.01                                   # absolute
abs(a - b) <= 0.001 * max(abs(a), abs(b))            # relative
math.isclose(a, b, rel_tol=1e-9, abs_tol=0.005)      # both, either one passes
Decimal("2.675").quantize(Decimal("0.01"), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP)   # Decimal('2.68')
```

A group reconciliation in PostgreSQL:

```sql-syntax
SELECT COALESCE(s.grp, t.grp) AS grp,
       COALESCE(s.total, 0) AS source_total,
       COALESCE(t.total, 0) AS target_total,
       COALESCE(s.total, 0) - COALESCE(t.total, 0) AS diff
FROM (SELECT grp, SUM(amount) AS total FROM source GROUP BY grp) s
FULL OUTER JOIN (SELECT grp, SUM(amount) AS total FROM target GROUP BY grp) t ON t.grp = s.grp;
```

| Item | What it does or returns |
|---|---|
| `abs(a - b)` | The size of the difference, ignoring its sign. |
| `math.isclose(a, b, rel_tol, abs_tol)` | True when the values are within either tolerance. |
| `Decimal("0.10")` | Exact decimal number; build it from a string, never from a float. |
| `Decimal.quantize(Decimal("0.01"), rounding=...)` | Rounds to two places with an explicit rule. |
| `df.groupby(key)["amount"].sum()` | Total per group. |
| `Series.sub(other, fill_value=0)` | Difference that treats a missing group as 0. |
| `COALESCE(x, 0)` | Replaces a NULL total (a missing group) with 0. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Totals, differences and a tolerance verdict

Two small revenue totals are compared in absolute and relative terms. The verdict function takes both tolerances, so a reader can see what "equal" meant for each check. Counts use zero tolerance, because a count cannot be off by rounding.

```python run
def compare(name, source, target, abs_tol=0.0, rel_tol=0.0):
    diff = target - source
    limit = max(abs_tol, rel_tol * max(abs(source), abs(target)))
    verdict = "OK" if abs(diff) <= limit else "FAIL"
    print(f"{name}: source={source} target={target} diff={round(diff, 2):+g} limit={limit:.2f} {verdict}")

compare("order count", 120, 120)
compare("order count", 120, 119)
compare("revenue (cents)", 4120000, 4120000)
compare("revenue (eur)", 41200.00, 41200.04, abs_tol=0.05)
compare("revenue (eur)", 41200.00, 41350.00, rel_tol=0.001)
compare("small total", 50.0, 55.0, rel_tol=0.001)
```

```output
order count: source=120 target=120 diff=+0 limit=0.00 OK
order count: source=120 target=119 diff=-1 limit=0.00 FAIL
revenue (cents): source=4120000 target=4120000 diff=+0 limit=0.00 OK
revenue (eur): source=41200.0 target=41200.04 diff=+0.04 limit=0.05 OK
revenue (eur): source=41200.0 target=41350.0 diff=+150 limit=41.35 FAIL
small total: source=50.0 target=55.0 diff=+5 limit=0.06 FAIL
```

The 150 difference on a total of 41,200 exceeds its 0.1 percent limit of 41.35. The last line fails too: a difference of 5 on a total of 50 is 10 percent, far above its limit of 0.06. A relative tolerance scales with the size of the total.

### Example 2: Floats, `isclose` and `Decimal`

Adding `0.1` ten times in a loop gives a float that does not equal `1.0` (recent Python versions make the built-in `sum` of floats more accurate, so the loop shows the effect plainly). `math.isclose` passes it with a tolerance, and `Decimal` built from strings sums exactly. The last lines show a rounding-policy difference: three prices of 0.333 summed and then rounded give a different cent total than rounding each price first. A reconciliation must state which rule the target uses.

```python run
import math
from decimal import Decimal, ROUND_HALF_UP

total = 0.0
for _ in range(10):
    total += 0.1
print(total == 1.0, round(total, 12))
print(math.isclose(total, 1.0, rel_tol=1e-9))
print(math.isclose(0.0, 1e-12, rel_tol=1e-9), math.isclose(0.0, 1e-12, rel_tol=1e-9, abs_tol=1e-9))

print(sum(Decimal("0.1") for _ in range(10)) == Decimal("1.0"))

prices = [Decimal("0.333")] * 3
cent = Decimal("0.01")
print("round once:", sum(prices).quantize(cent, rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP))
print("round each:", sum(p.quantize(cent, rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP) for p in prices))
```

```output
False 1.0
True
False True
True
round once: 1.00
round each: 0.99
```

Comparing 0.0 with a value that is merely tiny fails under `rel_tol` alone, because a relative tolerance of 0 is zero. Add `abs_tol` whenever a total can legitimately be near zero.

### Example 3: A waterfall that explains the difference

The ledger holds six orders; the warehouse loaded at a cutoff of 2026-01-08. The waterfall classifies ledger rows by documented reason (failed orders are excluded on purpose, orders posted after the cutoff are timing), then compares the warehouse with what remains. Whatever is left is unexplained and tells the team to look at one order.

```python run
ledger = [
    (1, 1000, "paid", "2026-01-05"),
    (2, 2500, "paid", "2026-01-06"),
    (3, 400, "failed", "2026-01-06"),
    (4, 1800, "paid", "2026-01-09"),
    (5, 700, "paid", "2026-01-07"),
    (6, 1200, "paid", "2026-01-08"),
]
warehouse = {1: 1000, 2: 2500, 5: 700, 6: 1000}
CUTOFF = "2026-01-08"

ledger_total = sum(r[1] for r in ledger)
wh_total = sum(warehouse.values())
failed = sum(r[1] for r in ledger if r[2] == "failed")
late = sum(r[1] for r in ledger if r[2] != "failed" and r[3] > CUTOFF)
in_scope = [r for r in ledger if r[2] != "failed" and r[3] <= CUTOFF]
unexplained = wh_total - (ledger_total - failed - late)

print("ledger total:", ledger_total)
print("less failed orders (excluded by rule):", -failed)
print("less orders after cutoff (timing):", -late)
print("expected warehouse total:", ledger_total - failed - late)
print("warehouse total:", wh_total)
print("unexplained:", unexplained)
print("orders that differ:", [r[0] for r in in_scope if warehouse.get(r[0]) != r[1]])
```

```output
ledger total: 7600
less failed orders (excluded by rule): -400
less orders after cutoff (timing): -1800
expected warehouse total: 5400
warehouse total: 5200
unexplained: -200
orders that differ: [6]
```

The unexplained -200 (the warehouse is short) is traced to order 6 by the last line. The explained 2,200 needed no investigation because each part has a rule behind it.

### Example 4: Group reconciliation in PostgreSQL

Per-day totals from both tables are joined with a full outer join so that a day missing on one side still appears. The grand totals differ by 100, but the per-day view shows that two days are off in opposite directions and one day is missing completely: a hidden problem that the grand total partly cancels.

```sql run
CREATE TABLE ledger (order_id int, day date, amount numeric(12,2));
CREATE TABLE wh (order_id int, day date, amount numeric(12,2));
INSERT INTO ledger VALUES (1, '2026-01-05', 100.00), (2, '2026-01-05', 50.00), (3, '2026-01-06', 80.00), (4, '2026-01-07', 60.00);
INSERT INTO wh VALUES (1, '2026-01-05', 100.00), (2, '2026-01-05', 80.00), (3, '2026-01-06', 30.00);

SELECT COALESCE(s.day, t.day) AS day,
       COALESCE(s.total, 0) AS source_total,
       COALESCE(t.total, 0) AS target_total,
       COALESCE(s.total, 0) - COALESCE(t.total, 0) AS diff
FROM (SELECT day, SUM(amount) AS total FROM ledger GROUP BY day) s
FULL OUTER JOIN (SELECT day, SUM(amount) AS total FROM wh GROUP BY day) t ON t.day = s.day
ORDER BY day
LIMIT 10;
```

```output
day | source_total | target_total | diff
2026-01-05 | 150.00 | 180.00 | -30.00
2026-01-06 | 80.00 | 30.00 | 50.00
2026-01-07 | 60.00 | 0 | 60.00
```

The three days are off by -30, +50 and +60, so the grand-total difference is only 80 and the first day's opposite sign is partly hidden. Per-group tolerances flag all three days.

### Example 5: A reconciliation report with a written explanation

The function reconciles totals per currency, applies a per-currency tolerance and builds sentences from the numbers. The explanation text is part of the output, so the person reading the log does not have to recompute anything. The numbers are integers (cents), so the tolerances are exact.

```python run
source = {"USD": 1250000, "EUR": 830000, "GBP": 210000}
target = {"USD": 1250000, "EUR": 829400, "GBP": 210500}
tolerance = {"USD": 0, "EUR": 1000, "GBP": 100}
explained = {"EUR": 600}

def reconcile(source, target, tolerance, explained):
    lines = []
    for cur in sorted(source):
        diff = target[cur] - source[cur]
        unexplained = diff + explained.get(cur, 0)
        if diff == 0:
            lines.append(f"{cur}: match")
        elif abs(unexplained) <= tolerance[cur]:
            lines.append(f"{cur}: difference {diff:+} cents, explained {explained.get(cur, 0)}, remainder {unexplained:+} within tolerance {tolerance[cur]}")
        else:
            lines.append(f"{cur}: INVESTIGATE difference {diff:+} cents, remainder {unexplained:+} exceeds tolerance {tolerance[cur]}")
    return lines

for line in reconcile(source, target, tolerance, explained):
    print(line)
```

```output
EUR: difference -600 cents, explained 600, remainder +0 within tolerance 1000
GBP: INVESTIGATE difference +500 cents, remainder +500 exceeds tolerance 100
USD: match
```

EUR is short by 600 cents, and the same 600 was recorded as explained (a refund after the extract), so the remainder is 0. GBP is 500 high and nothing explains it, which exceeds its tolerance of 100, so the line says to investigate.

## Real-world applications

- **Finance close.** At month end the warehouse revenue is reconciled to the ledger per currency and per day. A waterfall shows timing items and the unexplained remainder; accountants sign off only when the remainder is inside tolerance.
- **Payments pipeline.** The sum of settled payments is reconciled with the provider's statement. Group-level checks per settlement date show which file failed to load.
- **Migration cut-over.** Before an old report is switched off, the old and new totals are reconciled over three months by day, and every unexplained difference is closed or documented.
- **Metric disputes.** When two dashboards show different revenue, a reconciliation by dimension (country, channel) finds which filter or definition differs.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Reconciliation compares totals between systems and explains the gap; validation (Day 136) compares rows.
- Use zero tolerance for counts and integer cents, absolute tolerance for rounded money, relative tolerance for scale-dependent totals.
- Use `Decimal` or integer cents for money. Floats do not sum exactly.
- Reconcile by group, then overall, and bridge the difference with a waterfall: explained items first, unexplained last.
- Write the tolerance and the reason next to the check, and record explanations in the output.

### Common mistakes

- **Comparing float sums with `==`.** A correct load fails (for example `0.9999999999999999`). Use integer cents, `Decimal`, or `math.isclose`.
- **`isclose` with only `rel_tol` near zero.** Comparing 0 with a small number fails. Add `abs_tol`.
- **Only checking the grand total.** Opposite errors cancel. Check each group too.
- **Forcing the target to match with an adjustment row.** The cause stays hidden. Explain it or fix the pipeline.
- **Widening the tolerance until the check passes.** Decide the tolerance from the rounding rule before looking at the failure.

### Revision notes

- Difference = target minus source; verdict compares `abs(diff)` with the tolerance.
- Tolerances: zero for counts and cents, absolute for rounded money, relative for big totals.
- Waterfall: source total, minus exclusions, minus timing, equals expected target; remainder is unexplained.
- Group first (`GROUP BY` and `FULL OUTER JOIN`), then the grand total.
- `Decimal("0.1")` from strings; `quantize` with an explicit rounding mode.
