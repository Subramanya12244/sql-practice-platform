## Objectives

- Compute probabilities by counting equally likely outcomes, and use the complement, addition and multiplication rules.
- Calculate a conditional probability from a table of counts, and test whether two events are independent.
- Use the normal distribution: the 68-95-99.7 rule, areas under the curve with `scipy.stats.norm`, and percentile cut-offs.
- Convert values to z-scores and use them to compare values on different scales and to flag unusual ones.

## Understand

### Probability as a number between 0 and 1

The **probability** of an event is a number from 0 to 1 that measures how likely the event is. Zero means impossible, 1 means certain, and 0.25 means it happens in about one quarter of many repeats. The simplest case has equally likely outcomes: the probability of an event is the number of outcomes where it happens divided by the total number of outcomes. A fair die has six outcomes, so the probability of a 4 is 1/6, and the probability of an even number is 3/6. A probability is a statement about what happens over many repeats, not a promise about the next one. After five heads in a row, a fair coin is still 50-50 on the next toss; the belief that it is "due" a tail is called the gambler's fallacy.

An **event** is a set of outcomes, such as "the sum of two dice is 7". With two dice there are 36 outcomes, each pair (first die, second die) equally likely, and 6 of them sum to 7, so the probability is 6/36 = 1/6. Listing outcomes with a short program is a reliable way to check a probability you calculated by reasoning.

### The rules

The **complement rule**: the probability that an event does not happen is 1 minus the probability that it does. This is useful for "at least one" questions. The chance of at least one six in two dice is 1 minus the chance of no six, which is 1 - (5/6)(5/6) = 11/36.

The **addition rule**: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B). The subtraction avoids counting outcomes in both events twice. If A and B cannot happen together, they are **mutually exclusive** and the last term is 0.

The **multiplication rule** for **independent** events, which do not influence each other: P(A and B) = P(A) * P(B). Two separate coin tosses are independent, so two heads has probability 1/4. Independence is an assumption that must be checked, not a default. Events like "customer is in the UK" and "order is returned" may be linked.

### Conditional probability

The **conditional probability** P(A | B), read "A given B", is the probability of A when you already know B has happened. It restricts attention to the cases where B is true: P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). With a table of counts it is a ratio of counts. If 80 orders are from the UK and 20 of those were returned, then P(returned | UK) = 20/80 = 0.25, while P(returned) over all orders could be different. Rearranged, the formula gives the general multiplication rule P(A and B) = P(B) * P(A | B). Two events are independent exactly when P(A | B) equals P(A): knowing B changes nothing.

Conditional probability is easily reversed by mistake. P(returned | UK) is not P(UK | returned). The first asks which share of UK orders are returned, the second which share of returned orders are from the UK. They answer different questions and generally have different values.

### The normal distribution

A **distribution** says how likely each value of a variable is. The **normal distribution**, the bell curve, is symmetric around its mean and described fully by two numbers: the mean and the standard deviation (Day 87). Many measurements cluster this way: heights, measurement errors, and, by a result of the next day, averages of many values. The **68-95-99.7 rule** says that about 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two, and about 99.7% within three. For a continuous variable, probability is an **area under the curve**, because the chance of exactly one value is zero. The **cumulative distribution function**, cdf, gives the area to the left of a value: `norm.cdf(x, loc=mean, scale=sd)` is the probability that the variable is at most `x`. The reverse function, the **percent point function** `norm.ppf(p)`, returns the value below which a share `p` of the data lies.

The normal distribution is a model, not a fact about your data. Order amounts and waiting times are skewed (Day 87) and are not normal. Before using it, look at a histogram and the skewness.

### Z-scores

A **z-score** says how many standard deviations a value is above (positive) or below (negative) the mean: z = (x - mean) / sd. It removes the units, so values on different scales can be compared. A student with 78 on an exam where the mean is 70 and the standard deviation 4 (z = 2) did better, relative to the class, than a student with 85 on an exam where the mean is 75 and the standard deviation 10 (z = 1). In roughly normal data, a z-score beyond 2 in either direction happens for about 5% of values and beyond 3 for about 0.3%, which makes large z-scores a common flag for unusual values. A z-score uses the mean and standard deviation, both pulled by outliers, so on skewed data the IQR rule of Day 87 is more robust.

### Common misconception

Learners often treat "unlikely" as "impossible", or the reverse. An event with probability 0.001 will happen in a table with a million independent rows about a thousand times. A second misconception is that the normal curve applies to any column. It applies to columns that are roughly symmetric and bell-shaped, and the z-score threshold of 3 is a convention, not a boundary of reality.

### Why it matters

Fraud rules, quality limits and A/B tests are probability statements. The confidence intervals of Day 89 and the hypothesis tests of Day 90 are built from the normal curve and conditional probabilities. Misreading a conditional probability, for example confusing the chance of a positive test given a disease with the chance of the disease given a positive test, is one of the most common errors in reasoning about data.

## Syntax and key functions

```python nocheck
from itertools import product
outcomes = list(product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))      # all 36 pairs
from fractions import Fraction
Fraction(6, 36)                                      # exact: 1/6
from scipy.stats import norm
norm.cdf(x, loc=mean, scale=sd)                      # P(X <= x)
norm.ppf(0.975)                                      # value with 97.5% below it
z = (s - s.mean()) / s.std()                         # z-scores in pandas
```

| Idea | Formula or call |
|---|---|
| Complement | `1 - P(A)` |
| Addition | `P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)` |
| Independent multiplication | `P(A) * P(B)` |
| Conditional | `P(A and B) / P(B)` |
| `norm.cdf(x, loc, scale)` | area to the left of `x` |
| `norm.ppf(p)` | the value below which a share `p` lies |
| z-score | `(x - mean) / sd` |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Counting outcomes of two dice

All 36 outcomes are listed, and the rules are checked against the counts.

```python run
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product
rolls = list(product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))
def p(event):
    return Fraction(sum(1 for r in rolls if event(r)), len(rolls))
seven = p(lambda r: r[0] + r[1] == 7)
some_six = p(lambda r: 6 in r)
no_six = p(lambda r: 6 not in r)
big = p(lambda r: r[0] + r[1] >= 10)
both = p(lambda r: 6 in r and r[0] + r[1] >= 10)
either = p(lambda r: 6 in r or r[0] + r[1] >= 10)
print(seven, some_six, no_six, some_six == 1 - no_six)
print(big, both, either, either == some_six + big - both)
```

```output
1/6 11/36 25/36 True
1/6 5/36 1/3 True
```

The complement rule gives 11/36 for at least one six. The addition rule holds: 11/36 + 6/36 - 5/36 = 12/36 = 1/3.

### Example 2: Conditional probability and independence

A table of counts gives a conditional probability by division. The reverse conditional is different.

```python run
import pandas as pd
counts = {("UK", "returned"): 20, ("UK", "kept"): 60, ("US", "returned"): 30, ("US", "kept"): 30}
total = sum(counts.values())
uk = counts[("UK", "returned")] + counts[("UK", "kept")]
returned = counts[("UK", "returned")] + counts[("US", "returned")]
p_ret = returned / total
p_ret_given_uk = counts[("UK", "returned")] / uk
p_uk_given_ret = counts[("UK", "returned")] / returned
print(round(p_ret, 3), round(p_ret_given_uk, 3), round(p_uk_given_ret, 3))
print("independent:", abs(p_ret - p_ret_given_uk) < 1e-9)
print(round(uk / total * p_ret_given_uk, 3) == round(counts[("UK", "returned")] / total, 3))
rows = [c for c, n in counts.items() for _ in range(n)]
df = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["country", "status"])
print(pd.crosstab(df["country"], df["status"], normalize="index").round(3).to_string())
```

```output
0.357 0.25 0.4
independent: False
True
status   kept  returned
country                
UK       0.75      0.25
US       0.50      0.50
```

P(returned | UK) is 0.25 while P(returned) is 0.357, so the events are not independent. P(UK | returned) is 0.4, a different question. The crosstab of Day 82 gives the same conditional probabilities by row.

### Example 3: Checking a probability by simulation

A seeded simulation of 100,000 rolls estimates P(sum = 7). Simulation approximates, and the exact value is 1/6.

```python run
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
dice = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(100000, 2))
est = (dice.sum(axis=1) == 7).mean()
print(round(est, 2), round(1 / 6, 2))
print(abs(est - 1 / 6) < 0.01)
```

```output
0.17 0.17
True
```

The estimate is within 0.01 of 1/6. Rerunning with a different seed gives a slightly different estimate, which is what the word estimate means.

### Example 4: The normal distribution

The 68-95-99.7 rule is checked with the cdf. Then exam scores with mean 70 and standard deviation 10 give a probability and a percentile cut-off.

```python run
from scipy.stats import norm
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    print(k, round(norm.cdf(k) - norm.cdf(-k), 4))
print(round(norm.ppf(0.975), 3))
print(round(1 - norm.cdf(85, loc=70, scale=10), 4))
print(round(norm.ppf(0.9, loc=70, scale=10), 2))
```

```output
1 0.6827
2 0.9545
3 0.9973
1.96
0.0668
82.82
```

About 6.7% of scores exceed 85, which is z = 1.5. The top 10% of students score above about 82.8.

### Example 5: Z-scores across two scales

Two exams are on different scales. Z-scores show which student did better relative to their own class. Large z-scores are flagged.

```python run
import pandas as pd
exams = pd.DataFrame({
    "student": list("ABCDEF"),
    "math": [78, 70, 66, 72, 68, 66],
    "essay": [85, 60, 72, 90, 55, 88],
})
for col in ("math", "essay"):
    exams[col + "_z"] = ((exams[col] - exams[col].mean()) / exams[col].std()).round(2)
print(exams[["student", "math_z", "essay_z"]].to_string(index=False))
best_math = exams.loc[exams["math_z"].idxmax(), "student"]
print(best_math, exams.loc[exams["essay_z"].abs() > 1.5, "student"].tolist())
```

```output
student  math_z  essay_z
      A    1.75     0.67
      B    0.00    -1.00
      C   -0.88    -0.20
      D    0.44     1.00
      E   -0.44    -1.33
      F   -0.88     0.87
A []
```

Student A has the highest math z-score. No student passes the threshold of 1.5, so the flag list is empty. Z-scores use the sample standard deviation here, the pandas default.

## Real-world applications

- **Fraud and anomaly flags.** A payments team flags transactions whose z-score within a customer's history exceeds 3 for review.
- **Service targets.** A support manager uses `norm.ppf(0.95)` on roughly normal response times to set a time limit that 95% of tickets meet.
- **Risk of conditional events.** An analyst reports P(returned | UK) and P(returned | US) side by side rather than one overall return rate.
- **Fair comparison.** Scores from different tests or branches are converted to z-scores so they can be ranked on a common scale.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Probability is favourable outcomes over equally likely outcomes, between 0 and 1.
- Complement for "at least one", addition with subtraction of the overlap, multiplication for independent events.
- P(A | B) is P(A and B) divided by P(B); it is not the same as P(B | A).
- The normal distribution is described by its mean and standard deviation: 68% within 1, 95% within 2, 99.7% within 3.
- A z-score counts standard deviations from the mean and puts different scales on one footing.

### Common mistakes

- Multiplying probabilities of events that are not independent. Check with a table of counts first.
- Using `norm.cdf(x)` without `loc` and `scale` for data not centred at 0 with spread 1. The result is for the standard normal.
- Reading P(A | B) as P(B | A). Say each aloud as "of all B, which share is A".
- Applying the normal rule to a skewed column and expecting 95% within two standard deviations. Check the histogram and skewness first.

### Revision notes

- Complement: `1 - P`. Addition: `P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)`.
- Conditional: `P(A and B) / P(B)`.
- `norm.cdf` to the left, `norm.ppf` is the inverse.
- z = (x - mean) / sd.
