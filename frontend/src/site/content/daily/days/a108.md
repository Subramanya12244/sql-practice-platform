## Objectives

- Choose the right bivariate summary for a pair of variables: two numbers, a category and a number, or two categories.
- Measure the strength and direction of a numeric relationship with Pearson and Spearman correlation, and check it with group averages.
- Compare a numeric variable across groups with `groupby`, reporting the group size, the mean and the median together.
- Build and read cross-tabs as row percentages, and run the chi-square test from Day 104 to check whether a difference could be chance.
- Produce a correlation matrix, extract the strongest pairs from it, and describe why a pattern may be confounded.

## Understand

### From one variable to two

**Bivariate analysis** examines the relationship between two variables. After the univariate pass of Day 107 told you what each column looks like alone, the question changes from "what is typical?" to "do these two move together, and how?" The answers to the questions you wrote on Day 106 almost always live here: does a later delivery lower the rating, does a deeper discount raise the quantity, does one product category get returned more often?

The method depends on the types of the two variables. There are three cases, and knowing which one you are in picks the tool.

### Numeric against numeric

When both variables are numbers, the first thing to look at is a scatter plot, one dot per row. Because plotting is covered later (Day 115), here you read the relationship from numbers instead. The **correlation coefficient** summarises the pattern in one value between -1 and +1. You met two versions on Day 101. **Pearson correlation** measures how well the points follow a straight line. **Spearman correlation** applies Pearson to the ranks of the values, so it measures whether one variable consistently rises (or falls) with the other even when the curve is not straight, and extreme values affect it less. The sign gives the direction: positive means both rise together, negative means one falls as the other rises. Values near 0 mean no linear pattern. Rough guides treat about 0.1 as weak, 0.3 as moderate and 0.5 or more as strong, but those words depend on the field.

A single coefficient can hide the shape. Two columns can have a correlation of 0 and still be strongly related if the relationship is U-shaped. A helpful companion is the **binned mean**: cut one variable into bands with `pd.cut`, then show the average of the other in each band. If the averages step steadily down, the relationship is real and roughly monotonic. Always show the number of rows in each band, because a band with three rows says little.

### Categorical against numeric

Here you compare a number across groups: order value by category, rating by device. The tool is `groupby`, which you used on Days 79 and 80. Report the group size, the mean and the median together. The median guards against a skewed group, and the size tells the reader how much to trust the group's average. A difference between group means is only interesting relative to the spread inside the groups; if the groups overlap heavily, the difference is small compared with the noise. A grouped boxplot (later days) or a table of quartiles per group shows that overlap. For a formal check of two groups you can use the t-test from Day 103.

### Categorical against categorical

The tool is the **cross-tab** (`pd.crosstab`, Day 82). Counts alone are misleading when groups have different sizes, so read the table as **row percentages**: within each row, what share falls in each column. If the rows' percentages are all similar, the variables look unrelated; if they differ, there may be an association. The chi-square test of independence from Day 104 gives a p-value for whether the differences could be chance, and Cramér's V gives the strength. With many categories, some cells will have small counts; check the expected counts before trusting the p-value.

### The correlation matrix

A **correlation matrix** is a square table with every numeric variable on both axes, each cell holding the correlation of that pair. It is symmetric, with 1.0 on the diagonal because every variable correlates perfectly with itself. It is a quick screen: scan for the pairs far from zero. But a matrix with 10 variables holds 45 distinct pairs, and with that many comparisons, a few moderate values will appear by chance. Treat the matrix as a place to find questions, not as proof. Also note which pairs are related by construction: `order_total` is computed from `quantity`, `unit_price` and `discount_pct`, so a high correlation between them is arithmetic, not a discovery.

### Correlation is not causation, and a third variable may be hiding

A relationship between two variables does not show that one causes the other (Day 101). Often a third variable, a **confounder**, drives both. Suppose electronics have higher return rates and also higher prices. Price and returns would correlate, but the category might be the real driver. When you see a pattern, ask what else could produce it, and check by splitting the data on the suspected third variable. Another trap is **reverse direction**: slow shipping may lower ratings, but low-rated orders are not what makes shipping slow.

### Missing values in pairs

A correlation uses only the rows where both values are present. If `rating` is missing for a quarter of the orders, a correlation involving rating is computed on the rated orders only, and those may not represent everyone: customers who rate may be happier or angrier than those who do not. State the number of rows behind every bivariate result.

### Common misconception

"A correlation near zero means the variables are unrelated." It means there is no linear (Pearson) or consistently rising or falling (Spearman) pattern; curved patterns can hide behind it. The other misconception is that a significant result implies a large one: in a large dataset a tiny correlation is significant, so report size as well.

### Why it matters

Most findings in an EDA are bivariate: a driver of ratings, a category with a return problem, a discount that shifts basket size. Doing it carefully, with sizes, medians and a check for confounding, separates findings that can be defended from patterns that disappear next month.

## Syntax and key functions

Correlation and binned means:

```python
df[["a", "b"]].corr()
df["a"].corr(df["b"], method="spearman")
df.groupby(pd.cut(df["a"], bins), observed=True)["b"].mean()
```

`corr()` ignores rows where either value is missing. `pd.cut` turns numbers into labelled intervals.

Group comparison and cross-tab:

```python
df.groupby("cat").agg(n=("x", "size"), mean=("x", "mean"), median=("x", "median"))
pd.crosstab(df["cat"], df["flag"], normalize="index")
```

| Function | Returns |
|---|---|
| `df.corr(numeric_only=True)` | Matrix of Pearson correlations. |
| `s1.corr(s2, method="spearman")` | Rank correlation of two Series. |
| `pd.cut(s, bins)` | Interval label for each value. |
| `groupby(...).agg(name=(col, func))` | One row per group with named summaries. |
| `pd.crosstab(a, b, normalize="index")` | Row percentages of a two-way table. |
| `stats.chi2_contingency(table)` | Statistic, p-value, df, expected counts. |
| `df.corr().abs().unstack()` | All pairs as a long Series, for ranking. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Numeric against numeric

Shipping time and rating are both numbers. Pearson and Spearman agree closely, so the relationship is close to a steady decline. Order total against unit price shows a case where the two measures differ because of the long tail in the total. `corr()` uses only the rows where both values are present, and the number of those rows is printed.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

pd.set_option("display.width", 200)
pd.set_option("display.max_columns", 20)


def make_orders(messy=False):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 600
    category = rng.choice(["home", "beauty", "sports", "electronics", "toys"], size=n, p=[0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10])
    base = pd.Series({"home": 40, "beauty": 25, "sports": 55, "electronics": 180, "toys": 20})
    discount = rng.choice([0, 5, 10, 20], size=n, p=[0.6, 0.15, 0.15, 0.1])
    ship = np.clip(rng.poisson(3.5, n) + 1, 1, 12).astype(float)
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1001 + n),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 180, n), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 260, n),
        "channel": rng.choice(["organic", "paid", "email", "referral"], size=n, p=[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]),
        "device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop", "tablet"], size=n, p=[0.55, 0.35, 0.10]),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": np.clip(rng.poisson(0.8 + discount / 20, n) + 1, 1, 6),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * rng.lognormal(0, 0.25, n)).round(2),
        "discount_pct": discount,
        "shipping_days": ship,
    })
    df.loc[rng.choice(n, 4, replace=False), "unit_price"] *= 8
    df["order_total"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"] * (1 - df["discount_pct"] / 100)).round(2)
    df["rating"] = np.clip(np.round(4.3 - 0.2 * ship + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)), 1, 5)
    df["returned"] = rng.random(n) < 0.06 + 0.06 * (category == "electronics") + 0.03 * (ship > 6)
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.05, "shipping_days"] = np.nan
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.25, "rating"] = np.nan
    if messy:
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 25, replace=False), "channel"] = "Paid "
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 3, replace=False), "quantity"] = -1
        df = pd.concat([df, df.sample(6, random_state=1)], ignore_index=True)
    return df
orders = make_orders()
pair = orders[["shipping_days", "rating"]].dropna()
print("rows used:", len(pair))
print("pearson :", round(pair["shipping_days"].corr(pair["rating"]), 3))
print("spearman:", round(pair["shipping_days"].corr(pair["rating"], method="spearman"), 3))
print()
print("unit_price vs order_total")
print("pearson :", round(orders["unit_price"].corr(orders["order_total"]), 3))
print("spearman:", round(orders["unit_price"].corr(orders["order_total"], method="spearman"), 3))
```

```output
rows used: 417
pearson : -0.439
spearman: -0.428

unit_price vs order_total
pearson : 0.844
spearman: 0.8
```

A cross-tab of category and returned flag, as row percentages, shows the return rate within each category. ### Example 6: A confounding check

Discounts seem to raise quantity: the average quantity climbs from the no-discount group to the 20% group. A cautious analyst asks whether a third variable could explain it. Here quantity rises with the discount within every category (with small dips in a few cells), so category does not explain it, which strengthens the case, although a pattern in observed data still does not prove that the discount caused the larger baskets.

## Real-world applications

- **Driver analysis.** An analyst asked "what lowers ratings?" runs Examples 1 and 2: the correlation, then the banded means with their counts, to show how ratings step down as delivery slows.
- **Segment comparison.** Product and finance teams compare order value or margin across categories with a table like Example 3, using the median and the group size, so a single large order in a small group does not mislead.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- The pair of variable types picks the tool: correlation and binned means (numeric, numeric), group summaries (category, numeric), row-percentage cross-tabs (category, category).
- Pearson measures straight-line association and Spearman measures consistent rise or fall; compare them.
- Always show group size, and use the median beside the mean for skewed groups.
- A correlation matrix is a screen with many pairs; some pairs are related by construction, and some by chance.
- Look for a confounder before reading a pattern as a cause, and state how many rows each result uses.

### Common mistakes

- Reporting a correlation without the number of rows. Fix: drop missing values explicitly and print `len`.
- Reading a correlation of 0 as "no relationship", when a curve can hide behind it. Fix: check binned means.
- Comparing group averages without group sizes, so a group of four orders seems as reliable as a group of four hundred. Fix: include `size`.
- Comparing cross-tab counts instead of row percentages. A larger group always has larger counts. Fix: use `normalize="index"`.
- Treating the highest entries of a correlation matrix as discoveries when the columns are linked by a formula, such as a total and its parts. Fix: know how each column is made.
- Reading a pattern as causal. Fix: ask what third variable could drive both, and split the data on it.

### Revision notes

- Numeric pair: `a.corr(b)`, `method="spearman"`, `pd.cut` plus `groupby(...).mean()`.
- Category and number: `groupby(...).agg(n, mean, median)`.
- Two categories: `pd.crosstab(a, b, normalize="index")` and `chi2_contingency`.
- Matrix: `df[cols].corr()`; rank pairs with `stack()` on the upper triangle.
- Confounder check: repeat the comparison within levels of the third variable.
