## Objectives

- Build a retention curve from customer lifetimes and read it, including why recent cohorts must be left out of the later points.
- Compute a monthly churn rate as lost customers divided by customers at risk, and derive an expected lifetime from it.
- Explain survival thinking: why customers still active are "censored" and why averaging only the churned customers is biased.
- Compare churn between groups to look for drivers, with group sizes shown, and describe what the comparison can and cannot prove.

## Understand

### Retention and churn are two views of one fact

**Retention** is the share of a group of customers who are still active after a given time. **Churn** is the share who are lost in a period. Over one period, `churn rate = customers lost during the period / customers active at the start of the period`, and `retention rate = 1 - churn rate`. Each definition needs three decisions that must be written next to the number: who is in the group (all customers, one signup cohort, paying customers only), what counts as "active" (placed an order, logged in, has a live subscription), and the length of the period (a month, a quarter). The same business can report 3 percent monthly churn and 31 percent yearly churn without contradiction, because the periods differ. Two reports that disagree on retention usually disagree on one of these three decisions.

For a business with a subscription, "churned" is a recorded event (the cancellation). For a shop where customers buy at irregular intervals, there is no cancellation: a customer is simply silent. The analyst must then define churn by a **rule**, for example "no order for 180 days", and the rule should match the normal buying interval. A 30-day rule for a product bought twice a year labels almost everyone as churned.

### The retention curve

A **retention curve** plots, for each number of periods since the customer started (period 0, 1, 2, and so on), the share of the starting group still active. It always starts at 100 percent and never rises if "active" means "has not left". It answers questions such as "how many customers are still here after 3 months?" and shows where customers leave: a steep fall at the start means the first experience is the problem, while a curve that flattens means a loyal core remains. Retention is measured **by age** (months since signup), not by calendar month, so that customers who joined in different months can be compared on equal terms (the cohort idea of Day 111).

### Censoring: the data window ends

Data stops at some date. A customer who signed up two months before it can be observed for at most two months. Such a customer has not churned, but we also do not know whether they would have stayed to month six. Their observation is **censored**. The rule for the curve is to use, at each age, only customers who could have been observed for that long: for age 6, only those who signed up at least 6 months before the end of the data. If you divide the number still active at age 6 by all customers, including recent signups who were too young, retention is understated, and the recent months of the curve look worse than they are. Keeping the number of eligible customers beside every point is the safest habit.

### Survival thinking and the lifetime trap

**Survival thinking** treats customer life as a time until an event (churn) and uses the share who have survived to each age. Its main lesson is that "average lifetime" is hard to estimate honestly. Averaging the tenure of customers who have already churned ignores everyone still active, and the active ones are the long-lived ones, so the average comes out too short and gets worse the more recent the data window. When churn is roughly constant, a simple estimate is `expected lifetime = 1 / monthly churn rate` (a 5 percent monthly churn gives about 20 months). It is an approximation that fails when churn is high in the first months and low later, so check the shape of the curve first.

### Looking for drivers

A **driver** is a characteristic associated with churn: the plan, the acquisition channel, the country, the number of support complaints, or a late delivery. The method is to compute the churn or retention for each group, with the group size, and compare. Group differences are suggestions for investigation, not proof of cause: customers on a free plan may churn more because they are less committed, not because of the plan's features. Check size first (a difference between groups of 12 and 15 customers is noise), then check that the groups were observed for similar periods, and only then consider causes. Controlled experiments, where customers are assigned to groups at random, are the way to test a cause.

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Acquiring a customer costs money, so a business that loses customers at a high rate must keep paying to refill the base. Small improvements in retention compound: reducing monthly churn from 6 to 5 percent lengthens the expected lifetime from about 17 to 20 months. The misconception is that a low churn figure means a healthy business. A blended churn rate can hide that new customers leave quickly while the old base stays. Always split by age or cohort before concluding.

## Learn by example

### Example 1: A retention curve with eligibility

Customer lifetimes are simulated (seeded). For each age `t` the code keeps only the customers who signed up early enough to be observed at that age, then reports the share still active. The `eligible` column shows how the base shrinks.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 400
cust = pd.DataFrame({
    "signup": rng.integers(0, 10, n),
    "plan": rng.choice(["free", "paid"], n, p=[0.6, 0.4]),
})
hazard = np.where(cust["plan"] == "paid", 0.08, 0.18)
cust["life"] = np.ceil(np.log(1 - rng.random(n)) / np.log(1 - hazard)).astype(int)
LAST = 11
cust["last_active"] = cust["signup"] + cust["life"] - 1
cust["churned"] = cust["last_active"] < LAST
rows = []
for t in range(0, 8):
    elig = cust[cust["signup"] + t <= LAST]
    rows.append((t, len(elig), int((elig["life"] > t).sum()), round(float((elig["life"] > t).mean() * 100), 1)))
print(pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["age_month", "eligible", "active", "retention_pct"]).to_string(index=False))
```

```output
 age_month  eligible  active  retention_pct
         0       400     400          100.0
         1       400     335           83.8
         2       400     283           70.8
         3       350     211           60.3
         4       304     158           52.0
         5       269     118           43.9
         6       232      92           39.7
         7       187      71           38.0
```

Retention falls fastest in the first months and then slowly. The curve is read from the point where it starts to flatten.

### Example 2: Monthly churn rate

For each calendar month the code counts customers active in it and those whose last active month is that month. The churn rate is lost divided by active at risk, and the table shows the last months of the data.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 400
cust = pd.DataFrame({
    "signup": rng.integers(0, 10, n),
    "plan": rng.choice(["free", "paid"], n, p=[0.6, 0.4]),
})
hazard = np.where(cust["plan"] == "paid", 0.08, 0.18)
cust["life"] = np.ceil(np.log(1 - rng.random(n)) / np.log(1 - hazard)).astype(int)
LAST = 11
cust["last_active"] = cust["signup"] + cust["life"] - 1
cust["churned"] = cust["last_active"] < LAST
rows = []
for m in range(0, LAST):
    active = cust[(cust["signup"] <= m) & (cust["last_active"] >= m)]
    lost = int((active["last_active"] == m).sum())
    rows.append((m, len(active), lost, round(lost / len(active) * 100, 1)))
tab = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["month", "active", "lost", "churn_pct"])
print(tab.tail(5).to_string(index=False))
overall = tab["lost"].sum() / tab["active"].sum()
print("overall monthly churn:", round(float(overall) * 100, 1), "% | expected lifetime:", round(1 / float(overall), 1), "months")
```

```output
 month  active  lost  churn_pct
     6     178    33       18.5
     7     180    25       13.9
     8     201    23       11.4
     9     228    30       13.2
    10     198    27       13.6
overall monthly churn: 14.1 % | expected lifetime: 7.1 months
```

The overall churn rate is total lost divided by total customer-months at risk, which weights each month by its size. The expected lifetime is an approximation based on constant churn.

### Example 3: The lifetime trap

Two estimates of lifetime are compared with the lifetime used to generate the data. Averaging only the churned customers underestimates it because the long-lived customers are still active when the data ends.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 400
cust = pd.DataFrame({
    "signup": rng.integers(0, 10, n),
    "plan": rng.choice(["free", "paid"], n, p=[0.6, 0.4]),
})
hazard = np.where(cust["plan"] == "paid", 0.08, 0.18)
cust["life"] = np.ceil(np.log(1 - rng.random(n)) / np.log(1 - hazard)).astype(int)
LAST = 11
cust["last_active"] = cust["signup"] + cust["life"] - 1
cust["churned"] = cust["last_active"] < LAST
true_mean = float(cust["life"].mean())
churned_only = float(cust.loc[cust["churned"], "life"].mean())
churn_events = int(cust["churned"].sum())
exposure = int(np.minimum(cust["life"], LAST - cust["signup"] + 1).sum())
rate_based = exposure / churn_events
print("customers:", len(cust), "| churned within the window:", churn_events)
print("average life of churned customers only:", round(churned_only, 1))
print("1 / churn rate (events over exposure):", round(rate_based, 1))
print("true average life in the simulated data:", round(true_mean, 1))
```

```output
customers: 400 | churned within the window: 229
average life of churned customers only: 3.2
1 / churn rate (events over exposure): 7.9
true average life in the simulated data: 8.1
```

The churned-only average is far too low, while the events-over-exposure estimate is close to the truth. The difference is caused by censoring, and in a real dataset you never see the true value, which is why the method must be right.

### Example 4: Drivers: churn by plan

Churn is compared between the free and paid plans with the number of customers and the exposure (customer-months), because the comparison must be on rate per month, not on how many customers left.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 400
cust = pd.DataFrame({
    "signup": rng.integers(0, 10, n),
    "plan": rng.choice(["free", "paid"], n, p=[0.6, 0.4]),
})
hazard = np.where(cust["plan"] == "paid", 0.08, 0.18)
cust["life"] = np.ceil(np.log(1 - rng.random(n)) / np.log(1 - hazard)).astype(int)
LAST = 11
cust["last_active"] = cust["signup"] + cust["life"] - 1
cust["churned"] = cust["last_active"] < LAST
cust["exposure"] = np.minimum(cust["life"], LAST - cust["signup"] + 1)
g = cust.groupby("plan").agg(customers=("life", "size"), churned=("churned", "sum"), exposure=("exposure", "sum"))
g["monthly_churn_pct"] = g["churned"] / g["exposure"] * 100
print(g.round(1).to_string())
ratio = g.loc["free", "monthly_churn_pct"] / g.loc["paid", "monthly_churn_pct"]
print("free churns", round(float(ratio), 1), "times as fast as paid")
```

```output
      customers  churned  exposure  monthly_churn_pct
plan                                                 
free        230      154       860               17.9
paid        170       75       940                8.0
free churns 2.2 times as fast as paid
```

The free plan churns more than twice as fast. The group sizes (hundreds of customers) are large enough to take the difference seriously. Whether the plan causes the churn or merely marks less committed customers is a separate question that needs an experiment.

## Real-world applications

- **Subscription health**: a retention curve by signup cohort tells a product team whether the onboarding changes they made improved month-3 retention, as in Example 1.
- **Marketing budget**: the expected lifetime from Example 2, together with margin per month, caps how much the business can spend to acquire one customer.
- **Early warning**: comparing churn between groups, as in Example 4, directs customer success staff to the plan or channel where customers leave fastest.
- **Retail win-back**: a rule such as "no order in 180 days" turns irregular purchasing into a churn flag and a mailing list.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Churn rate is lost over at-risk customers in a period, retention is its complement, and each needs the group, the definition of active and the period written beside it.
- Measure retention by age, and at each age use only customers who could have been observed that long.
- Customers still active at the end of the data are censored; ignoring them biases lifetime downward.
- Compare groups by rate per customer-period, with sizes, and treat differences as leads, not causes.

### Common mistakes

- Dividing the number still active at age 6 by all customers. Recent signups were too young; the curve is understated. Use only eligible customers.
- Averaging the tenure of churned customers only. The estimate is too short; use events over exposure instead.
- Comparing counts of churned customers between groups of different size. Compare rates.
- Mixing a 30-day inactivity rule with a product bought twice a year. Pick the rule from the normal buying interval.
- Reading a blended churn rate as health. Split by age and cohort.

### Revision notes

- `churn = lost / at_risk`; `retention = 1 - churn`; `lifetime ~ 1 / churn`.
- Eligible at age t: signup month + t <= last month of the data.
- Rate per exposure: churn events / customer-periods.
- Always show group sizes beside rates.
