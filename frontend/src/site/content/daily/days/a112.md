## Objectives

- Write a finding as a claim that is backed by a specific number, a named group and a comparison.
- Rate the strength of the evidence behind a finding and word the claim to match it.
- Attach a confidence interval and the base (denominator) to a number so that readers see its uncertainty.
- Structure an EDA report: purpose, data, key findings, caveats, decisions and next steps.
- Review a draft finding with a checklist to find missing evidence, missing context or overstated wording.

## Understand

### Findings are claims with evidence

After several days of exploring the orders, you have dozens of numbers. A reader wants only a handful of **findings**: statements about the data that matter for a decision. A finding has four parts. The **claim** says what is true ("customers in the top spending quarter produce most of our revenue"). The **evidence** is the number or comparison that supports it ("59% of revenue from 59 of 236 customers"). The **base** says what the number is computed on (all 236 customers who ordered between January and June). The **caveat** states what limits the claim (the data covers six months and the first month includes customers who started earlier). A claim with no number is an opinion, and a number with no base cannot be judged.

Order matters. Put the finding first and the evidence after it. Readers decide in the first sentence whether to keep reading, so the headline should carry the message, not the method. "Return rates differ by category" is a topic; "Electronics are returned about twice as often as other categories (12.6% against 6.6%)" is a finding.

### Matching the wording to the strength of the evidence

Not all findings deserve the same confidence. A **descriptive** finding states a fact about the data you have, such as a share of revenue, and is reliable if the data is. A **comparative** finding says two groups differ, and it depends on sample size and noise, which you assess with the tools of the previous days: a test such as the chi-square test (Day 104), an interval, and the group sizes. A **causal** claim, that one thing makes another happen, cannot come from observational EDA (Days 101 and 108). A simple vocabulary keeps wording honest: "strong evidence" for a small p-value with large groups and a clear effect, "suggestive" for a p-value near the threshold or small groups, "no clear difference" when the interval comfortably includes zero, and "associated with" instead of "causes". Words like "proves", "because" and "drives" need an experiment, as in Day 105.

A p-value just above 0.05 is not a failure to be hidden. It is a result: say that the data suggest a difference but cannot rule out chance, report the interval and say what more data would settle it.

### Uncertainty in the number itself

Every sample number has uncertainty. A return rate of 12.6% from 103 orders could plausibly be anything from about 6% to 19%. For a proportion p measured on n rows, an approximate 95% interval is p plus or minus 1.96 times the square root of p(1 - p) / n (from Day 89). Reporting "12.6% (95% interval 6% to 19%, n = 103)" is more honest than "12.6%", and shows the reader which numbers can be trusted for decisions. A very wide interval is itself a finding: the data cannot settle the question.

### Structure of an EDA report

An EDA report is read by people who did not do the work, so it follows the reader's needs rather than the order in which you worked. A reliable layout is:

1. **Purpose and questions**: what decision the exploration supports.
2. **Data**: source, period, number of rows, definitions of key metrics, and what was cleaned (from the assumptions log of Day 106).
3. **Key findings**: three to five, each as claim, evidence, base and a short chart or table.
4. **Caveats and data quality**: what limits the findings.
5. **Recommended actions and next steps**: what to do, and what to measure or test next.

Keep the raw exploration (every table you looked at) in an appendix or in the notebook, not in the summary. A reader who wants more can follow the link.

### Caveats that earn their place

A caveat should say what could change the conclusion, not simply that "more data would help". Useful caveats name the issue and its likely effect: "27.5% of orders have no rating, and the missing pattern is unknown, so the average rating describes only rated orders"; "the first week is partial and has been excluded"; "6 duplicate rows were removed, which changes revenue by less than 1%". Too many caveats bury the message, so order them by importance and keep to the ones that matter.

### Showing numbers well

Round to what is meaningful: percentages to one decimal or whole numbers, money to the unit. State units (dollars, days, percentage points) and the comparison base ("against 6.6% for other categories"). Distinguish percentage points from percent: a rise from 6% to 9% is 3 points, or 50% in relative terms. Use tables for exact values and charts for patterns, a topic that the visualisation days cover. In text, prefer a short comparison to a list of figures.

### Common misconception

"A good report includes everything I found." A report that includes everything has no message. Choose the findings that change a decision, put the rest in an appendix, and be willing to leave out an interesting but irrelevant pattern.

### Why it matters

Analysis only has value when it is understood and trusted. Findings that state their evidence, base and limits get acted on appropriately; findings that overclaim lose credibility the first time a stakeholder checks a number.

## Syntax and key functions

A finding as a record:

```python
finding = {
    "claim": "Electronics are returned about twice as often as other categories",
    "evidence": "12.6% (n = 103) against 6.6% (n = 497)",
    "base": "600 orders, January to June",
    "strength": "suggestive",
    "caveat": "p = 0.061; groups differ in price and mix",
    "action": "review electronics packaging and descriptions",
}
```

Interval for a proportion:

```python
half = 1.96 * np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)
low, high = p - half, p + half
```

| Item or function | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `f"{value:.1%}"` | Formats a fraction as a percentage with one decimal. |
| `f"{value:,.0f}"` | Formats a number with thousands separators. |
| `stats.chi2_contingency(table)` | p-value for a difference in rates (Day 104). |
| `np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)` | Standard error of a proportion. |
| `"\n".join(lines)` | Assembles report lines into text. |
| `all(key in finding for key in keys)` | Checks that a finding has every required part. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Collect the evidence in one place

Writing a report starts from numbers computed in code, not copied by hand. The helper `evidence` returns the key numbers from four explorations: revenue concentration, shipping time against rating, the electronics return gap and the missing ratings. Every figure used later comes from this dictionary, so the text cannot drift from the data.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

pd.set_option("display.width", 200)
pd.set_option("display.max_columns", 20)


def make_orders(messy=False):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 600
    category = rng.choice(["home", "beauty", "sports", "electronics", "toys"], size=n, p=[0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10])
    base = pd.Series({"home": 40, "beauty": 25, "sports": 55, "electronics": 180, "toys": 20})
    discount = rng.choice([0, 5, 10, 20], size=n, p=[0.6, 0.15, 0.15, 0.1])
    ship = np.clip(rng.poisson(3.5, n) + 1, 1, 12).astype(float)
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1001 + n),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 180, n), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 260, n),
        "channel": rng.choice(["organic", "paid", "email", "referral"], size=n, p=[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]),
        "device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop", "tablet"], size=n, p=[0.55, 0.35, 0.10]),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": np.clip(rng.poisson(0.8 + discount / 20, n) + 1, 1, 6),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * rng.lognormal(0, 0.25, n)).round(2),
        "discount_pct": discount,
        "shipping_days": ship,
    })
    df.loc[rng.choice(n, 4, replace=False), "unit_price"] *= 8
    df["order_total"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"] * (1 - df["discount_pct"] / 100)).round(2)
    df["rating"] = np.clip(np.round(4.3 - 0.2 * ship + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)), 1, 5)
    df["returned"] = rng.random(n) < 0.06 + 0.06 * (category == "electronics") + 0.03 * (ship > 6)
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.05, "shipping_days"] = np.nan
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.25, "rating"] = np.nan
    if messy:
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 25, replace=False), "channel"] = "Paid "
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 3, replace=False), "quantity"] = -1
        df = pd.concat([df, df.sample(6, random_state=1)], ignore_index=True)
    return df


def evidence(orders):
    cust = orders.groupby("customer_id")["order_total"].sum().sort_values(ascending=False)
    k = int(round(len(cust) * 0.25))
    pair = orders[["shipping_days", "rating"]].dropna()
    elec = orders["category"] == "electronics"
    table = pd.crosstab(elec, orders["returned"])
    chi2, p_ret, dof, exp = stats.chi2_contingency(table)
    return {
        "revenue_top_share": cust.head(k).sum() / cust.sum(),
        "customers": len(cust),
        "corr": pair["shipping_days"].corr(pair["rating"]),
        "corr_n": len(pair),
        "elec_rate": orders.loc[elec, "returned"].mean(),
        "other_rate": orders.loc[~elec, "returned"].mean(),
        "elec_n": int(elec.sum()),
        "p_return": p_ret,
        "missing_rating": orders["rating"].isna().mean(),
    }
orders = make_orders()
ev = evidence(orders)
for key, value in ev.items():
    print(f"{key}: {round(float(value), 4)}")
```

```output
revenue_top_share: 0.5935
customers: 236.0
corr: -0.4394
corr_n: 417.0
elec_rate: 0.1262
other_rate: 0.0664
elec_n: 103.0
p_return: 0.061
missing_rating: 0.275
```

### Example 2: Rating the strength of each finding

A small rule turns evidence into a strength label. A comparison with p below 0.01 and at least 100 rows in the smaller group is `strong`, p below 0.10 is `suggestive`, and anything else is `no clear difference`. The correlation is tested with `pearsonr` from `scipy.stats`. A descriptive finding is labelled `descriptive`. The rule is deliberately simple and stated, so a reader can challenge it.

### Example 6: A checklist for a draft finding

Before sending, each finding is checked against the required parts. The function returns the list of missing parts and flags forbidden causal words. Run on two drafts, it shows how a vague finding fails while a complete one passes.

```python run
REQUIRED = ["claim", "number", "base", "comparison", "caveat"]
CAUSAL = ["proves", "because", "causes", "drives"]

def review(finding):
    missing = [k for k in REQUIRED if not finding.get(k)]
    words = [w for w in CAUSAL if w in finding.get("claim", "").lower()]
    return missing, words

drafts = {
    "vague": {"claim": "Discounts drives higher sales", "number": "", "base": "", "comparison": "", "caveat": ""},
    "complete": {"claim": "Orders with a 20% discount contain more items",
                 "number": "2.67 against 1.89 items", "base": "600 orders",
                 "comparison": "no-discount orders", "caveat": "association only; no experiment"},
}
for name, finding in drafts.items():
    missing, words = review(finding)
    print(name, "| missing:", missing, "| causal words:", words)
```

```output
vague | missing: ['number', 'base', 'comparison', 'caveat'] | causal words: ['drives']
complete | missing: [] | causal words: []
```

## Real-world applications

- **Stakeholder updates.** An analyst sends a one-page summary built like Example 5, with findings first, evidence and base beside each, and caveats in order of importance, so that a manager can decide without opening the notebook.
- **Peer review.** A checklist like Example 6 is used before sharing work: a colleague can see at once whether a number lacks a base, whether a causal word has slipped in and whether the caveat is specific.
- **Regenerating reports.** Building text from computed values (Examples 3 and 5) lets a weekly report refresh from new data without retyping numbers, which removes a common source of errors.
- **Decision memos.** Reporting the interval with the point estimate (Example 4) tells the decision-maker whether to act now or collect more data first.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- A finding has a claim, evidence, base and caveat; lead with the claim, then the number.
- Match wording to strength: descriptive, strong, suggestive, no clear difference; avoid causal words without an experiment.
- Show uncertainty: an interval, the group size and the p-value beside the point estimate.
- Structure a report as purpose, data, key findings, caveats, next steps, with detail in an appendix.
- Caveats must name what could change the conclusion and its likely effect.

### Common mistakes

- Reporting a percentage without its base. "12.6% returned" could mean 13 of 103 or 1,260 of 10,000. Fix: add n.
- Writing "drives", "causes" or "because" about an association. Fix: say "is associated with" and suggest an experiment.
- Hiding a p-value of 0.06 or treating it as proof of no difference. Fix: call it suggestive and show the interval.
- Confusing percentage points and percent. A move from 6% to 9% is 3 points, not 3%. Fix: say which one, and give both if useful.
- Pasting every table into the summary. Fix: three to five findings, the rest in an appendix.
- Copying numbers by hand into the text and later changing the data. Fix: generate the text from the computed values.

### Revision notes

- Finding = claim + number + base + comparison + caveat.
- Strength vocabulary: descriptive, strong, suggestive, no clear difference.
- Proportion interval: `p +- 1.96 * sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)`.
- Report layout: purpose, data, findings, caveats, next steps.
- Format with `:.1%` and state the unit.
