## Understand

### Why inventory is a trade-off

Inventory is money sitting on shelves. Too much of it ties up cash, takes up space, and risks goods going out of date. Too little means **stock-outs**, which are moments when a customer wants a product and it is not available, and the sale is lost or delayed. Operations analytics measures both sides of this trade-off and helps choose how much stock to hold. The data needed is modest: units in stock, units sold over time, the time it takes to get more stock, and in some cases the cost of the goods.

### Turnover, cover and days of inventory

**Stock turnover** says how many times the inventory is sold and replaced in a period: `turnover = cost of goods sold / average inventory value`. When cost data is missing, a units version is used: `units sold / average units in stock`. A turnover of 6 a year means the stock is sold and replaced every two months. **Days of inventory** converts it to days: `365 / turnover`. A closely related practical measure is **stock cover**: `units in stock / average daily demand`, the number of days the current stock would last at the usual selling rate. Low cover warns of a stock-out, very high cover warns of overstock. Both need a sensible window for the demand figure: the last 12 months can hide a recent trend, the last week is noisy, and a seasonal product needs a comparison with the same period last year.

A high turnover is not automatically good. It can mean efficient operations, or it can mean the shelves are too bare and the business keeps running out. Turnover must be read together with the stock-out rate.

### Lead time and reorder point

**Lead time** is the number of days between placing a replenishment order and receiving the goods. During that time the business keeps selling from the stock it has. The **reorder point** is the stock level at which a new order is placed, and its minimum value is the expected demand during the lead time: `reorder point = average daily demand * lead time`. If demand and lead time were perfectly constant that would be enough. Real demand varies from day to day and lead time varies from order to order, and the extra stock held to cover that variation is the **safety stock**. A standard approximation for variable daily demand with constant lead time is `safety stock = z * sigma_daily * sqrt(L)`, where `sigma_daily` is the standard deviation of daily demand, `L` is the lead time in days and `z` sets the protection level: z = 1.28 for about 90 percent, 1.64 for about 95 percent and 2.33 for about 99 percent protection against stock-out in a cycle. The square root appears because variation over several days partly cancels. The formula assumes that daily demands are independent and roughly normal; it works less well for slow-moving items with many zero days, and it ignores variation in lead time. Variable lead times need a larger buffer, and the average lead time is the wrong number to use when late deliveries cause the stock-outs: look at the high percentiles of lead time too.

### Service levels

A **service level** measures how well the business serves demand, and two definitions are in common use:

- **Cycle service level** is the share of replenishment cycles in which no stock-out occurs. It is what the z-value above targets.
- **Fill rate** is the share of demanded units that were supplied from stock: `units sold / units demanded`. A business can have a cycle service level of 90 percent and a fill rate of 99 percent, because a stock-out may last briefly and miss only a few units.

State which one you are quoting. A related simple measure, the **stock-out rate**, is the share of days (or of item-days) on which a product had no stock.

### Reading the numbers

Some warnings. Demand observed in the data is **sales**, and sales are capped by stock: on a day with a stock-out, sales were zero even though customers wanted the product. Average sales therefore understate demand for items that run out often. Averages hide products: a warehouse with high average cover can still have a handful of products about to run out, so analyse by product and rank by cover. And the cost of holding stock (capital, storage, spoilage) must be compared with the cost of a lost sale (margin lost, possibly a lost customer) in money, so that the target service level is a business choice rather than a statistical default.

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Inventory is often the largest asset on a trading company's balance sheet, and stock-outs reduce revenue directly. The misconception is that more stock is always safer. Holding stock has a cost, and the benefit of each extra unit of safety stock shrinks as the service level approaches 100 percent: moving from 95 to 99 percent protection needs far more extra stock than moving from 90 to 95. The analyst's job is to show this curve so that the manager can choose a point on it.

## Syntax and key functions

```sql-syntax
SELECT p.product_name, p.units_in_stock,
       SUM(od.quantity) / 365.0                       AS daily_demand,
       p.units_in_stock / (SUM(od.quantity) / 365.0)  AS cover_days
FROM products p JOIN order_details od USING (product_id)
GROUP BY p.product_id;
```

```python

```

| Function or formula | Returns |
|---|---|
| `COGS / average inventory` | Stock turnover. |
| `365 / turnover` | Days of inventory. |
| `units_in_stock / daily_demand` | Stock cover in days. |
| `NormalDist().inv_cdf(p)` | The z value for protection level p. |
| `z * sigma * sqrt(L)` | Safety stock. |
| `units_sold / units_demanded` | Fill rate. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Check the data before computing stock position

A reorder-level report is the usual first query, but a rule only works if its input is filled in. The query counts the products, the products whose `reorder_level` is above zero, the products at or below their level and the products with no stock, then lists the lowest stock holdings.

```sql run
SELECT COUNT(*) AS products,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE reorder_level > 0) AS with_reorder_level,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE units_in_stock <= reorder_level) AS at_or_below,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE units_in_stock = 0) AS out_of_stock,
       MIN(units_in_stock) AS lowest_stock,
       MAX(units_on_order) AS max_on_order
FROM products;
```

```output
products | with_reorder_level | at_or_below | out_of_stock | lowest_stock | max_on_order
77 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 21 | 0
```

Every product has a reorder level of zero, so "at or below the reorder level" can never be true and a report built on it would show nothing at risk. This is a data problem, not good news: the field is not maintained in this dataset (and no product has stock on order). Stock cover, which uses demand, does not depend on that field, so Example 2 computes it instead. In real work this is why you profile a column before building a rule on it.

### Example 2: Stock cover from 1998 demand

Units sold in 1998 are divided by 365 to give average daily demand, and the stock cover is units in stock over daily demand. Products with no sales in 1998 are not in the list because there is no demand to divide by. The lowest cover is the urgent end.

```sql run
SELECT p.product_name,
       p.units_in_stock,
       SUM(od.quantity) AS units_1998,
       ROUND((p.units_in_stock / (SUM(od.quantity) / 365.0))::numeric, 0) AS cover_days
FROM products p
JOIN order_details od ON od.product_id = p.product_id
JOIN orders o ON o.order_id = od.order_id
WHERE o.order_date >= DATE '1998-01-01' AND o.order_date < DATE '1999-01-01'
GROUP BY p.product_id, p.product_name, p.units_in_stock
ORDER BY cover_days, p.product_name
LIMIT 8;
```

```output
product_name | units_in_stock | units_1998 | cover_days
Chang | 22 | 40 | 201
Thüringer Rostbratwurst | 49 | 79 | 226
Aniseed Syrup | 23 | 35 | 240
Queso Cabrales | 31 | 40 | 283
Chef Anton's Cajun Seasoning | 24 | 30 | 292
Uncle Bob's Organic Dried Pears | 27 | 33 | 299
Gumbär Gummibärchen | 46 | 45 | 373
Gorgonzola Telino | 51 | 49 | 380
```

The lowest cover is about 200 days, which is far from a stock-out for any realistic lead time, so nothing is urgent in this synthetic data. The method is what matters: rank by cover, then compare the lowest values with the lead time of each product.

### Example 3: Safety stock and reorder point

Daily demand for one product over 180 days is generated (seeded). The mean and the standard deviation of daily demand, a lead time of 7 days and a 95 percent protection level give the safety stock and reorder point. The last lines show how the safety stock grows with the service target.

```python run
import numpy as np
from statistics import NormalDist

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
demand = np.clip(np.round(rng.normal(20, 6, 180)), 0, None)
mean, sd = float(demand.mean()), float(demand.std(ddof=1))
lead = 7
print("mean daily demand:", round(mean, 1), "| std:", round(sd, 1))
for p in (0.90, 0.95, 0.99):
    z = NormalDist().inv_cdf(p)
    safety = z * sd * lead ** 0.5
    print(f"protection {p:.0%}: z={z:.2f} safety stock={safety:.0f} reorder point={mean * lead + safety:.0f}")
```

```output
mean daily demand: 18.9 | std: 5.3
protection 90%: z=1.28 safety stock=18 reorder point=150
protection 95%: z=1.64 safety stock=23 reorder point=155
protection 99%: z=2.33 safety stock=32 reorder point=165
```

Going from 90 to 99 percent protection roughly doubles the safety stock. Each step is cheap at the start and expensive near 100 percent.

### Example 4: Simulating stock-outs

A simple policy is simulated over the 180 days: when the stock on hand plus the stock on order falls to the reorder point, an order of 200 units is placed and it arrives 7 days later. Unmet demand is lost. The same demand is used for two reorder points, so that the only difference is the safety stock.

```python run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
demand = np.clip(np.round(rng.normal(20, 6, 180)), 0, None)
lead, qty = 7, 200

def simulate(rop):
    stock, pipeline, sold, lost, out_days = 250, [], 0, 0, 0
    for day, d in enumerate(demand):
        stock += sum(q for t, q in pipeline if t == day)
        pipeline = [(t, q) for t, q in pipeline if t != day]
        on_order = sum(q for _, q in pipeline)
        if stock + on_order <= rop:
            pipeline.append((day + lead, qty))
        s = min(stock, d)
        stock -= s
        sold += s
        lost += d - s
        out_days += int(s < d)
    return int(sold), int(lost), out_days, round(sold / (sold + lost) * 100, 1)

for label, rop in (("no safety stock", 20 * 7), ("with safety stock", 20 * 7 + 30)):
    sold, lost, out_days, fill = simulate(rop)
    print(f"{label}: sold {sold}, lost {lost}, stock-out days {out_days}, fill rate {fill}%")
```

```output
no safety stock: sold 3246, lost 162, stock-out days 14, fill rate 95.2%
with safety stock: sold 3403, lost 5, stock-out days 1, fill rate 99.9%
```

The extra safety stock reduces lost sales and stock-out days. The fill rate shows that even the weaker policy serves most of the demand, which is the difference between fill rate and cycle service level described above.

## Real-world applications

- **Replenishment planning**: a buyer's weekly list is the products with the lowest cover relative to their lead time, as in Example 2, which still works when the reorder-level field is not maintained.
- **Working capital**: turnover and days of inventory tell finance how much cash is locked in stock and what releasing a day of stock is worth.
- **Service targets**: the curve in Example 3 lets operations and finance choose a service level by comparing the cost of the extra stock with the margin of the sales it protects.
- **Supplier management**: lead-time percentiles per supplier show who causes stock-outs and who deserves a larger buffer or a replacement.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Turnover is COGS over average inventory; cover is stock over daily demand; read them with the stock-out rate.
- Reorder point is demand during lead time plus safety stock.
- Safety stock grows with the service target and with the square root of the lead time; variable lead times need more.
- Cycle service level and fill rate are different measures; say which you use.
- Sales are capped by stock, so observed demand understates real demand for items that run out.

### Common mistakes

- Dividing by zero demand when computing cover. Products with no sales return an error or a missing value; filter or use `NULLIF`.
- Using average lead time only. A supplier that is usually on time and sometimes two weeks late causes stock-outs; check the high percentile.
- Building a rule on a column that is empty or constant (here, a reorder level of zero everywhere). Profile the column first.
- Taking the average over all products as the stock position. Rank by product, because a few at risk matter more than the average.
- Using last week's sales as demand. The estimate is noisy; use a window long enough to smooth it and compare with the same season.
- Quoting a service level without saying whether it is cycle service level or fill rate.

### Revision notes

- Cover = units in stock / average daily demand.
- Reorder point = daily demand x lead time + safety stock.
- Safety stock = z x sigma x sqrt(lead time); z = 1.28, 1.64, 2.33 for 90, 95, 99 percent.
- Fill rate = units supplied / units demanded.
