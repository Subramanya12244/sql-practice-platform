## Objectives

- Choose a sequential, diverging or categorical palette to match the type of data, and keep the number of colours small.
- Test a palette for readers with colour-vision differences and for greyscale printing by computing luminance, and apply a redundant cue (marker, line style or label).
- Highlight one series with a focus colour and grey context, and annotate the point that carries the message.
- Write chart titles that state the finding, and generate one from the data.

## Understand

### Colour carries meaning, so spend it carefully

On Day 114 colour hue was the weakest channel for comparing values, but it is excellent for one job: telling groups apart at a glance, and for a second job: marking what matters. Every use of colour in a chart should answer the question "what does this colour mean?". If the answer is "nothing, it makes the chart look nice", leave it grey. The more colours a chart uses, the less each one stands out, and a legend of eight hues sends the reader's eye back and forth to match swatches to lines.

### Three kinds of palette

A **categorical** (qualitative) palette is a set of distinct hues for unordered groups, such as product categories. Use at most five or six, because people cannot reliably tell more apart, and keep the same colour for the same category on every chart of a report. A **sequential** palette runs from light to dark in one hue (or along a perceptually even scale such as Viridis) for ordered values that run from low to high, such as counts or revenue. A **diverging** palette has two hues that meet at a light or neutral middle, for values with a meaningful centre such as correlations around zero or change around zero. Mixing the kinds is the most common error: a rainbow for ordered values, or a diverging scale for values that have no middle, invents boundaries that are not in the data. Rainbow scales also have uneven brightness, so some parts of the range look more important than others.

### Colour-vision differences and greyscale

A noticeable minority of readers see some colours alike; red-green confusion is the most common form and is often cited as affecting around one in twelve men. Designing for them does not mean giving up colour; it means never relying on colour alone. Two practical tests work without special tools. First, choose a palette designed for it (matplotlib's and seaborn's `colorblind` palette, or the eight-colour Okabe-Ito set used in the examples). Second, check **luminance**, the perceived brightness of a colour: two colours with similar luminance look the same in greyscale and are hard to separate for many colour-vision types, whatever their hue. In the examples, you will compute luminance from the red, green and blue values using the standard formula for sRGB colours, and the **contrast ratio** between two colours, `(L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05)` with the lighter colour first. Accessibility guidelines for web text ask for a ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 for normal-size text; black on white is 21 to 1. A redundant cue is the second line of defence: add different markers, line styles or direct labels so each series can be identified without colour. Direct labels (the name written at the end of the line) are better than a legend for everyone.

### Focus colour and annotation

The strongest use of colour in an analysis chart is the **focus colour technique**: draw everything in grey, and the one series or bar that matters in a single strong colour. The reader's eye goes straight to it before reading anything. Add an **annotation** (Day 116) on the same element, a short text with an arrow, saying what the reader should see ("Electronics: 46% of revenue"). Annotating one or two elements beats labelling all of them. Keep text legible: sufficient size, dark text on light backgrounds, and no text over busy parts of the chart.

### Titles that say something

A **descriptive title** says what the chart is ("Revenue by category, 2025"). An **insight title** says what the chart shows ("Electronics earns almost half of revenue"). Insight titles do the reader's first step for them and make the chart quotable in a slide, but they raise the stakes: the claim must be true and tied to the data. The safest way is to generate or check the title from the numbers: compute the share, then write the sentence. Keep the descriptive information (what is measured, units, period) in the axis labels and a subtitle. Never write a title that claims a cause the data cannot show; "revenue rose after the redesign" is a claim about order in time, not cause.

### Misconceptions and why it matters

One belief is that a colour-blind-safe palette makes a chart accessible; it helps, but small text, low contrast and colour-only legends still exclude readers. Another is that a title should be neutral; a chart without a message makes the reader do the analysis. For analysts, accessibility and clear messages are what make a chart usable by the whole audience, including the stakeholder who prints it in greyscale and the reader on a phone.

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Measure brightness and contrast

The WCAG relative luminance converts the red, green and blue of a colour to one brightness number. Black and white are the two extremes, 0 and 1, and the ratio between them is 21. The same functions then rank an eight-colour palette by brightness, showing which pairs would be confused in greyscale. This is plain Python and runs in the browser.

```python run
def luminance(hex_colour):
    """WCAG relative luminance of a colour like '#D55E00' (0 = black, 1 = white)."""
    h = hex_colour.lstrip("#")
    channels = []
    for i in (0, 2, 4):
        c = int(h[i:i + 2], 16) / 255
        channels.append(c / 12.92 if c <= 0.03928 else ((c + 0.055) / 1.055) ** 2.4)
    return 0.2126 * channels[0] + 0.7152 * channels[1] + 0.0722 * channels[2]


def contrast(a, b):
    la, lb = sorted([luminance(a), luminance(b)], reverse=True)
    return (la + 0.05) / (lb + 0.05)

print("black on white:", round(contrast("#000000", "#FFFFFF"), 1))
print("grey #999999 on white:", round(contrast("#999999", "#FFFFFF"), 2))
palette = {"orange": "#E69F00", "sky": "#56B4E9", "green": "#009E73", "blue": "#0072B2", "red": "#D55E00", "pink": "#CC79A7"}
for name, hex_colour in sorted(palette.items(), key=lambda kv: luminance(kv[1])):
    print(f"{name:7} {hex_colour} luminance {luminance(hex_colour):.2f}")
```

```output
black on white: 21.0
grey #999999 on white: 2.85
blue    #0072B2 luminance 0.15
red     #D55E00 luminance 0.22
green   #009E73 luminance 0.26
pink    #CC79A7 luminance 0.29
sky     #56B4E9 luminance 0.41
orange  #E69F00 luminance 0.42
```

### Example 2: Redundant cues on a line chart

A colour-blind-safe palette is set with `set_prop_cycle`, and each line also gets its own marker and line style, so the lines can be told apart without colour. The checks read the colours, markers and line styles back from the chart and confirm that no two lines share a marker.

```python local
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import to_hex
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_orders():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 240
    category = rng.choice(["Home", "Electronics", "Beauty", "Sports"], size=n, p=[0.35, 0.2, 0.25, 0.2])
    base = pd.Series({"Home": 45, "Electronics": 160, "Beauty": 28, "Sports": 60})
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * (0.8 + 0.4 * rng.random(n))).round(2),
        "shipping_days": rng.integers(1, 9, n),
    })
    df["revenue"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"]).round(2)
    return df.sort_values("order_date").reset_index(drop=True)

orders = make_orders()
table = orders.pivot_table(index=orders["order_date"].dt.month, columns="category", values="revenue", aggfunc="sum", fill_value=0)
OKABE = ["#E69F00", "#56B4E9", "#009E73", "#D55E00"]
markers = ["o", "s", "^", "D"]
styles = ["-", "--", "-.", ":"]

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.set_prop_cycle(color=OKABE)
for name, marker, style in zip(table.columns, markers, styles):
    ax.plot(table.index, table[name], marker=marker, linestyle=style, label=name)
ax.legend()

print("colours:", [to_hex(line.get_color()) for line in ax.lines])
print("markers:", [line.get_marker() for line in ax.lines])
print("styles:", [line.get_linestyle() for line in ax.lines])
print("distinct markers:", len({line.get_marker() for line in ax.lines}) == len(ax.lines))
```

```output
colours: ['#e69f00', '#56b4e9', '#009e73', '#d55e00']
markers: ['o', 's', '^', 'D']
styles: ['-', '--', '-.', ':']
distinct markers: True
```

### Example 4: Focus colour with one annotation

All bars are grey except Electronics, which carries the message, and the annotation states the share. The share is computed from the data first, so the annotation text cannot drift from the numbers.

```python local
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import to_hex
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_orders():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 240
    category = rng.choice(["Home", "Electronics", "Beauty", "Sports"], size=n, p=[0.35, 0.2, 0.25, 0.2])
    base = pd.Series({"Home": 45, "Electronics": 160, "Beauty": 28, "Sports": 60})
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * (0.8 + 0.4 * rng.random(n))).round(2),
        "shipping_days": rng.integers(1, 9, n),
    })
    df["revenue"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"]).round(2)
    return df.sort_values("order_date").reset_index(drop=True)

orders = make_orders()
totals = orders.groupby("category")["revenue"].sum().sort_values(ascending=False)
share = totals / totals.sum() * 100
top = totals.index[0]
colours = ["#D55E00" if name == top else "#999999" for name in totals.index]

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
bars = ax.bar(totals.index, totals.values, color=colours)
ax.annotate(f"{top}: {share[top]:.0f}% of revenue", xy=(0, totals.iloc[0]), xytext=(1, totals.iloc[0] * 0.95),
            arrowprops={"arrowstyle": "->"})
ax.set_title(f"{top} earns almost half of revenue")
ax.set_ylim(bottom=0)

print("bar colours:", [to_hex(b.get_facecolor()) for b in bars])
print("annotation:", [t.get_text() for t in ax.texts])
print("title:", ax.get_title())
```

```output
bar colours: ['#d55e00', '#999999', '#999999', '#999999']
annotation: ['Electronics: 46% of revenue']
title: Electronics earns almost half of revenue
```

### Example 5: A title generated from the data

The title function reads the data and writes the sentence, choosing the wording from the size of the share. If the data changes, the title changes with it, which stops a stale claim reaching a slide. It runs in the browser.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_orders():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 240
    category = rng.choice(["Home", "Electronics", "Beauty", "Sports"], size=n, p=[0.35, 0.2, 0.25, 0.2])
    base = pd.Series({"Home": 45, "Electronics": 160, "Beauty": 28, "Sports": 60})
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * (0.8 + 0.4 * rng.random(n))).round(2),
        "shipping_days": rng.integers(1, 9, n),
    })
    df["revenue"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"]).round(2)
    return df.sort_values("order_date").reset_index(drop=True)


def insight_title(totals):
    top = totals.idxmax()
    share = totals.max() / totals.sum() * 100
    if share >= 45:
        size = "almost half" if share < 50 else "half or more"
    elif share >= 30:
        size = "about a third"
    else:
        size = "a modest part"
    return f"{top} earns {size} of revenue ({share:.0f}%)"


orders = make_orders()
print(insight_title(orders.groupby("category")["revenue"].sum()))
print(insight_title(orders.groupby("category")["quantity"].sum()))
```

```output
Electronics earns almost half of revenue (46%)
Home earns about a third of revenue (35%)
```

## Real-world applications

- **Brand-safe report templates.** A fixed colour-blind-safe list (Example 2) is set once in a reporting script so every chart uses the same colour for the same category.
- **Print and projector checks.** Computing luminance (Example 1) finds palettes that collapse to the same grey when a report is printed in black and white.
- **Executive slides.** The focus colour and one annotation (Example 4) make a slide read in seconds without a legend.
- **Automated narrative.** A title function (Example 5) keeps the headline of a weekly report in step with the numbers behind it.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Match the palette to the data: categorical for groups, sequential for ordered values, diverging around a meaningful middle.
- Do not rely on colour alone: use markers, line styles, direct labels and enough luminance difference.
- Focus colour plus one annotation guides the eye; grey for context.
- Insight titles state the finding, so generate or check them from the data and avoid causal claims.
- Contrast ratio (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05) runs from 1 to 21.

### Common mistakes

- Using a rainbow scale for ordered values. Symptom: apparent boundaries and bright bands in smooth data. Fix: a sequential scale such as Viridis.
- Eight or more categorical colours. Symptom: a legend nobody can read. Fix: group small categories into `Other`, or use small multiples.
- Red and green as the only difference. Symptom: series look identical to some readers and in greyscale. Fix: a colour-blind-safe palette plus markers or direct labels.
- A title that claims a cause. Symptom: "Sales rose because of the new site". Fix: state what the data show: "Sales rose 12% after the new site launched".

### Revision notes

- Palette types: categorical, sequential, diverging.
- Luminance from linearised RGB; contrast ratio from 1 to 21; 4.5 for normal text.
- Focus colour: grey plus one strong colour plus one annotation.
- Insight title: claim, number, base; check it against the data.
