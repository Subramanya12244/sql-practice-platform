## Objectives

- Turn a product idea into an experiment plan with one primary metric, a hypothesis, a minimum detectable effect, a significance level and a power target.
- Compute the required sample size per group for a conversion-rate test and see how it changes with the effect you want to detect.
- Check that random assignment worked, using a sample-ratio-mismatch test.
- Analyse a two-group conversion experiment with a two-proportion z-test, a confidence interval for the difference and both absolute and relative lift.
- Explain why peeking at results early inflates false positives, and write up a result with a clear decision and caveats.

## Understand

### What an A/B test is

An **A/B test** (a randomised controlled experiment) compares two versions of one thing. Group A, the **control**, sees the current version. Group B, the **treatment** (or variant), sees the change. The key ingredient is **random assignment**: each visitor is placed in A or B by chance, so the two groups differ only in the change and in luck. That is what lets you say the change caused any difference, which the correlations of Day 101 and the chi-square test of Day 104 on observational data cannot do. Without randomisation, a difference might come from who happened to see each version.

### Designing before collecting

The decisions that make a test trustworthy are all made before any data arrives.

- **Hypothesis.** State the change and the expected effect in one sentence: "Showing delivery cost on the product page raises the purchase rate."
- **Primary metric.** Choose one number that decides success, such as purchase conversion (purchases divided by visitors). Other metrics are secondary or **guardrail metrics**, which you watch only to make sure the change does not damage something else, for example refund rate or page load time. Choosing the winning metric after seeing the results is a form of cheating called metric shopping, because with many metrics one of them will look good by chance.
- **Unit of randomisation.** Usually a user, not a page view. If the same person could see both versions, observations are not independent.
- **Significance level (alpha).** The false-positive rate you accept, normally 0.05. A **false positive** (Type I error, Day 90) means declaring a winner when the two versions are really the same.
- **Power.** The probability of detecting a real effect of the planned size, normally 0.8. The remaining 0.2 is the **false-negative** rate (Type II error, beta).
- **Minimum detectable effect (MDE).** The smallest improvement worth acting on, for example "from 10% to 11%", an absolute difference of 0.01. It is a business decision, not a statistical one.

### Sample size

Detecting a small difference in a noisy metric needs many users. For conversion rates the sample size per group, with baseline rate p1 and target rate p2 = p1 + MDE, is approximately

n = (z_a x sqrt(2 x pbar x (1 - pbar)) + z_b x sqrt(p1 x (1 - p1) + p2 x (1 - p2)))² / (p2 - p1)²

where pbar is the average of p1 and p2, z_a is the normal quantile for alpha (1.96 for a two-sided 0.05) and z_b is the normal quantile for power (0.84 for 80%). Read the formula as a story: the numerator is the "noise" you must overcome, built from the variance of each group's rate, and the denominator is the "signal", the squared effect. Halving the MDE makes the denominator four times smaller, so you need roughly four times as many users. This is why testing for tiny improvements is expensive, and why you fix the sample size before the test starts.

### Checking the assignment

Before reading any result, confirm that randomisation did what you intended. If the design was a 50/50 split but you see 50,000 versus 48,500 users, that **sample ratio mismatch (SRM)** signals a bug, such as one version failing to load for some browsers. A chi-square goodness-of-fit test (Day 104) against the intended split detects it; a very small p-value, conventionally below 0.001 for this check, means the results should not be trusted until the cause is found.

### Analysing the result

For conversion, each group has a count of conversions out of a number of users. The **two-proportion z-test** compares the two rates. Under H0 (no difference) both groups share one pooled rate, and the standard error of the difference uses that pooled value. The z statistic is the observed difference divided by that standard error, and the two-sided p-value comes from the normal curve. Report more than the p-value:

- **Absolute lift**: rate B minus rate A, in percentage points.
- **Relative lift**: absolute lift divided by rate A. A move from 10% to 11% is +1.0 point or +10% relative; mixing the two is a classic reporting error.
- **Confidence interval** for the difference, which shows the plausible range of the true effect. An interval of 0.2 to 1.8 points is a very different message from 0.0 to 2.0, even if both are significant.

### Peeking and stopping rules

If you check the p-value every day and stop when it dips below 0.05, the real false-positive rate climbs well above 5%, because each look is another chance for noise to cross the line. The remedy is to fix the sample size in advance and read the result once at the end (sequential methods exist, but are beyond this lesson). Other common traps are running for less than a full weekly cycle, when behaviour differs between weekdays and weekends, and ending the test early because the result "looks good".

### Common misconception

"p = 0.04 means there is a 96% chance the variant is better." It does not. The p-value is the probability of data this extreme if there were no difference. It is not the probability that either hypothesis is true. Another misconception is that "not significant" means "no effect": an under-powered test often cannot tell, and the confidence interval shows what effects remain possible.

### Why it matters

Companies make real decisions on experiment readouts: ship, drop or iterate. Analysts are expected to design the test, confirm it ran cleanly, analyse it correctly and say clearly what the evidence does and does not support.

## Syntax and key functions

Sample size and critical values:

```python
z_a = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)
z_b = stats.norm.ppf(power)
```

`norm.ppf` is the inverse of the normal cumulative curve: give it a probability and it returns the matching z value.

Two-proportion z-test with statsmodels:

```python
z, p = proportions_ztest(count=[conv_b, conv_a], nobs=[n_b, n_a])
low, high = proportion_confint(conv, n, alpha=0.05, method="normal")
```

`count` and `nobs` are lists with the conversions and the group sizes in the same order. The statistic is positive when the first group's rate is higher.

| Function or quantity | Returns |
|---|---|
| `stats.norm.ppf(q)` | The z value with probability q to its left. |
| `stats.norm.cdf(z)` | Probability to the left of z. |
| `stats.chisquare(obs, f_exp=exp)` | SRM check against the intended split. |
| `proportions_ztest(count, nobs)` | z statistic and two-sided p-value. |
| `p_b - p_a` | Absolute lift. |
| `(p_b - p_a) / p_a` | Relative lift. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Writing the plan as data

An experiment plan is a small dictionary written before launch. Storing it in code makes the decisions explicit and reusable by the later steps. Here the baseline is 10% and the smallest effect worth acting on is one percentage point.

```python run
plan = {
    "hypothesis": "Showing delivery cost on the product page raises purchase rate",
    "primary_metric": "purchase conversion per user",
    "guardrails": ["refund rate", "page load time"],
    "baseline": 0.10,
    "mde_abs": 0.01,
    "alpha": 0.05,
    "power": 0.80,
}
for key, value in plan.items():
    print(f"{key}: {value}")
print("target rate:", round(plan["baseline"] + plan["mde_abs"], 2))
```

```output
hypothesis: Showing delivery cost on the product page raises purchase rate
primary_metric: purchase conversion per user
guardrails: ['refund rate', 'page load time']
baseline: 0.1
mde_abs: 0.01
alpha: 0.05
power: 0.8
target rate: 0.11
```

### Example 2: Sample size per group

The function turns the formula into code. The table then shows how fast the requirement grows when the effect you want to detect shrinks, with the baseline fixed at 10%.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def sample_size(p1, mde, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    p2 = p1 + mde
    pbar = (p1 + p2) / 2
    z_a = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)
    z_b = stats.norm.ppf(power)
    top = z_a * np.sqrt(2 * pbar * (1 - pbar)) + z_b * np.sqrt(p1 * (1 - p1) + p2 * (1 - p2))
    return int(np.ceil(top ** 2 / (p2 - p1) ** 2))

for mde in (0.02, 0.01, 0.005):
    print(f"MDE {mde:.3f}: {sample_size(0.10, mde):>7,} users per group")
```

```output
MDE 0.020:   3,841 users per group
MDE 0.010:  14,751 users per group
MDE 0.005:  57,763 users per group
```

### Example 3: Random assignment and the SRM check

Users are assigned with a seeded random generator, a stand-in for the hashing a real platform uses. The observed group sizes are compared with the intended 50/50 split by a goodness-of-fit test from Day 104. A large p-value means no sign of a broken split.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
group = rng.choice(["A", "B"], size=20000)
n_a = int((group == "A").sum())
n_b = int((group == "B").sum())
print("A:", n_a, "B:", n_b)
chi2, p = stats.chisquare([n_a, n_b], f_exp=[10000, 10000])
print("SRM p-value:", round(p, 4))
print("split looks fine" if p > 0.001 else "sample ratio mismatch: investigate")

chi2, p = stats.chisquare([10000, 9400], f_exp=[9700, 9700])
print("broken split p-value:", format(p, ".2e"))
```

```output
A: 10013 B: 9987
SRM p-value: 0.8541
split looks fine
broken split p-value: 1.65e-05
```

### Example 4: Analysing the result

Group A converted 520 of 5,000 users and group B converted 590 of 5,000. The z-test is computed by hand with the pooled rate and checked against statsmodels. The confidence interval for the difference uses the unpooled standard error.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest

conv_a, n_a = 520, 5000
conv_b, n_b = 590, 5000
p_a, p_b = conv_a / n_a, conv_b / n_b

pooled = (conv_a + conv_b) / (n_a + n_b)
se = np.sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * (1 / n_a + 1 / n_b))
z = (p_b - p_a) / se
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z)))
print("by hand: z =", round(z, 3), "p =", round(p_value, 4))

z2, p2 = proportions_ztest([conv_b, conv_a], [n_b, n_a])
print("statsmodels: z =", round(z2, 3), "p =", round(p2, 4))

se_diff = np.sqrt(p_a * (1 - p_a) / n_a + p_b * (1 - p_b) / n_b)
low = (p_b - p_a) - 1.96 * se_diff
high = (p_b - p_a) + 1.96 * se_diff
print("absolute lift:", round(p_b - p_a, 4), "relative lift:", round((p_b - p_a) / p_a, 3))
print("95% CI for difference:", round(low, 4), "to", round(high, 4))
```

```output
by hand: z = 2.228 p = 0.0259
statsmodels: z = 2.228 p = 0.0259
absolute lift: 0.014 relative lift: 0.135
95% CI for difference: 0.0017 to 0.0263
```

### Example 5: Why peeking inflates false positives

This simulation runs 1,000 A/A tests, where both groups have the same true 10% rate, so every "win" is a false positive. Each test collects five batches of 500 users per group. A fixed-sample analyst reads the result once, after the last batch. A peeking analyst stops at the first batch where p is below 0.05.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
sims, batches, batch_n, rate = 1000, 5, 500, 0.10
a = (rng.random((sims, batches, batch_n)) < rate).sum(axis=2).cumsum(axis=1)
b = (rng.random((sims, batches, batch_n)) < rate).sum(axis=2).cumsum(axis=1)
n = batch_n * np.arange(1, batches + 1)

pa, pb = a / n, b / n
pooled = (a + b) / (2 * n)
se = np.sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * 2 / n)
z = (pb - pa) / se
p = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(np.abs(z)))

print("false positives, one look at the end:", round((p[:, -1] < 0.05).mean(), 3))
print("false positives, stop at first p < 0.05:", round((p < 0.05).any(axis=1).mean(), 3))
```

```output
false positives, one look at the end: 0.053
false positives, stop at first p < 0.05: 0.148
```

### Example 6: A short readout

The final step packages the analysis as a report: the result, the effect with its uncertainty, the decision against the plan and the caveats. The decision rule is written down in code so that it cannot drift after the fact.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

conv = {"A": (520, 5000), "B": (590, 5000)}
(ca, na), (cb, nb) = conv["A"], conv["B"]
pa, pb = ca / na, cb / nb
pooled = (ca + cb) / (na + nb)
se = np.sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * (1 / na + 1 / nb))
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs((pb - pa) / se)))
se_d = np.sqrt(pa * (1 - pa) / na + pb * (1 - pb) / nb)
ci = ((pb - pa) - 1.96 * se_d, (pb - pa) + 1.96 * se_d)

mde = 0.01
decision = "ship" if p_value < 0.05 and ci[0] > 0 else "do not ship"
print(f"Rates: A {pa:.1%}, B {pb:.1%}")
print(f"Lift: {(pb - pa) * 100:+.2f} points ({(pb - pa) / pa:+.1%} relative)")
print(f"95% CI: {ci[0]:+.2%} to {ci[1]:+.2%}; p = {p_value:.4f}")
print("Planned MDE reached:", pb - pa >= mde)
print("Decision:", decision)
print("Caveat: one test, one week; the CI lower end is below the planned MDE.")
```

```output
Rates: A 10.4%, B 11.8%
Lift: +1.40 points (+13.5% relative)
95% CI: +0.17% to +2.63%; p = 0.0259
Planned MDE reached: True
Decision: ship
Caveat: one test, one week; the CI lower end is below the planned MDE.
```

## Real-world applications

- **Product and growth teams.** Every checkout, onboarding or pricing change goes through a plan like Example 1, a sample-size calculation as in Example 2 and a readout like Example 6 before it is shipped.
- **Experiment platform quality.** Data teams run the SRM check from Example 3 automatically on every experiment, because a broken split invalidates all later metrics.
- **Marketing.** Email subject lines and landing pages are compared with the two-proportion test on open or click rates, reporting the confidence interval of the lift, as in Example 4.
- **Governance.** Fixing sample size and reading once, as Example 5 motivates, is the rule that stops teams from shipping noise.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Random assignment makes an A/B test causal; decide hypothesis, one primary metric, alpha, power and MDE before launch.
- Sample size grows with the inverse square of the effect: half the MDE needs about four times the users.
- Check the split with a sample-ratio-mismatch test before looking at results.
- Report absolute and relative lift, the confidence interval and the p-value, not the p-value alone.
- Peeking and stopping at the first significant result inflates false positives well above alpha.

### Common mistakes

- Choosing the success metric after seeing the data, or reporting whichever metric was significant.
- Stopping the test the day the p-value first drops below 0.05. The false-positive rate is then far above 5%.
- Confusing absolute and relative lift: "+1 point" and "+10%" can describe the same result.
- Reading p = 0.2 as "no effect" instead of "not enough evidence at this sample size"; look at the confidence interval.
- Ignoring a sample ratio mismatch and analysing a broken experiment.
- Randomising by page view instead of by user, so one person contributes to both groups.

### Revision notes

- `sample_size(p1, mde)` uses z_a, z_b and the two group variances; ceil the result.
- `proportions_ztest([conv_b, conv_a], [n_b, n_a])` returns z and a two-sided p.
- Absolute lift = pB - pA; relative lift = (pB - pA) / pA.
- CI for the difference: (pB - pA) plus or minus 1.96 x unpooled standard error.
- SRM: `chisquare` of group sizes against the intended split.
