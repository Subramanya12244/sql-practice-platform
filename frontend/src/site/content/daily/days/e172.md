## Objectives

- Plan and run a timed mock interview of about 60 minutes with a SQL part, a Python part and a design part, and keep to the time boxes.
- Score the mock with a rubric of weighted dimensions and turn the score into a short list of next actions.
- Give and receive feedback in a structured form: what worked, what to change, one specific drill.
- Check a portfolio against a checklist (problem, data, code, tests, README, results) and write the README of one project.
- Review the engineer skills practised so far, mark each as strong, adequate or weak, and choose what to revise first.

## Understand

### Why a mock interview

Knowing the material and performing under a clock are different skills. In a real interview you have to read the problem, speak while thinking, write code that others can follow and stay calm when stuck. The only way to practise this is to simulate the conditions: a fixed time, an unfamiliar question, a person (or a recording) watching, and no looking things up. Run a mock after each block of study, not only at the end, because each one shows what to fix next.

### The format

A useful mock lasts about 60 minutes and mirrors a data engineering loop: 5 minutes of introduction (who you are, one project in two minutes), 15 minutes of SQL (one question, solved in steps, with edge cases), 15 minutes of Python (one data-structure or file-processing question with tests), 20 minutes of pipeline design (requirements to trade-offs, as in Day 171) and 5 minutes for your questions and for feedback. Use an unseen question for each part. Choose questions from the sets and exercises of the last days that you have not solved, and keep a record of the questions used so that you do not repeat them. Speak your thinking aloud during the whole time, since the interviewer can only assess what they hear.

### Scoring

Score each part on the same four dimensions, from 1 (weak) to 4 (strong):

- **Correctness**: does the solution work, including edge cases?
- **Approach**: was there a plan, a sensible structure and a stated complexity or cost?
- **Communication**: did you clarify, think aloud and explain results and trade-offs?
- **Robustness and quality**: tests, naming, handling of bad data, failure and idempotency.

Give each dimension a weight (for example correctness 40 percent, approach 25, communication 20, robustness 15) and compute a weighted score out of 4 for each part, and an overall score across the parts. The numbers are for tracking your own progress, not a prediction of any real outcome: different employers weigh things differently. What matters is the trend across mocks and the specific weakness it reveals (for instance, high correctness and low communication means you should practise speaking while solving).

### Feedback

Feedback is only useful if it is specific and actionable. Use three parts: **what worked** (keep doing it), **what to change** (one or two items, with an example from the session), and **one drill** (a concrete exercise for the coming week, such as "solve three window-function questions in 12 minutes each, saying the plan first"). If you practise alone, record yourself and review the recording against the rubric, pausing wherever you went quiet or skipped the edge cases.

### The portfolio

A portfolio is a small number of projects that show you can build something end to end. For each project the checklist is: a clear **problem statement** and who would use the result; **data** that is public or generated in code, with its source stated; **code** that runs from a clean checkout with one command; **tests** for the transformations and checks; a **README** with the architecture in a few lines, how to run it, the results, the decisions and trade-offs, and the known limitations; and **evidence of quality** such as reconciliation counts or a data-quality report. Two or three finished, documented projects (for example an incremental ETL pipeline, a validation pipeline and a star schema warehouse like the ones in this programme) are worth more than ten half-finished ones. Remove secrets, keep the repository tidy and make sure the README explains why you chose each tool.

### Reviewing the engineer skills

Before the capstone, take stock of the path: Python and pandas for data work; SQL from joins and windows to constraints and upserts; loading patterns (full, incremental, upsert, change data capture); orchestration; data quality and testing; warehouse modelling (facts, dimensions, slowly changing dimensions); Spark for larger data; and now interviews. Mark each as strong, adequate or weak with an example of evidence ("I wrote an idempotent upsert in the incremental project"), then give the weak ones the first revision hours. Honest self-assessment, backed by evidence from exercises, beats a feeling of familiarity.

### Why it matters, and the misconception

The mock and the portfolio are how a skill becomes visible to others. The misconception is that more study replaces practice under conditions: reading ten more solutions helps less than one timed attempt followed by feedback.

## Syntax and key functions

```text
60-minute mock: intro 5 | SQL 15 | Python 15 | design 20 | questions and feedback 5
score = sum(weight x rating) / sum(weight)       # rating 1 to 4 per dimension
```

| Item | What it is |
|---|---|
| rubric dimension | One aspect scored 1 to 4 (correctness, approach, communication, robustness). |
| weight | Share of the total score given to a dimension. |
| time box | A fixed number of minutes for a part of the interview. |
| drill | A short, specific practice exercise chosen from the feedback. |
| portfolio checklist | The list of things each project must contain. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: A time plan that adds up

The plan is a dictionary of parts and minutes. The code checks that the parts add up to the 60-minute slot and prints the clock time at which each part should end, so that you can see during the mock whether you are behind.

```python run
plan = [("introduction", 5), ("SQL", 15), ("Python", 15), ("design", 20), ("questions and feedback", 5)]
total = sum(m for _, m in plan)
print("total minutes:", total, "| fits the 60-minute slot:", total == 60)
clock = 0
for name, minutes in plan:
    clock += minutes
    print(f"{name:24} {minutes:2d} min, ends at minute {clock}")
```

```output
total minutes: 60 | fits the 60-minute slot: True
introduction              5 min, ends at minute 5
SQL                      15 min, ends at minute 20
Python                   15 min, ends at minute 35
design                   20 min, ends at minute 55
questions and feedback    5 min, ends at minute 60
```

If you reach minute 30 and are still in the SQL part, you know that the Python part will be rushed, and you should simplify rather than polish.

### Example 2: A weighted score and a next action

Scores for the three technical parts are entered with the four dimensions. The weighted score per part is computed, and the weakest dimension across the whole mock is reported as the thing to practise.

```python run
weights = {"correctness": 0.40, "approach": 0.25, "communication": 0.20, "robustness": 0.15}
scores = {
    "SQL":    {"correctness": 4, "approach": 3, "communication": 2, "robustness": 3},
    "Python": {"correctness": 3, "approach": 3, "communication": 2, "robustness": 2},
    "design": {"correctness": 3, "approach": 4, "communication": 3, "robustness": 2},
}
part_scores = {p: sum(weights[d] * r for d, r in s.items()) for p, s in scores.items()}
for p, v in part_scores.items():
    print(f"{p:7} weighted score: {v:.2f} / 4")
print("overall:", round(sum(part_scores.values()) / len(part_scores), 2))
average = {d: sum(s[d] for s in scores.values()) / len(scores) for d in weights}
weakest = min(average, key=average.get)
print("weakest dimension:", weakest, "->", round(average[weakest], 2))
```

```output
SQL     weighted score: 3.20 / 4
Python  weighted score: 2.65 / 4
design  weighted score: 3.10 / 4
overall: 2.98
weakest dimension: communication -> 2.33
```

The scores are not a prediction. They show that, in this example, the answers were correct but the candidate did not explain them well, so next week's drill is to solve questions while speaking the plan aloud.

### Example 3: A self-test harness for a Python answer

In the Python part of an interview you are expected to test your own function. A small harness runs named cases against your function and prints PASS or FAIL for each, which you can write in two minutes on any question. The function under test finds the second highest distinct value.

```python run
def second_highest(values):
    distinct = sorted(set(values), reverse=True)
    return distinct[1] if len(distinct) > 1 else None

cases = [
    ("normal", [3, 1, 4, 1, 5], 4),
    ("duplicates at the top", [5, 5, 4], 4),
    ("single value", [7, 7], None),
    ("empty", [], None),
    ("negatives", [-1, -5, -3], -3),
]
failures = 0
for name, data, expected in cases:
    got = second_highest(data)
    ok = got == expected
    failures += not ok
    print(("PASS " if ok else "FAIL ") + name, "->", got)
print("failures:", failures)
```

```output
PASS normal -> 4
PASS duplicates at the top -> 4
PASS single value -> None
PASS empty -> None
PASS negatives -> -3
failures: 0
```

Edge cases (duplicates at the top, a single distinct value, empty input) are exactly what interviewers probe. Saying "I would test these" and then running them is worth more than a longer solution.

### Example 4: A SQL warm-up under the clock

A 10-minute warm-up: "For each country of the customers, show the number of customers and the number that have ordered, and keep countries with at least 3 customers." The approach is to join customers to orders with a left join and count distinct customers on each side.

```sql run
SELECT c.country,
       COUNT(DISTINCT c.customer_id) AS customers,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.customer_id) AS ordering
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
GROUP BY c.country
HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT c.customer_id) >= 3
ORDER BY customers DESC, c.country
LIMIT 8;
```

```output
country | customers | ordering
USA | 13 | 13
France | 11 | 10
Germany | 11 | 11
Brazil | 9 | 9
UK | 7 | 7
Mexico | 5 | 5
Spain | 5 | 4
Venezuela | 4 | 4
```

Say the plan first ("left join so that customers without orders are counted, `DISTINCT` because the join repeats customers"), then write, then explain one number: a country where the two counts differ has customers that never ordered.

### Example 5: A portfolio checklist as a completeness score

Each project is checked against the checklist. The code reports the share of items done for each project and the first missing item to work on, so the portfolio review produces a to-do list instead of a feeling.

```python run
checklist = ["problem statement", "data source", "runs from a clean checkout", "tests", "README with architecture", "results", "limitations"]
projects = {
    "incremental ETL": {"problem statement", "data source", "runs from a clean checkout", "tests", "README with architecture", "results"},
    "validation pipeline": {"problem statement", "data source", "tests"},
    "star schema warehouse": set(checklist),
}
for name, done in projects.items():
    missing = [item for item in checklist if item not in done]
    pct = round(100 * len(done) / len(checklist))
    print(f"{name:22} {pct:3d}% done | next: {missing[0] if missing else 'nothing, ready to share'}")
```

```output
incremental ETL         86% done | next: limitations
validation pipeline     43% done | next: runs from a clean checkout
star schema warehouse  100% done | next: nothing, ready to share
```

A project at 100 percent is ready to show. The others have a named next step. Finish the closest to done first, because one finished project beats two almost-finished ones.

## Real-world applications

- **Interview preparation**: a weekly 60-minute mock with the rubric gives a measurable trend and a drill for the next week.
- **Peer review**: a study partner uses the same rubric, which makes feedback comparable between sessions.
- **Hiring signal**: a portfolio with a README, tests and reconciliation output answers the question "can this person ship a pipeline" before the interview starts.
- **Team onboarding**: the skills review format (strong, adequate, weak, with evidence) is the same one used in career conversations.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Simulate real conditions: time boxes, unseen questions, thinking aloud, no lookups.
- Score the same four dimensions every time and track the trend; scores are for you, not predictions.
- Feedback has three parts: what worked, what to change, one drill.
- A portfolio needs few, finished, documented projects with tests and results.
- Review skills with evidence and revise the weak ones first.

### Common mistakes

- Practising by reading solutions only. You recognise answers but cannot produce them under a clock; do timed attempts.
- Repeating questions you have solved. The mock tests recall, not skill; use unseen ones.
- Going silent while thinking. The interviewer cannot score thoughts; narrate the plan.
- Collecting many half-finished projects. Finish and document two or three.
- Treating a mock score as a prediction. It tracks your progress against your own rubric only.

### Revision notes

- Mock: 5 + 15 + 15 + 20 + 5 = 60 minutes.
- Dimensions: correctness, approach, communication, robustness.
- Feedback: worked, change, drill.
- Portfolio: problem, data, code, tests, README, results, limitations.
