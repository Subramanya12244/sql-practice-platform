## Understand

### Price is a lever with a cost

Price is the one lever that changes revenue and margin at the same time. Raising the price earns more on each unit but usually sells fewer units; lowering it sells more units at less per unit. Pricing analytics asks by how much demand reacts, and whether the trade is worth it. Two measures come first. **List revenue** is `unit_price * quantity` before any discount, and **net revenue** is `unit_price * quantity * (1 - discount)`. The difference is the **discount cost**. A report that shows only net revenue hides how much was given away. In Northwind each order line has a `discount` between 0 and 0.15, and `order_details.unit_price` is the price charged, which can differ from the current product list price.

### Discounts and margin

A discount lowers the margin on every unit sold, not only on the extra units it brings. Take a product with price 100 and cost 70, so the gross margin is 30 per unit (30 percent of price). A 10 percent discount cuts the price to 90 and the margin per unit to 20, a loss of a third of the profit per unit. To earn the same total profit, the business must sell 30 / 20 = 1.5 times as many units, which is 50 percent more volume. The general break-even rule is: required volume increase = `d / (m - d)`, where `d` is the discount and `m` is the gross margin, both as fractions of the original price. With d = 0.10 and m = 0.30 this gives 0.10 / 0.20 = 0.5. When the discount is as large as the margin (d = m) no volume increase is enough, because every extra unit loses money. This rule is the first test of any promotion, and many promotions fail it.

### Price elasticity

**Price elasticity of demand** is the percentage change in units sold divided by the percentage change in price. If a 10 percent rise in price lowers units by 15 percent, the elasticity is -1.5. Elasticity is normally negative, and what matters is its size:

- If it is below -1 (for example -1.5), demand is **elastic**: units fall by a larger percentage than price rises, so revenue falls when price rises and rises when price falls.
- If it is between -1 and 0 (for example -0.5), demand is **inelastic**: units fall by less than the price rises, so raising the price raises revenue.

Elasticity is estimated from data in two ways. The **arc** method uses two observations: `(Q2 - Q1) / midpoint Q` divided by `(P2 - P1) / midpoint P`. The **log-log** method fits a straight line to `log(units)` against `log(price)` over several price points, and the slope is the elasticity directly: that comes from the fact that a constant elasticity means `Q = k * P^e`, whose logarithm is a straight line with slope `e`. The log-log slope uses all the points and so is less sensitive to one noisy observation.

### Where elasticity data comes from, and its limits

Elasticity can only be trusted when price changed **independently** of other causes. If a company raised prices in December, units may have risen because it is December, and a regression of units on price would show a positive (wrong) slope. The cleanest evidence is an experiment where price is randomly assigned across stores or customers, or a natural change with a comparison group. Observational data from past prices also mixes in seasonality, promotions, competitors, and the fact that businesses discount when demand is weak. A good analyst states the range of plausible elasticity, not a single decimal, and notes that it holds only inside the range of prices that were tested.

### Product mix

The **mix** is the proportion of each product (or category) in sales. Two questions come with it. Which products carry the revenue? A **revenue share** table by category answers it. And what is the **average price per unit** of the mix, calculated as total revenue divided by total units? That average moves when the mix moves, even if no product changed its price: selling relatively more of a premium product lifts the average, selling more of a cheap product lowers it. The same holds for margin. **Blended margin** is total gross profit divided by total revenue, which is the revenue-weighted average of product margins. If customers shift toward a low-margin product, the blended margin falls while every individual margin stays constant. This is why a finance report can show falling margin when nobody changed a price (Day 144 split revenue into price, volume and mix effects).

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Pricing decisions are among the highest-return choices in a business, because a one percent price improvement usually flows to profit almost entirely, whereas a one percent volume improvement still carries costs. The misconception is that a discount is a growth tool that always pays for itself. It pays only when the volume gain exceeds the break-even rule, and part of the gain is often only borrowed from the future or from other products (cannibalisation). Elasticity numbers deserve the same caution: they describe the tested range and the tested customers.

## Syntax and key functions

```sql-syntax
SELECT discount,
       SUM(unit_price * quantity)                       AS list_revenue,
       SUM(unit_price * quantity * (1 - discount))      AS net_revenue,
       SUM(unit_price * quantity * discount)            AS discount_cost
FROM order_details GROUP BY discount;
```

```python
elasticity = np.polyfit(np.log(price), np.log(units), 1)[0]
breakeven_uplift = d / (m - d)
blended_margin = (units * (price - cost)).sum() / (units * price).sum()
```

| Function or formula | Returns |
|---|---|
| `np.log(x)` | Natural logarithm of each value. |
| `np.polyfit(x, y, 1)` | Slope and intercept of the best straight line. |
| `d / (m - d)` | Volume increase needed for a discount to break even. |
| `SUM(a) OVER ()` | Total over all rows, for shares. |
| `revenue / units` | Average price per unit of a mix. |
| `profit / revenue` | Blended margin. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Discount tiers in Northwind

Order lines are grouped by their discount. The table shows how many lines and units sold at each discount, the list revenue, the net revenue and the cost of the discount.

```sql run
SELECT discount,
       COUNT(*) AS lines,
       SUM(quantity) AS units,
       ROUND(SUM(unit_price * quantity)::numeric, 0) AS list_revenue,
       ROUND(SUM(unit_price * quantity * (1 - discount))::numeric, 0) AS net_revenue,
       ROUND(SUM(unit_price * quantity * discount)::numeric, 0) AS discount_cost
FROM order_details
GROUP BY discount
ORDER BY discount;
```

```output
discount | lines | units | list_revenue | net_revenue | discount_cost
0 | 100 | 1920 | 48443 | 48443 | 0
0.05 | 50 | 1000 | 35442 | 33670 | 1772
0.1 | 50 | 1080 | 37067 | 33361 | 3707
0.15 | 50 | 1080 | 26878 | 22846 | 4032
```

Discounted lines sold only slightly more units per line (about 21 or 22) than full-price lines (about 19), yet the three discount tiers together cost about 9,500 in revenue that was never collected. Whether the extra units paid for that is the question of Example 4.

### Example 2: Category mix and average price per unit

Revenue by category with its share of the total, and the average net price per unit, for the categories in Northwind. The share uses a window sum over the grouped rows.

```sql run
SELECT c.category_name,
       ROUND(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount))::numeric, 0) AS revenue,
       ROUND((100 * SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) / SUM(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount))) OVER ())::numeric, 1) AS share_pct,
       ROUND((SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) / SUM(od.quantity))::numeric, 2) AS avg_price_per_unit
FROM order_details od
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = od.product_id
JOIN categories c ON c.category_id = p.category_id
GROUP BY c.category_name
ORDER BY revenue DESC, c.category_name;
```

```output
category_name | revenue | share_pct | avg_price_per_unit
Beverages | 29336 | 21.2 | 35.60
Confections | 20512 | 14.8 | 24.05
Meat/Poultry | 18705 | 13.5 | 73.07
Dairy Products | 18330 | 13.3 | 24.28
Seafood | 16579 | 12.0 | 19.41
Condiments | 15177 | 11.0 | 19.68
Produce | 11841 | 8.6 | 32.26
Grains/Cereals | 7840 | 5.7 | 19.60
```

The categories with the highest revenue are not always the ones with the highest price per unit: a category can lead because it sells many cheap units. The mix, not one price, determines the average.

### Example 3: Price elasticity from a price test

Units sold at five tested prices (with a little noise, seeded) are fitted in log-log form. The slope is the elasticity. The sign of revenue change for a 10 percent price rise is predicted from it and compared with the fitted demand.

```python run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
price = np.array([8.0, 9.0, 10.0, 11.0, 12.0])
units = 1000 * (price / 10) ** -1.5 * (1 + rng.normal(0, 0.03, 5))
e = float(np.polyfit(np.log(price), np.log(units), 1)[0])
print("units:", [round(float(u)) for u in units])
print("elasticity:", round(e, 2))
change = 1.10 ** (e + 1)
print("revenue change for +10% price:", round((change - 1) * 100, 1), "%")
print("demand is", "elastic" if e < -1 else "inelastic")
```

```output
units: [1398, 1182, 992, 844, 750]
elasticity: -1.56
revenue change for +10% price: -5.2 %
demand is elastic
```

An elasticity below -1 means a price rise loses more in units than it gains per unit, so revenue falls. The revenue formula uses the fact that with constant elasticity revenue is proportional to price raised to the power one plus elasticity. The estimate holds only for the tested range of 8 to 12.

### Example 4: Does a discount pay for itself?

For a price of 100 and a gross margin of 30 percent, the break-even volume increase of three discount levels is calculated with the rule `d / (m - d)`, then checked by computing profit directly at that volume.

```python run
price, margin = 100.0, 0.30
cost = price * (1 - margin)
base_units = 1000
print("base profit:", round(base_units * (price - cost)))
for d in (0.05, 0.10, 0.20):
    need = d / (margin - d)
    units = base_units * (1 + need)
    profit = units * (price * (1 - d) - cost)
    print(f"discount {d:.0%}: volume must rise {need:.1%}; profit at that volume {profit:.0f}")
```

```output
base profit: 30000
discount 5%: volume must rise 20.0%; profit at that volume 30000
discount 10%: volume must rise 50.0%; profit at that volume 30000
discount 20%: volume must rise 200.0%; profit at that volume 30000
```

A 20 percent discount at a 30 percent margin needs the volume to triple, which is rarely realistic. A 5 percent discount needs only a one-fifth increase. The profit check proves the formula.

### Example 5: A mix shift lowers blended margin

Two products with fixed price and cost. In the second period customers buy relatively more of the low-margin product. Nothing changes except the mix, and the blended margin falls.

```python run
import numpy as np

price = np.array([50.0, 20.0])
cost = np.array([30.0, 16.0])
for name, units in (("period 1", np.array([400, 600])), ("period 2", np.array([300, 900]))):
    revenue = float((units * price).sum())
    profit = float((units * (price - cost)).sum())
    print(name, "revenue", round(revenue), "| avg price per unit", round(revenue / units.sum(), 2), "| blended margin", round(profit / revenue * 100, 1), "%")
print("margin per unit: product 1", round(float((price[0] - cost[0]) / price[0]) * 100), "%, product 2", round(float((price[1] - cost[1]) / price[1]) * 100), "%")
```

```output
period 1 revenue 32000 | avg price per unit 32.0 | blended margin 32.5 %
period 2 revenue 33000 | avg price per unit 27.5 | blended margin 29.1 %
margin per unit: product 1 40 %, product 2 20 %
```

The margin of each product is unchanged at 40 and 20 percent, yet the blended margin fell, and so did the average price per unit. This is a pure mix effect.

## Real-world applications

- **Promotion planning**: the break-even rule of Example 4 is applied to every proposed discount before it goes to the commercial team, with the expected volume uplift stated explicitly.
- **Price setting**: an elasticity estimate such as Example 3's suggests whether a price rise is likely to increase revenue, subject to tests and the range tested.
- **Portfolio management**: a revenue-share table, as in Example 2, shows how dependent the business is on one category and where the average price per unit can be improved.
- **Margin reporting**: finance teams separate a margin change caused by mix (Example 5) from one caused by price or cost, so that the right team investigates.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Discount cost is list revenue minus net revenue; report it next to net revenue.
- Break-even volume uplift for a discount is `d / (m - d)`, and impossible when `d >= m`.
- Elasticity below -1 means price cuts raise revenue; between -1 and 0 means price rises do.
- A log-log fit gives elasticity as the slope; trust it only for tested prices and independent price changes.
- Mix shifts move average price and blended margin even with fixed prices and costs.

### Common mistakes

- Using `products.unit_price` instead of `order_details.unit_price`. Historic revenue is then at today's list price; use the price at sale.
- Fitting units on price without logs and reading the slope as elasticity. It is a slope in units per currency unit, not a percentage ratio.
- Reading a positive slope as proof that raising prices increases demand. Seasonality or a promotion usually moved both; find an independent price change.
- Judging a discount by revenue alone. Revenue can rise while profit falls; check the break-even rule.
- Blaming a margin drop on a price cut when the cause was mix. Compare the margins within products first.

### Revision notes

- Net revenue = `unit_price * quantity * (1 - discount)`; discount cost = list minus net.
- Elasticity = % change in units / % change in price = log-log slope.
- Break-even volume uplift = `d / (m - d)`.
- Blended margin = total profit / total revenue; it moves with mix.
