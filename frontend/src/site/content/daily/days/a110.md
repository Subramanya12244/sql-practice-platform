## Objectives

- Aggregate event rows into a regular time series (weekly or monthly) and handle partial periods correctly.
- Build derived metrics from raw columns: orders, revenue, average order value and return rate per period.
- Smooth a series with rolling means and compute week-over-week and period-over-period growth rates.
- Describe a trend with a fitted slope and compare it with the normal week-to-week noise.
- Detect a repeating weekday pattern (seasonality) with a seasonal index, and test whether an apparent pattern is distinguishable from chance.

## Understand

### Time series and what makes them different

A **time series** is a set of values recorded in time order: daily orders, weekly revenue, monthly sign-ups. Time adds structure that ordinary columns lack. Rows are not interchangeable, because today depends on yesterday; the order matters, and recent values often resemble nearby values. Time-series EDA asks four questions: what is the **level** (typical size), is there a **trend** (a steady rise or fall), is there **seasonality** (a pattern that repeats at a fixed period, such as a weekly cycle), and is there **noise** (random variation that remains)? A fifth question is whether anything happened at a specific moment, such as a promotion or an outage.

### From events to a series

The orders table has one row per order, not one row per day. To get a series you **aggregate**: group the rows into regular periods and compute a metric for each. In pandas, `set_index("order_date").resample("W-SUN")` groups by calendar week ending on Sunday (you used `resample` on Day 77), and `agg` computes several metrics at once. Choose the period to match the question and the data volume. With about 600 orders over six months, daily counts are very noisy (a handful of orders each), weekly counts are readable, and monthly counts show only six points.

**Partial periods** are a common trap. If the data begins on a Wednesday, the first week contains five days, and its total is not comparable with the full weeks that follow. Comparing a five-day week with a seven-day week would show an invented drop. Either drop incomplete periods from the trend or compare per-day averages. Always check the number of days that each period actually contains.

### Derived metrics

A **derived metric** is a new quantity computed from raw columns. Revenue per week is the sum of `order_total`. **Average order value (AOV)** is revenue divided by the number of orders. **Return rate** is returns divided by orders. The important rule is to compute ratios from sums, not from averages of averages: weekly AOV is weekly revenue divided by weekly orders. Averaging each week's AOV over the weeks would weight a quiet week equally with a busy one and give a different, misleading figure. Document each metric's definition in one line, since "revenue" or "active customer" can mean different things to different people.

### Smoothing with rolling windows

Week-to-week values jump around. A **rolling mean** replaces each value by the average of the last k periods (Day 83), which smooths noise so the trend is easier to see. A 4-week window averages a month. The cost is lag: a rolling mean reacts to a change only gradually, and the first k - 1 values are missing because the window is not yet full. A wider window gives a smoother but slower line.

### Growth rates

A **growth rate** measures change relative to the starting point: (new - old) / old. Week-over-week growth compares a week with the previous week (`pct_change()`). Month-over-month and year-over-year compare longer gaps. Two warnings. First, percentages from a small base are unstable: going from 2 returns to 4 is +100% and means little. Second, growth rates of noisy weekly values swing wildly, so growth of a smoothed series or of a longer period is often more informative. Report the absolute change beside the percentage.

### Trend

A simple way to describe a trend is to fit a straight line through the series against the period number and read the slope: the average change per period. The slope means little until you compare it with the noise. If revenue changes by about 50 per week on average, but the week-to-week standard deviation is 800, the trend is small compared with the ordinary variation, and it would be unwise to call it a decline without more evidence. This is the same idea as the signal and noise in the A/B tests of Day 105.

### Seasonality

Seasonality is a repeating pattern, such as higher weekend sales. To measure a weekly pattern, divide each day's value by the rolling 7-day average around it (which removes the weekly cycle and the trend), then average these ratios for each weekday. The result is a **seasonal index**: 1.0 is average, 1.2 means that weekday is 20% above the weekly average. A weekly pattern needs enough data. With only a few orders per day, counts by weekday fluctuate a lot, so test whether the apparent pattern is real with the goodness-of-fit test of Day 104, comparing observed weekday counts with an even split.

### Common misconception

"A line going down means the business is shrinking." Check three things before saying so: that the last period is complete, that the drop is larger than the usual week-to-week variation, and that the starting point was not a one-off peak. Another misconception is that a rolling mean "removes" outliers. It spreads one extreme value across k periods.

### Why it matters

Leadership decisions are made on trend lines and growth numbers. A partial week, a ratio computed from averages or a growth rate from a tiny base can each produce a confident but wrong headline. Careful aggregation and a comparison with noise guard against that.

Weekly aggregation:

```python

np.polyfit(np.arange(len(s)), s, 1)
```

`np.polyfit(x, y, 1)` returns the slope and the intercept of the best straight line.

| Function | Returns |
|---|---|
| `resample("W-SUN")` | Groups by calendar week ending on Sunday. |
| `resample("MS")` | Groups by calendar month, labelled at the month start. |
| `.rolling(k).mean()` | Mean of the last k values; first k - 1 are missing. |
| `.pct_change(periods=1)` | Relative change from the value `periods` earlier. |
| `.diff()` | Absolute change from the previous value. |
| `.cumsum()` | Running total. |
| `np.polyfit(x, y, 1)` | Slope and intercept of a fitted line. |
| `.dt.dayofweek` | Weekday number, Monday is 0 and Sunday is 6. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: From orders to weekly counts, and the partial-week check

The helper `weekly_table` aggregates the orders into weeks ending on Sunday and adds a `days` column that counts how many days of the data fall into each week. The data starts on a Wednesday, so the first week has five days. Only weeks with seven days are used for trend work.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

pd.set_option("display.width", 200)
pd.set_option("display.max_columns", 20)


def make_orders(messy=False):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 600
    category = rng.choice(["home", "beauty", "sports", "electronics", "toys"], size=n, p=[0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10])
    base = pd.Series({"home": 40, "beauty": 25, "sports": 55, "electronics": 180, "toys": 20})
    discount = rng.choice([0, 5, 10, 20], size=n, p=[0.6, 0.15, 0.15, 0.1])
    ship = np.clip(rng.poisson(3.5, n) + 1, 1, 12).astype(float)
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1001 + n),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 180, n), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 260, n),
        "channel": rng.choice(["organic", "paid", "email", "referral"], size=n, p=[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]),
        "device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop", "tablet"], size=n, p=[0.55, 0.35, 0.10]),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": np.clip(rng.poisson(0.8 + discount / 20, n) + 1, 1, 6),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * rng.lognormal(0, 0.25, n)).round(2),
        "discount_pct": discount,
        "shipping_days": ship,
    })
    df.loc[rng.choice(n, 4, replace=False), "unit_price"] *= 8
    df["order_total"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"] * (1 - df["discount_pct"] / 100)).round(2)
    df["rating"] = np.clip(np.round(4.3 - 0.2 * ship + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)), 1, 5)
    df["returned"] = rng.random(n) < 0.06 + 0.06 * (category == "electronics") + 0.03 * (ship > 6)
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.05, "shipping_days"] = np.nan
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.25, "rating"] = np.nan
    if messy:
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 25, replace=False), "channel"] = "Paid "
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 3, replace=False), "quantity"] = -1
        df = pd.concat([df, df.sample(6, random_state=1)], ignore_index=True)
    return df


def weekly_table(orders):
    first, last = orders["order_date"].min(), orders["order_date"].max()
    weekly = orders.set_index("order_date").resample("W-SUN").agg(
        orders=("order_id", "size"), revenue=("order_total", "sum"),
        returns=("returned", "sum"))
    ends = weekly.index
    starts = ends - pd.to_timedelta(6, unit="D")
    lo = starts.where(starts > first, first)
    hi = ends.where(ends < last, last)
    weekly["days"] = (hi - lo).days + 1
    weekly["aov"] = weekly["revenue"] / weekly["orders"]
    weekly["return_rate"] = weekly["returns"] / weekly["orders"]
    return weekly
orders = make_orders()
weekly = weekly_table(orders)
print(weekly[["orders", "revenue", "days"]].head(3).round(2).to_string())
print()
print("weeks:", len(weekly), "| complete weeks:", int((weekly["days"] == 7).sum()))
full = weekly[weekly["days"] == 7]
print("orders per complete week: min", full["orders"].min(), "max", full["orders"].max())
```

### Example 5: Is there a weekday pattern?

First, the orders table: counts by weekday are compared with an even split by the chi-square test of Day 104. The p-value is large, so the weekday differences in this data are compatible with chance. Second, a synthetic daily series that really contains a weekly cycle is built, and the seasonal index recovers it by dividing each day by the centred 7-day mean.

### Example 6: A growth-rate pitfall

Return counts per month are small (between 3 and 12), so the percentage changes of the count are huge (-75%, then +233%) and mostly reflect a tiny base. The return rate with its change in percentage points gives the same movement on a scale that can be compared across months. Showing counts, rate and changes together prevents a dramatic "returns tripled" headline.

## Real-world applications

- **Weekly business reviews.** An analyst builds the weekly table from Examples 1 to 3, restricts it to complete weeks and shows a rolling mean so that managers see the direction instead of the weekly noise.
- **Forecast and capacity planning.** A seasonal index like the one in Example 5 tells a warehouse how many more orders arrive on a weekend, so staffing can follow the cycle.
- **Metric reporting.** Defining AOV and return rate from sums, as in Example 2, keeps dashboard figures consistent with the finance numbers.
- **Anomaly review.** Comparing a week's change with the usual week-to-week standard deviation (Examples 3 and 4) shows whether a drop is news or normal variation.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Aggregate events into regular periods, and drop or normalise partial periods.
- Derived ratios come from sums: AOV is revenue divided by orders, not an average of averages.
- Rolling means smooth noise at the price of lag; the first k - 1 values are missing.
- Growth rates from small bases are unstable; show the absolute change and the underlying counts.
- Compare a trend slope with the normal variation, and test a weekday pattern before reporting it.

### Common mistakes

- Comparing a partial first or last period with full ones, which invents a drop. Fix: count the days per period and exclude or normalise.
- Averaging weekly ratios to get the overall ratio. The answer differs from revenue divided by orders. Fix: sum the numerator and denominator first.
- Reporting week-over-week percentage changes of tiny counts as findings. Fix: show counts and use longer periods.
- Using `resample("M")` or `"W"` without checking which day the label means. Fix: use explicit anchors such as `"W-SUN"` and `"MS"`, and inspect the first rows.
- Calling a line "a decline" without comparing the slope with the noise. Fix: print the slope next to the standard deviation.
- Forgetting that `rolling(k)` returns missing values at the start, which then break later arithmetic. Fix: check `isna().sum()`.

### Revision notes

- `resample("W-SUN").agg(...)`; add a `days` column to spot partial weeks.
- AOV = sum(revenue) / sum(orders).
- `rolling(4).mean()`, `pct_change()`, `diff()`.
- `np.polyfit(x, y, 1)` gives slope and intercept; compare slope with std.
- Seasonal index: value divided by centred 7-day mean, averaged by weekday.
