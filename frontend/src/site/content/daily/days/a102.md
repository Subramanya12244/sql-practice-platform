## Objectives

- Fit a straight line by least squares, and calculate its slope and intercept from means, standard deviations and the correlation.
- Interpret the intercept and slope in the units of the data, including what "holding other variables constant" means in a model with two predictors.
- Calculate and interpret R-squared, and say what it does not tell you.
- Calculate residuals, check that they behave as the model assumes, and recognise a curved pattern in them.
- Fit models with `numpy.polyfit` and `statsmodels` and print coefficients and R-squared rounded, without relying on the summary table.

## Understand

### From correlation to a prediction line

Correlation (Day 101) tells you that two variables move together and how tightly. **Linear regression** goes one step further: it finds the straight line that best predicts one variable from the other and describes the relationship in the units of the data. The variable you predict is the **response** or dependent variable, written `y`. The variable you predict from is the **predictor**, explanatory or independent variable, written `x`. The line has the form

`predicted y = intercept + slope * x`

The **slope** is the amount by which the predicted `y` changes when `x` rises by one unit. The **intercept** is the predicted `y` when `x` is 0. Unlike correlation, regression is not symmetric: predicting sales from advertising gives a different line from predicting advertising from sales.

### Least squares

Many lines could be drawn through a cloud of points, so we need a rule for the best one. For any candidate line, each point has a **residual**: the actual `y` minus the predicted `y`, that is, the vertical distance from the point to the line (positive if the point is above the line). **Ordinary least squares** (OLS) chooses the line that makes the sum of the squared residuals as small as possible. Squaring makes every residual positive and punishes large misses heavily. Calculus gives a closed form:

`slope = r * (std_y / std_x)` and `intercept = mean_y - slope * mean_x`

where `r` is the Pearson correlation. Two consequences are worth remembering. The line always passes through the point of means `(mean_x, mean_y)`. And the residuals of a least squares line with an intercept always add up to exactly 0, which is a property of the method and not a sign that the model is good.

### Interpreting coefficients

Suppose the fitted line for weekly units sold against price is `units = 195 - 3.8 * price`. The slope says that, in this data, each extra unit of price goes with 3.8 fewer units sold on average. The intercept says that at a price of 0 the model predicts 195 units, which may be meaningless if no price near 0 was ever observed. The words matter: the slope describes an *association* in the data, and "causes" is justified only if the data came from a designed experiment. Each coefficient has a **standard error** that measures how much it would vary from sample to sample, and a **p-value** for the null hypothesis that the true slope is 0 (Day 90). A confidence interval, such as `-4.17 to -3.41`, gives a range of plausible values for the true slope.

With more than one predictor the model is `y = b0 + b1*x1 + b2*x2`. Now each slope is read with "**holding the other predictors constant**": `b1` is the expected change in `y` for a one-unit rise in `x1` when `x2` stays the same. This matters when the predictors are themselves related. If higher-priced products also get more advertising, the simple regression on price mixes the effect of price and the effect of advertising, and its slope can be quite different from the slope for price once advertising is in the model.

### R-squared

**R-squared** (`R²`) is the share of the variation in `y` that the model accounts for. It compares the leftover squared error to the total squared variation around the mean:

`R² = 1 - SSE / SST`

where `SSE` is the sum of squared residuals and `SST` is the sum of squared distances of `y` from its mean. With one predictor `R²` equals `r²`. It runs from 0 (the model predicts no better than the mean) to 1 (a perfect fit). What it does not mean: it does not show that the model is correct, that the relationship is causal, or that predictions are accurate in the units you care about (the typical residual size does that). A high `R²` can come from a curved relationship fitted by a line, and a low `R²` can still go with a real, useful slope when the outcome is noisy. Adding predictors never lowers `R²` on the data it was fitted to, so a larger `R²` after adding a variable is not by itself proof that the variable matters.

### Residuals and assumptions

A regression line is trusted only if the residuals look like patternless noise. The usual assumptions are these.

- **Linearity**: the relationship is roughly a straight line. If not, the residuals show a curve: positive at both ends and negative in the middle, or the reverse.
- **Independence**: one row's error does not tell you another's. Time series and repeated measurements often break this.
- **Constant spread** (homoscedasticity): the residuals are about equally spread at all fitted values. A funnel shape means they are not.
- **Roughly normal residuals**: matters mainly for p-values and intervals with small samples.

You check them by plotting residuals against fitted values and looking for pattern. Where plots are not available you can summarise the same thing in numbers: the mean residual in the left, middle and right thirds of `x`, or the correlation of the residuals with the fitted values. A last warning is **extrapolation**: predicting outside the range of observed `x` assumes the line continues. A model of units against price fitted on prices from 10 to 30 can predict negative units at a price of 100, which is impossible.

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Regression turns a pattern into a quantity a business can use, such as "each extra 1,000 of advertising goes with about 1,200 more sales", and it is the basis of forecasting and many later modelling methods. The common misconception is that a large `R²` means a good model and a significant slope means a causal effect. Neither follows. Look at the residuals and ask how the data were collected.

## Syntax and key functions

```python
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

slope, intercept = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)          # degree 1 = straight line

X = sm.add_constant(x)                          # adds the intercept column of ones
model = sm.OLS(y, X).fit()
model.params                                    # intercept first, then slopes
model.rsquared                                  # R-squared
model.resid                                     # residuals
model.fittedvalues                              # predicted y for the data
model.bse, model.pvalues                        # standard errors and p-values
model.conf_int()                                # 95% confidence intervals
model.predict(new_X)                            # predictions for new rows
```

| Item | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `np.polyfit(x, y, 1)` | Returns `[slope, intercept]` for the least squares line. |
| `sm.add_constant(X)` | Adds the column that lets the model have an intercept. |
| `sm.OLS(y, X).fit()` | Fits the model; note that `y` comes first. |
| `params`, `rsquared`, `resid` | Coefficients, R-squared and residuals. |
| `conf_int()` | Confidence intervals for the coefficients. |
| Residual | Actual `y` minus predicted `y`. |

Printing `model.summary()` gives a long table whose layout changes between versions, so these lessons print rounded numbers instead.

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Least squares by hand

Using the six advertising and sales pairs from Day 101.

| x | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | 2 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 7 | 9 |

```python run
import numpy as np

x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], dtype=float)
y = np.array([2, 4, 5, 4, 7, 9], dtype=float)
r = np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]
slope = r * y.std(ddof=1) / x.std(ddof=1)
intercept = y.mean() - slope * x.mean()
print(round(slope, 4), round(intercept, 4))
print([round(float(v), 4) for v in np.polyfit(x, y, 1)])

def sse(a, b):
    return float(((y - (a + b * x)) ** 2).sum())

print(round(sse(intercept, slope), 3), round(sse(0.5, 1.3), 3), round(sse(1.0, 1.2), 3))
```

```output
1.2286 0.8667
[1.2286, 0.8667]
4.419 4.59 4.44
```

The slope is 1.2286 and the intercept 0.8667: each extra thousand of spend goes with about 1.23 more thousand of sales. `polyfit` gives the same numbers. The sum of squared residuals for this line is 4.419, and any other line, such as `0.5 + 1.3x` (SSE 4.59) or `1.0 + 1.2x` (SSE 4.44), is worse; that is what "least squares" means.

### Example 2: statsmodels coefficients and R-squared

```python run
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], dtype=float)
y = np.array([2, 4, 5, 4, 7, 9], dtype=float)
model = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit()
print(model.params.round(3).tolist())
print(round(model.rsquared, 4), round(float(np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]) ** 2, 4))
print(model.bse.round(3).tolist(), model.pvalues.round(4).tolist())
print(round(float(model.predict([[1.0, 7.0]])[0]), 3))
```

```output
[0.867, 1.229]
0.8567 0.8567
[0.978, 0.251] [0.4258, 0.0081]
9.467
```

The parameters are listed intercept first, then slope: `[0.867, 1.229]`. `R²` is 0.8567, equal to `r²` for one predictor, so the line accounts for about 86% of the variation of sales in this sample. The standard error of the slope is 0.251 and its p-value 0.0081. The prediction at `x = 7` (just outside the observed range 1 to 6) is 9.467, which is only reliable if the line keeps going.

### Example 3: Residuals

```python run
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], dtype=float)
y = np.array([2, 4, 5, 4, 7, 9], dtype=float)
model = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit()
res = model.resid
print(res.round(3).tolist())
print(round(abs(float(res.sum())), 6))
sse = float((res ** 2).sum())
sst = float(((y - y.mean()) ** 2).sum())
print(round(sse, 3), round(sst, 3), round(1 - sse / sst, 4))
print(int(np.argmax(np.abs(res))) + 1)
```

```output
[-0.095, 0.676, 0.448, -1.781, -0.01, 0.762]
0.0
4.419 30.833 0.8567
4
```

The residuals are -0.095, 0.676, 0.448, -1.781, -0.01 and 0.762. They add up to 0 (a property of least squares). `SSE` is 4.419, `SST` 30.833, and `1 - SSE/SST` gives the same `R²` of 0.8567. The largest miss is the fourth point (a residual of -1.781): the model expected sales of about 5.8 and the actual was 4.

### Example 4: Interpreting a fitted model, and extrapolation

A simulated price test: 60 weeks with price between 10 and 30, and units sold falling by 4 per unit of price, plus noise.

```python run
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
price = rng.random(60) * 20 + 10
units = 200 - 4 * price + rng.normal(0, 10, 60)
model = sm.OLS(units, sm.add_constant(price)).fit()
print(model.params.round(2).tolist(), round(model.rsquared, 3))
print(model.conf_int().round(2).tolist())
print(round(float(model.predict([[1.0, 20.0]])[0]), 1), round(float(model.predict([[1.0, 100.0]])[0]), 1))
```

```output
[194.91, -3.79] 0.873
[[187.08, 202.74], [-4.17, -3.41]]
119.1 -184.3
```

The fitted line is `units = 194.91 - 3.79 * price` with `R²` of 0.873. The true slope used to build the data was -4, and the 95% confidence interval for the slope, -4.17 to -3.41, does contain it. Each extra unit of price goes with about 3.8 fewer units sold. At a price of 20 the prediction is 119.1 units, a sensible value inside the observed price range. At a price of 100, far beyond the data, the line predicts -184.3 units, which is impossible: that is extrapolation.

### Example 5: Two predictors and "holding constant"

Advertising rises with price in this simulation, so ignoring it distorts the price slope.

```python run
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
price = rng.random(60) * 20 + 10
_ = rng.normal(0, 10, 60)  # unused draw: keeps the random stream as in Example 4
ad = 0.5 * price + rng.normal(0, 3, 60)
units = 100 - 4 * price + 3 * ad + rng.normal(0, 10, 60)

simple = sm.OLS(units, sm.add_constant(price)).fit()
both = sm.OLS(units, sm.add_constant(np.column_stack([price, ad]))).fit()
print(simple.params.round(2).tolist())
print(both.params.round(2).tolist())
print(round(simple.rsquared, 3), round(both.rsquared, 3))
```

```output
[100.86, -2.67]
[103.44, -3.94, 2.46]
0.648 0.768
```

With price alone the slope is -2.67, because higher prices came with more advertising, which pushes units up and hides part of the price effect. With both predictors the price slope is -3.94 (close to the true -4) and the advertising slope is 2.46 (the true value is 3, and the estimate is noisy with 60 rows). The price coefficient now reads "units change by -3.94 for one unit of price, holding advertising constant". `R²` rises from 0.648 to 0.768, and it can never fall when a variable is added.

### Example 6: Residuals reveal a curve

A straight line fitted to `y = x²` for `x` from 0 to 10 has a high `R²`, yet the residuals show the problem.

```python run
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

x = np.linspace(0, 10, 30)
y = x ** 2
model = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit()
print(round(model.rsquared, 3))
thirds = np.array_split(model.resid, 3)
print([round(float(t.mean()), 2) for t in thirds])
```

```output
0.934
[3.96, -7.93, 3.96]
```

`R²` is 0.934, which sounds excellent, but the mean residual is positive in the left third (3.96), strongly negative in the middle (-7.93) and positive again on the right (3.96). That U shape means the line is systematically wrong in each region: a curve, not a line, is needed. A high `R²` does not rule out a wrong model, so always look at the residuals.

## Real-world applications

- **Forecasting a simple trend.** Regress monthly sales on month number to estimate growth per month, with a confidence interval to show the uncertainty (Examples 2 and 4).
- **Price and promotion analysis.** Estimating price sensitivity needs the other drivers in the model (advertising here), otherwise the price slope is biased (Example 5).
- **Quality checks of a model.** Residual summaries per segment show where a model is systematically off, such as under-predicting large orders (Example 6).
- **Setting expectations.** The residual size and the range of observed `x` tell a manager how far to trust a prediction, and where it would be extrapolation (Example 4).

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Least squares chooses the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals: `slope = r * std_y / std_x`, `intercept = mean_y - slope * mean_x`.
- The slope is the change in predicted `y` per unit of `x`; with several predictors it is read "holding the others constant".
- `R² = 1 - SSE/SST` is the share of variation accounted for; it is not proof of a correct or causal model.
- Residuals should look like patternless noise; a curve or funnel signals a problem.
- Do not extrapolate beyond the observed range of the predictors.

### Common mistakes

- Reading the intercept literally when `x = 0` is outside the data.
- Calling the slope an effect and claiming causation from observational data.
- Trusting a high `R²` without looking at residuals.
- Passing `X` before `y` to `sm.OLS`, or forgetting `sm.add_constant`, which fits a line through the origin.
- Ignoring related predictors, so the coefficient of the one you looked at is biased.
- Predicting far outside the observed range and reporting impossible values.

### Revision notes

- `np.polyfit(x, y, 1)` gives `[slope, intercept]`; `sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit()`.
- `model.params`, `model.rsquared`, `model.resid`, `model.conf_int()`.
- Residual = actual - predicted; their sum is 0; check thirds or fitted values for patterns.
- `R²` equals `r²` with one predictor.
