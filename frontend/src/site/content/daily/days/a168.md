## Objectives

- Answer the standard Excel interview questions (lookups, conditional sums, PivotTables) with a formula, a one-sentence reason and a check.
- Answer the standard Power BI interview questions on measures, `CALCULATE`, filter context, and model design, with the DAX text and the logic behind it.
- Reproduce every number in an answer with pandas, so you can say what the result should be before you show it.
- Structure a spoken answer in three parts: what it does, why that choice, what could go wrong.

## Understand

### What these interviews ask

Analyst interviews for Excel and Power BI roles are usually practical. You may share a screen and build a lookup or a PivotTable in ten minutes, or you may be asked to explain a formula you wrote on a take-home task. The questions cluster in four groups. **Lookups**: which function and why (`VLOOKUP`, `XLOOKUP`, `INDEX`/`MATCH`), exact versus approximate match, what happens when the key is missing. **Conditional summaries**: `SUMIFS`, `COUNTIFS`, `AVERAGEIFS`, and the same questions in a PivotTable. **DAX and measures**: the difference between a measure and a calculated column, `CALCULATE`, filter context, percent of total, year to date. **Model design**: star schema, relationship cardinality and direction, a date table, and why two visuals show different totals. In each group a good answer has the formula, the reason you chose it over the alternative, and the failure you would check for.

### Lookups

`VLOOKUP(value, table, column_number, FALSE)` searches the first column of a table for `value` and returns the entry from the given column of the same row. The last argument matters: `FALSE` is an exact match, and `TRUE` (or omitted) is an approximate match that finds the largest value less than or equal to the lookup value and **requires the first column to be sorted in ascending order**. Approximate match is the right tool for tiers, such as a commission rate by sales band. `VLOOKUP` has two weaknesses interviewers like to hear: the column number breaks when a column is inserted, and it can only look to the right. `XLOOKUP(value, lookup_array, return_array, "Not found")` uses separate lookup and return ranges, defaults to exact match, can look left, and has a built-in message for missing keys. `INDEX(return_range, MATCH(value, lookup_range, 0))` does the same in every version of Excel. In pandas the exact match is a left `merge`, and the approximate match is `pd.merge_asof` on sorted keys.

### Conditional sums and PivotTables

`SUMIFS(sum_range, criteria_range1, criteria1, ...)` adds the values that meet all the criteria, and `COUNTIFS` and `AVERAGEIFS` count and average them. A PivotTable answers the same question for every combination at once and can show values as a percentage of the grand total, a row or a column. The usual follow-up is "how would you check the PivotTable?": compare its grand total with a `SUM` of the source column, and a cell with a `SUMIFS`. Another is "why does the PivotTable count instead of sum?": a column that contains text or blanks defaults to Count.

### Measures, CALCULATE and filter context

A **measure** is a calculation evaluated at report time for whatever the user has filtered, such as total revenue; a **calculated column** is computed row by row at refresh and stored in the table. Revenue in the sample model needs `quantity * unit_price` per row, so the measure is `Total Revenue = SUMX(Sales, Sales[quantity] * Sales[unit_price])`. **Filter context** is the set of filters in force for a cell of a visual: the country on an axis, the slicer selection, the row of a matrix. `CALCULATE(expression, filter)` evaluates an expression in a modified filter context: `Revenue UK = CALCULATE([Total Revenue], Customers[country] = "UK")` replaces any country filter with UK. `ALL(table)` removes filters, which gives the denominator of a percentage: `Revenue % of All = DIVIDE([Total Revenue], CALCULATE([Total Revenue], ALL(Customers)))`. `DIVIDE` returns a blank instead of an error when the denominator is zero. `TOTALYTD([Total Revenue], 'Calendar'[date])` accumulates from the start of the year and needs a proper date table. Explain each in terms of filter context, and say what a visual would show before you show it.

### Model questions

The classic question is "why do two visuals show different totals?". Work through a checklist: duplicate keys in a dimension (a join multiplies rows), orphan keys in the fact (rows appear under a blank), a relationship with the wrong direction or an inactive relationship, a measure that ignores a filter, a date table with gaps, or a partial load. Another is "star schema or flat table?": star, because dimensions are small and unique, facts are narrow, filters flow one way and fixing an attribute is one change.

### How to structure a spoken answer

Give the answer first, then the reason, then the risk. "I'd use `XLOOKUP` because it defaults to an exact match and does not break when columns move; the risk is missing keys, so I'd supply a not-found message and count the misses." Verify your claims against data when you can: in a take-home task, add a check cell that reconciles a total.

### Misconceptions and why it matters

A frequent misconception is that a PivotTable and `SUMIFS` can disagree because one is "wrong"; they differ because of filters, blanks or text numbers. Another is that `CALCULATE` adds a filter: it modifies the existing context, and a filter on the same column replaces the old one. Precise explanations, backed by a number you checked, are what separate a confident answer from a memorised one.

## Syntax and key functions

```excel
=VLOOKUP(E2, $A$2:$C$13, 3, FALSE)
=XLOOKUP(E2, $A$2:$A$13, $C$2:$C$13, "Not found")
=INDEX($C$2:$C$13, MATCH(E2, $A$2:$A$13, 0))
=SUMIFS(D:D, B:B, "UK", C:C, "Furniture")
=COUNTIFS(B:B, "UK", D:D, ">500")
```

```dax
Total Revenue = SUMX ( Sales, Sales[quantity] * Sales[unit_price] )
Revenue UK = CALCULATE ( [Total Revenue], Customers[country] = "UK" )
Revenue % of All = DIVIDE ( [Total Revenue], CALCULATE ( [Total Revenue], ALL ( Customers ) ) )
Average Order Value = DIVIDE ( [Total Revenue], DISTINCTCOUNT ( Sales[order_id] ) )
Revenue YTD = TOTALYTD ( [Total Revenue], 'Calendar'[date] )
```

| Question | Excel or Power BI answer | pandas check |
|---|---|---|
| Exact lookup | `XLOOKUP` / `VLOOKUP(..., FALSE)` | `merge(how="left")` |
| Tier lookup | `VLOOKUP(..., TRUE)` on sorted tiers | `pd.merge_asof` |
| Conditional sum | `SUMIFS` | boolean mask and `sum` |
| Percent of total | Pivot "% of Grand Total" / `ALL` | `value / total` |
| Year to date | `TOTALYTD` | `groupby(...).cumsum()` |
| Different totals | Check keys, direction, filters | `is_unique`, `isin` |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Exact and approximate lookups

Five sales reps get a commission rate from a tier table by approximate match: the largest tier minimum not above their sales. The ones at 9,800 and 24,999 test the boundaries. `merge_asof` is the pandas twin of `VLOOKUP(..., TRUE)`: both need the tiers sorted ascending. The exact lookup is a left merge, and a missing key gives `NaN`, which Excel shows as `#N/A`.

```python run
import pandas as pd

reps = pd.DataFrame({"rep": ["Ana", "Ben", "Cho", "Dev", "Eli"], "sales": [9800, 10000, 24999, 51000, 30250]})
tiers = pd.DataFrame({"min_sales": [0, 10000, 25000, 50000], "rate": [0.00, 0.02, 0.04, 0.06]})
out = pd.merge_asof(reps.sort_values("sales"), tiers, left_on="sales", right_on="min_sales").sort_values("rep")
out["commission"] = (out["sales"] * out["rate"]).round(2)
print(out[["rep", "sales", "rate", "commission"]].to_string(index=False))

lookup = pd.DataFrame({"rep": ["Ana", "Zoe"]}).merge(reps, on="rep", how="left")
print("exact lookup misses:", int(lookup["sales"].isna().sum()), "of", len(lookup))
```

```output
rep  sales  rate  commission
Ana   9800  0.00        0.00
Ben  10000  0.02      200.00
Cho  24999  0.02      499.98
Dev  51000  0.06     3060.00
Eli  30250  0.04     1210.00
exact lookup misses: 1 of 2
```

### Example 2: SUMIFS, COUNTIFS and AVERAGEIFS

Question: what is the revenue of UK customers for Furniture, how many such orders over 500 were there, and what is their average? Each is a boolean mask in pandas, the same as the criteria in the formulas. The check at the end compares a pivot-style total with the plain `SUM`.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd


def make_model():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    customers = pd.DataFrame({
        "customer_id": np.arange(1, 41),
        "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
        "country": rng.choice(["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"], size=40),
        "segment": rng.choice(["Consumer", "Corporate", "Small Business"], size=40, p=[0.5, 0.3, 0.2]),
    })
    names = ["Desk", "Chair", "Bookcase", "Laptop", "Monitor", "Phone", "Notebook", "Pen Set", "Stapler", "Printer", "Lamp", "Cabinet"]
    categories = ["Furniture", "Furniture", "Furniture", "Technology", "Technology", "Technology",
                  "Office Supplies", "Office Supplies", "Office Supplies", "Technology", "Furniture", "Furniture"]
    products = pd.DataFrame({
        "product_id": np.arange(1, 13),
        "product_name": names,
        "category": categories,
        "unit_cost": rng.integers(5, 120, size=12),
    })
    sales = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1401),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, 400), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, 400),
        "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, 400),
        "quantity": rng.integers(1, 8, 400),
    })
    cost = products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]
    sales["unit_price"] = (cost[sales["product_id"]].to_numpy() * (1.3 + 0.5 * rng.random(400))).round(2)
    sales = sales.sort_values(["order_date", "order_id"]).reset_index(drop=True)
    dates = pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31", freq="D")
    calendar = pd.DataFrame({
        "date": dates,
        "year": dates.year,
        "quarter": dates.quarter,
        "month_number": dates.month,
        "month_name": dates.strftime("%B"),
    })
    return sales, customers, products, calendar


def revenue_table():
    sales, customers, products, calendar = make_model()
    sales = sales.assign(revenue=sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"])
    return sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id").merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date")


t = revenue_table()
mask = (t["country"] == "UK") & (t["category"] == "Furniture")
print("SUMIFS:", round(float(t.loc[mask, "revenue"].sum()), 2))
big = mask & (t["revenue"] > 500)
print("COUNTIFS:", int(big.sum()), "| AVERAGEIFS:", round(float(t.loc[big, "revenue"].mean()), 2))
pivot = t.pivot_table(index="country", columns="category", values="revenue", aggfunc="sum", margins=True, margins_name="Total")
print("pivot grand total equals SUM:", round(float(pivot.loc["Total", "Total"]), 2) == round(float(t["revenue"].sum()), 2))
print("UK Furniture cell equals SUMIFS:", round(float(pivot.loc["UK", "Furniture"]), 2) == round(float(t.loc[mask, "revenue"].sum()), 2))
```

```output
SUMIFS: 6326.99
COUNTIFS: 1 | AVERAGEIFS: 790.5
pivot grand total equals SUM: True
UK Furniture cell equals SUMIFS: True
```

### Example 6: Why do two visuals disagree?

A visual by country shows less than the card for total revenue. The checklist finds the cause: some sales rows have a customer id missing from the customer table, which shows as a blank country. The orphan count and the blank revenue explain the whole gap.

## Real-world applications

- **Take-home tasks.** Employers send a workbook or a model and ask for a report; checking numbers with a second method (Example 2's pivot against `SUMIFS`) is the habit they look for.
- **Commission and pricing tiers.** Approximate-match lookups (Example 1) drive rate tables, discount bands and tax brackets in finance workbooks.
- **Model review.** The disagreement checklist (Example 6) is the first thing a Power BI developer runs when a business user says "your total is wrong".
- **Executive KPIs.** Percent of total and year to date (Examples 3 to 5) are the two measures most dashboards are built from.

### Key takeaways

- Answer in three parts: what it does, why that choice, what could go wrong.
- Exact lookup is `XLOOKUP` or `VLOOKUP(..., FALSE)`; tiers use approximate match on sorted data.
- `SUMIFS` and a PivotTable should reconcile with `SUM` of the source.
- `CALCULATE` modifies filter context; `ALL` removes filters for the denominator; `DIVIDE` avoids errors.
- When totals disagree, check keys, orphans, relationships, filters and the date table.

### Common mistakes

- Using approximate match on an unsorted table. Symptom: wrong tier for some values. Fix: sort ascending or use exact match.
- A hard-coded `VLOOKUP` column number. Symptom: wrong values after a column is inserted. Fix: `XLOOKUP` or `INDEX`/`MATCH`.
- Explaining `CALCULATE` as "adds a filter". Symptom: confusion when two filters on one column interact. Fix: it replaces filters on the columns it names.
- Quoting a number you did not check. Symptom: an answer contradicted by the data. Fix: reproduce it with a second method first.

### Revision notes

- Lookups: exact, approximate (sorted), `XLOOKUP`, `INDEX`/`MATCH`.
- `SUMIFS`, `COUNTIFS`, `AVERAGEIFS` = boolean masks.
- Measures: `SUMX`, `CALCULATE`, `ALL`, `DIVIDE`, `DISTINCTCOUNT`, `TOTALYTD`.
- Checklist: duplicate keys, orphans, direction, filters, date table.
