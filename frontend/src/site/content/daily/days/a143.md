## Objectives

- Define a metric precisely enough that two analysts compute the same number, and show how a vague definition changes the result.
- Build a metric tree as a nested dictionary and check that its parts multiply or add up to the top metric.
- Attribute a change in a top metric to the drivers in its tree.
- Distinguish leading from lagging indicators and test whether an indicator leads.
- Recognise vanity metrics and replace them with actionable ones.

## Understand

### A metric is a definition, not a number

A **metric** is a number computed by a stated rule, and the rule is the metric. "Active customers" is not a metric until you say what counts. Is a customer active if they placed any order in the month, or at least two? Over a calendar month or the last 30 days? Are cancelled orders included? Each choice gives a different number, and teams that skip this step spend meetings arguing about whose number is right. A complete definition has five parts: the **numerator** (what is counted or summed), the **denominator** if it is a ratio, the **time window** (a calendar month, a rolling 30 days, a year to date), the **grain** (per customer, per order, per day) and the **filters** (which rows are in or out). Day 140 added a target, owner and direction to make a metric a KPI; this lesson goes one level deeper and asks where a metric comes from and how metrics relate to each other.

Write the definition in one place, as code or a data dictionary entry, and reuse it. When the same metric is computed in three dashboards by three people, it will be computed three ways, and the first discrepancy costs more than writing the definition would have.

### Metric trees

A **metric tree** (or driver tree) breaks a top-level metric into the metrics that produce it. Revenue is orders times average order value. Orders is customers times orders per customer. Average order value is units per order times price per unit. Each split is either a product (multiplicative) or a sum (additive), and the parts should be **mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive**: no overlap and nothing missing, so that the pieces really rebuild the parent. The tree matters for two reasons. It shows which levers exist, because a leaf such as price per unit can be acted on by someone, whereas "revenue" cannot. And it explains movement: when revenue changes, the tree shows which branch moved. A tree is verified by arithmetic: multiply the leaves and compare with the top value. In pandas a tree is a nested dictionary and its check is one line, which is the approach used in the examples.

### Attributing a change to drivers

To explain why revenue changed between two years, compute the tree for each year and replace the drivers one at a time. Start with the earlier year's value, substitute the later year's customers and record how much the top metric moves, then substitute orders per customer, then units per order, then price per unit. The four moves add up exactly to the total change. Be aware that for a product of drivers the split depends on the **order** of substitution when two drivers change together, because their joint effect has to be assigned to one of them. State the order, keep it fixed in a report, and treat small differences between orders as noise. When one driver explains almost everything, the order hardly matters, and that is the usual case worth reporting.

### Leading and lagging indicators

A **lagging indicator** reports an outcome after it has happened: revenue, churn, profit. It is accurate and cannot be influenced any more. A **leading indicator** moves before the outcome and can be acted on: qualified leads before sales, trial sign-ups before paying customers, support backlog before customer churn. Leading indicators are valuable because there is still time to react, but a claimed leading indicator must be tested and not assumed. The simple test is to compare the indicator in one period with the outcome in the next: if the correlation between this month's value and next month's outcome is not clearly better than the same-month relationship, the metric is concurrent, not leading. A correlation is not proof of cause, and with twelve points it is weak evidence; use it to reject weak candidates and to choose what to examine further.

### Vanity and actionable metrics

A **vanity metric** looks good and does not guide decisions. The classic examples are cumulative counts: total registered customers or total orders ever placed can only rise, so they flatter the team whatever happens. Page views and followers are vanity when no one can say what action would follow if they fell. An **actionable metric** is tied to a decision, can move down as well as up, and is comparable across periods: monthly active customers, orders per active customer, share of revenue from repeat customers. A test for any metric is "what would we do differently if this halved?" If the answer is "nothing", it is not a KPI. Beware also that a metric used as a target tends to be gamed: when people are rewarded for a number, they optimise the number and not the goal it stood for. Pair a metric with a check that would reveal gaming, such as revenue with returns.

### Why it matters

Business analytics starts with a shared language. A metric tree gives a team one picture of how the business makes money, a definition keeps the number stable, and the leading/lagging and vanity tests keep the dashboard honest. The misconception is that more metrics mean more insight; a small, defined, connected set that explains the top number is worth more than a long list.

## Syntax and key functions

Metric trees are built with ordinary Python and pandas. The reference lists the standard drivers for a sales business and the checks.

| Item | Definition or check |
|---|---|
| Revenue | `orders * average order value` |
| Orders | `customers * orders per customer` |
| Average order value | `units per order * price per unit` |
| Tree check | The product of the leaves equals the top metric |
| Sequential substitution | Replace drivers one at a time in a fixed order; contributions sum to the change |
| Lag test | `x.corr(y.shift(-1))` against `x.corr(y)` |

A tree as a nested dictionary:

```python
tree = {"revenue": {"orders": {"customers": 40, "orders_per_customer": 10},
                    "aov": {"units_per_order": 3.0, "price_per_unit": 55.85}}}
```

## Learn by example

### Example 1: A definition changes the number

Count active customers per month with two definitions.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
countries = ["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"]
cats = ["Hardware", "Software", "Services"]
customers = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": range(1, 41),
    "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
    "country": [countries[i % 4] for i in range(40)],
    "segment": [["Retail", "Corporate"][(i // 4) % 2] for i in range(40)],
})
products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, 13),
    "product_name": [f"Product {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)],
    "category": [cats[i % 3] for i in range(12)],
    "unit_cost": [float(10 + 5 * i) for i in range(12)],
})
n = 400
sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n + 1),
    "order_date": pd.to_datetime("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, n),
    "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, n),
    "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
})
sales["unit_price"] = (sales["product_id"].map(products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]) * 1.5).round(2)
calendar = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31")})
calendar["year"] = calendar["date"].dt.year
calendar["quarter"] = calendar["date"].dt.quarter
calendar["month_number"] = calendar["date"].dt.month
calendar["month_name"] = calendar["date"].dt.strftime("%B")
sales["revenue"] = sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"]
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
fact["month"] = fact["order_date"].dt.to_period("M")
per = fact.groupby(["month", "customer_id"])["order_id"].nunique().reset_index(name="orders")
any_order = per.groupby("month")["customer_id"].nunique()
two_plus = per[per["orders"] >= 2].groupby("month")["customer_id"].nunique()
print(pd.DataFrame({"any_order": any_order, "two_or_more": two_plus}).head(4).to_string())
print(int(any_order.max()), int(two_plus.max()))
```

```output
         any_order  two_or_more
month                          
2025-01         20            9
2025-02         19            8
2025-03         19            9
2025-04         19            8
29 10
```

In January 20 customers have at least one order, but only 9 have two or more; the monthly maximum is 29 against 10. A report that says "active customers" without the rule could show either, and a quarterly comparison that switches rule halfway would show a fake collapse. The definition belongs next to the metric.

### Example 2: A metric tree as a nested dictionary

Build the revenue tree for 2025 and check that its leaves multiply back to revenue.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
countries = ["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"]
cats = ["Hardware", "Software", "Services"]
customers = pd.DataFrame({

    "country": [countries[i % 4] for i in range(40)],
    "segment": [["Retail", "Corporate"][(i // 4) % 2] for i in range(40)],
})
products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, 13),
    "product_name": [f"Product {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)],
    "category": [cats[i % 3] for i in range(12)],
    "unit_cost": [float(10 + 5 * i) for i in range(12)],
})
n = 400
sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n + 1),
    "order_date": pd.to_datetime("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, n),
    "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, n),
    "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
})
sales["unit_price"] = (sales["product_id"].map(products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]) * 1.5).round(2)
calendar = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31")})
calendar["year"] = calendar["date"].dt.year
calendar["quarter"] = calendar["date"].dt.quarter
calendar["month_number"] = calendar["date"].dt.month
calendar["month_name"] = calendar["date"].dt.strftime("%B")
sales["revenue"] = sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"]
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
customers_n = fact["customer_id"].nunique()
orders_n = fact["order_id"].nunique()
units = fact["quantity"].sum()
revenue = fact["revenue"].sum()
tree = {
    "revenue": {
        "orders": {"customers": customers_n, "orders_per_customer": orders_n / customers_n},
        "average_order_value": {"units_per_order": units / orders_n, "price_per_unit": revenue / units},
    }
}

def show(node, depth=0):
    for key, value in node.items():
        if isinstance(value, dict):
            print("  " * depth + key)
            show(value, depth + 1)
        else:
            print("  " * depth + f"{key}: {value:.4f}")

show(tree)
leaves = [tree["revenue"]["orders"]["customers"], tree["revenue"]["orders"]["orders_per_customer"],
          tree["revenue"]["average_order_value"]["units_per_order"], tree["revenue"]["average_order_value"]["price_per_unit"]]
product = 1.0
for v in leaves:
    product *= float(v)
print(round(product, 2), float(revenue))
```

```output
revenue
  orders
    customers: 40.0000
    orders_per_customer: 10.0000
  average_order_value
    units_per_order: 3.0050
    price_per_unit: 55.8507
67132.5 67132.5
```

The tree reads: 40 customers placing 10 orders each, with 3.0050 units per order at 55.8507 per unit. The four leaves multiply to 67,132.50, the revenue, so the split is exhaustive: nothing is missing and nothing is counted twice. A tree whose product does not equal the top metric is wrong, whatever it looks like.

### Example 3: Attributing the change in revenue to drivers

Compute the same drivers for 2024 (the extension from Day 131) and substitute them one at a time.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
countries = ["UK", "Germany", "France", "Spain"]
cats = ["Hardware", "Software", "Services"]
customers = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": range(1, 41),
    "customer_name": [f"Customer {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 41)],
    "country": [countries[i % 4] for i in range(40)],
    "segment": [["Retail", "Corporate"][(i // 4) % 2] for i in range(40)],
})
products = pd.DataFrame({
    "product_id": range(1, 13),
    "product_name": [f"Product {i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)],
    "category": [cats[i % 3] for i in range(12)],
    "unit_cost": [float(10 + 5 * i) for i in range(12)],
})
n = 400
sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id": range(1, n + 1),
    "order_date": pd.to_datetime("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 365, n), unit="D"),
    "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 41, n),
    "product_id": rng.integers(1, 13, n),
    "quantity": rng.integers(1, 6, n),
})
sales["unit_price"] = (sales["product_id"].map(products.set_index("product_id")["unit_cost"]) * 1.5).round(2)
calendar = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-12-31")})
calendar["year"] = calendar["date"].dt.year
calendar["quarter"] = calendar["date"].dt.quarter
calendar["month_number"] = calendar["date"].dt.month
calendar["month_name"] = calendar["date"].dt.strftime("%B")
sales["revenue"] = sales["quantity"] * sales["unit_price"]
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
s24 = sales.copy()
s24["order_date"] = s24["order_date"] - pd.DateOffset(years=1)
s24["quantity"] = (s24["quantity"] + 1) // 2
s24["revenue"] = s24["quantity"] * s24["unit_price"]
both = pd.concat([s24, sales], ignore_index=True)
monthly = both.groupby(both["order_date"].dt.to_period("M"))["revenue"].sum()
f24 = s24.merge(customers, on="customer_id")

def drivers(df):
    o = df["order_id"].nunique()
    c = df["customer_id"].nunique()
    u = df["quantity"].sum()
    r = df["revenue"].sum()
    return {"customers": c, "orders_per_customer": o / c, "units_per_order": u / o, "price_per_unit": r / u}

d24, d25 = drivers(f24), drivers(fact)

def attribute(order):
    cur = dict(d24)
    prev = 1.0
    for v in cur.values():
        prev *= v
    out = {}
    for k in order:
        cur[k] = d25[k]
        now = 1.0
        for v in cur.values():
            now *= v
        out[k] = round(float(now - prev), 2)
        prev = now
    return out

a = attribute(["customers", "orders_per_customer", "units_per_order", "price_per_unit"])
b = attribute(["customers", "orders_per_customer", "price_per_unit", "units_per_order"])
print(a)
print(b)
print(round(sum(a.values()), 2), round(float(fact["revenue"].sum() - f24["revenue"].sum()), 2))
```

```output
{'customers': 0.0, 'orders_per_customer': 0.0, 'units_per_order': 26563.55, 'price_per_unit': 53.95}
{'customers': 0.0, 'orders_per_customer': 0.0, 'price_per_unit': 32.58, 'units_per_order': 26584.92}
26617.5 26617.5
```

Revenue rose by 26,617.50. Customers and orders per customer did not change (40 customers and 10 orders each in both years), so their contribution is 0. Almost all of the change, 26,563.55, comes from units per order, and 53.95 from price per unit: the growth is basket size and not demand or price. With price per unit substituted before units per order, the split is 32.58 for price and 26,584.92 for units per order: price gets 32.58 instead of 53.95 because the joint effect of the two drivers is now assigned to units per order. Both splits add up to 26,617.50 and units per order explains more than 99.8% of the change either way, which is why the order matters little when one driver dominates.

### Example 4: Does the indicator lead?

Test whether monthly orders lead next month's revenue, by comparing a lag-1 and a same-month correlation.

```python run
fact = (sales.merge(customers, on="customer_id").merge(products, on="product_id")
        .merge(calendar, left_on="order_date", right_on="date"))
fact["month"] = fact["order_date"].dt.to_period("M")
orders = fact.groupby("month")["order_id"].nunique()
revenue = fact.groupby("month")["revenue"].sum()
print(round(float(orders.corr(revenue)), 3), round(float(orders.corr(revenue.shift(-1))), 3))
print("leading" if orders.corr(revenue.shift(-1)) > orders.corr(revenue) else "concurrent")
```

```output
0.625 -0.235
concurrent
```

Orders move with the same month's revenue (correlation 0.625) but not with the next month's (-0.235), so on this data orders are a concurrent indicator, not a leading one. The data are generated randomly, so no real lead exists; the point is the method. With only twelve months even a correlation of 0.6 is weak evidence, and a candidate leading indicator should be re-tested on more history before it is trusted.

## Real-world applications

- **Metric dictionary.** A shared definition of "active customer" or "net revenue" with numerator, denominator, window, grain and filters prevents conflicting dashboards (Example 1).
- **Growth reviews.** A driver tree explains a change in revenue by customers, frequency, basket size and price, so that effort goes to the lever that moved (Examples 2 and 3).
- **Early warning.** A tested leading indicator, such as qualified leads or support backlog, gives time to act before the lagging outcome (Example 4).
- **Dashboard pruning.** Replacing cumulative totals with period metrics that can fall makes a dashboard useful for decisions (Example 5).

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- A metric is its definition: numerator, denominator, window, grain and filters.
- A metric tree splits a top metric into drivers that multiply or add up to it; check by arithmetic.
- Attribute a change by substituting drivers in a fixed order; the parts sum to the change.
- A leading indicator must be tested against the later outcome; do not assume it.
- Vanity metrics only rise; actionable metrics can fall and tie to a decision.

### Common mistakes

- Computing "active customers" differently on two dashboards without stating the rule.
- Building a tree whose parts overlap or leave a gap, so the leaves do not rebuild the top number.
- Reporting a driver split without saying that the order of substitution was chosen.
- Calling a metric leading because it sounds forward-looking, without testing it.
- Showing cumulative totals as a success metric.
