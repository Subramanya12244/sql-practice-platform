## Objectives

- Choose a chart type from the question a reader needs answered, the type of each variable and the number of categories.
- Name the visual channel (position, length, angle, area, colour) a chart uses and say how accurately readers can compare values in it.
- Spot clutter (extra slices, gridlines, 3-D effects, redundant labels) and remove it without losing information.
- Explain why a bar chart needs a zero baseline and compute how much a truncated axis exaggerates a difference.

## Understand

### A chart answers one question

A chart is a table drawn so that the eye can do part of the comparison. The reader does not see rows; they see positions, lengths, angles and colours, and from those they form a judgement such as "March is the biggest month" or "the two regions moved together". Because the judgement is the product, the first step in choosing a chart is not choosing the chart. It is writing down, in one sentence, the question the reader must answer. "Which category earns the most?" is a comparison question. "Is revenue rising?" is a trend question. "Is the spread of order sizes wide or narrow?" is a distribution question. "Do larger orders ship later?" is a relationship question. "What share of revenue does each product make?" is a composition question. Almost every business chart falls into one of these five families, and each family has a default chart: bars for comparison, lines for trend over time, histograms or box plots for distribution, scatter plots for relationship, and stacked bars or a small number of labelled shares for composition.

The default is a starting point, not a rule. The default for comparison is a bar chart, but if you have two time points per category and the reader wants to see change, a slope chart or paired dots may answer the question better. The test is always the same: cover the title and ask what a stranger would conclude in five seconds. If the conclusion is not the one you intended, change the chart, not the audience.

### Encoding: how a number becomes a mark

Encoding is the mapping from a data value to a visual property. Position along a common scale (the height of a dot on a shared axis) is the most accurate channel people have; length (the height of a bar from a shared baseline) is almost as good; angle, area and colour intensity are progressively harder to compare. Studies of graphical perception from the 1980s onward found this ordering repeatedly, and it explains common advice: readers compare bars far more precisely than pie slices, and they cannot compare two circles' areas to within a few percent. Hue (red versus blue) is good for telling categories apart but poor for ordering values; lightness or saturation is better for ordering, and still weaker than position.

The practical rule is to give the variable that matters most the strongest channel. In a scatter plot of order size against shipping days, the two numbers get x and y position, which is why scatter plots are good at showing relationships. If you also colour by category and size the points by quantity, you have used three weaker channels on top of two strong ones; each extra encoding must earn its place by answering a question the reader has.

### Clutter and data-ink

Clutter is anything on the chart that does not help the reader answer the question. Typical clutter includes heavy gridlines, a border around the plot, a legend that repeats what direct labels already say, three-dimensional bars (the depth adds no information and distorts heights), shadows, and pie charts with eight slices. The idea of data-ink, introduced by the statistician Edward Tufte, is that most of the ink on a chart should represent data. You do not need to remove everything: light gridlines help reading values, and a legend is needed when colour carries meaning. Remove an element only when you can say what question it answers and the answer is "none". A useful trick is to remove an element and see whether the message is weaker; if not, leave it out.

Sorting is the cheapest decluttering step. A bar chart of ten categories in alphabetical order forces the reader to hunt for the largest bar; the same bars sorted by value make the ranking visible instantly. Sort unless the categories have a natural order such as months, age bands or Likert scale points.

### Honest axes

A bar encodes a value by its length, so its baseline must be zero; otherwise length no longer means value. If the bars show 96 and 100 and the axis starts at 90, the first bar is 6 units tall and the second is 10, so the chart makes the second look 67 percent bigger when it is really 4 percent bigger. A line chart encodes values by position and slope, not length, so a non-zero baseline is acceptable there, as long as the axis is labelled and the change is not made to look dramatic by zooming in on noise. Other honesty problems: a log scale without saying so, a dual axis that lets you make any two series look correlated by choosing the scales, cutting off the start of a time series to hide a drop, and cumulative totals presented as if they were rates.

Never use the common phrase "the chart doesn't lie, people misread it". A chart is a set of choices, and the choices are the analyst's responsibility.

### Why it matters and the misconception

In analysis work, charts are read by people who make decisions in seconds and never see your code. The common misconception is that more visual features mean more insight. In practice the opposite holds: a chart earns trust by being easy to read and hard to misread. The rest of this phase teaches the tools (matplotlib, seaborn, Plotly) that draw charts; this day teaches what to ask the tools to draw.

## Syntax and key functions

The code in this phase is Python, but this day's reference is a decision template. Write the chart brief before the code:

```text
Question:   <one sentence the chart answers>
Variables:  <name: numeric | category | time>, ...
Family:     comparison | trend | distribution | relationship | composition
Chart:      <default for the family>
Check:      zero baseline for bars? sorted? labelled units? title states the finding?
```

The chart-choice function used in the examples has this shape:

```python
def choose_chart(family, n_categories=0, has_time_order=False):
    ...
```

| Family | Default chart | Use something else when |
|---|---|---|
| Comparison | Horizontal or vertical bar, sorted | More than about 15 categories: show the top N and group the rest |
| Trend | Line | Few time points (3 to 5): bars or a slope chart |
| Distribution | Histogram or box plot | Comparing many groups: box or violin plots side by side |
| Relationship | Scatter | Very many points: bin them (hexbin, 2-D histogram) |
| Composition | Stacked bar or 3 to 5 labelled shares | Many parts: sorted bar of shares |

| Channel | Accuracy for comparing values | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Position on a common scale | Highest | Any numeric comparison |
| Length from a baseline | High | Bars |
| Angle, slope | Medium | Trends (lines), pie slices |
| Area | Lower | Size as a secondary encoding |
| Colour hue | Lowest for values | Telling categories apart |
| Colour lightness | Low to medium | Ordered values (heatmaps) |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: A chart-choice function

The function turns the decision table into code, so the logic can be tested. It takes the question family and facts about the data, and returns the default chart with a warning when the default breaks down. This is the same reasoning you do mentally, written down so it can be checked.

```python run
def choose_chart(family, n_categories=0, has_time_order=False):
    if family == "trend" and not has_time_order:
        return "bar (trend needs ordered time; this data has none)"
    if family == "comparison":
        return "bar, sorted" if n_categories <= 15 else "bar of top 10 plus 'Other'"
    if family == "trend":
        return "line" if n_categories >= 6 else "bar or slope chart"
    if family == "distribution":
        return "histogram" if n_categories <= 1 else "box plots side by side"
    if family == "relationship":
        return "scatter"
    if family == "composition":
        return "labelled shares" if n_categories <= 5 else "sorted bar of shares"
    raise ValueError(f"unknown family: {family}")


cases = [
    ("comparison", 6, False),
    ("comparison", 40, False),
    ("trend", 12, True),
    ("trend", 4, True),
    ("composition", 8, False),
    ("distribution", 3, False),
]
for fam, n, t in cases:
    print(f"{fam:12} n={n:<3} -> {choose_chart(fam, n, t)}")
```

```output
comparison   n=6   -> bar, sorted
comparison   n=40  -> bar of top 10 plus 'Other'
trend        n=12  -> line
trend        n=4   -> bar or slope chart
composition  n=8   -> sorted bar of shares
distribution n=3   -> box plots side by side
```

### Example 2: How much does a truncated axis exaggerate?

Two bars show 96 and 100. The visible height of a bar is its value minus the axis minimum. Computing the ratio of visible heights shows the lie directly. The true ratio stays 1.04 no matter where the axis starts, but the visual ratio grows as the baseline rises.

```python run
a, b = 96, 100
print("true ratio:", round(b / a, 3))
for baseline in [0, 50, 90, 94]:
    ha, hb = a - baseline, b - baseline
    print(f"baseline {baseline:>2}: visual ratio {hb / ha:.2f}")
```

```output
true ratio: 1.042
baseline  0: visual ratio 1.04
baseline 50: visual ratio 1.09
baseline 90: visual ratio 1.67
baseline 94: visual ratio 3.00
```

### Example 3: Sorting and grouping a bar chart (a local matplotlib check)

This example needs matplotlib, which the browser cannot load, so it is a `python local` example: run it on your own computer after `pip install matplotlib`. It draws a bar chart of revenue by category, sorted, with a zero baseline, and prints properties of the figure instead of showing it. Printing properties is how this course tests charts: the title, the axis label, the number of bars and their heights are facts you can check.

```python local
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import pandas as pd

sales = pd.DataFrame({
    "category": ["Toys", "Home", "Beauty", "Sports", "Electronics"],
    "revenue": [12400, 31800, 20900, 18700, 45200],
})
ordered = sales.sort_values("revenue", ascending=False)

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.bar(ordered["category"], ordered["revenue"])
ax.set_title("Electronics earns the most revenue")
ax.set_ylabel("Revenue (GBP)")
ax.set_ylim(bottom=0)

print("title:", ax.get_title())
print("y label:", ax.get_ylabel())
print("bars:", len(ax.patches))
print("bar order:", [t.get_text() for t in ax.get_xticklabels()])
print("heights:", [int(p.get_height()) for p in ax.patches])
print("y axis starts at:", ax.get_ylim()[0])
```

```output
title: Electronics earns the most revenue
y label: Revenue (GBP)
bars: 5
bar order: ['Electronics', 'Home', 'Beauty', 'Sports', 'Toys']
heights: [45200, 31800, 20900, 18700, 12400]
y axis starts at: 0.0
```

### Example 4: Pie slices versus bars, a clutter check

A pie chart is readable with two to four large slices. This check counts the slices smaller than 5 percent, the point where labels collide, and reports how many bars would replace them. The same data, shown as a sorted bar, puts every value on the same scale.

```python run
import pandas as pd

revenue = pd.Series({"Home": 31800, "Electronics": 45200, "Beauty": 20900, "Sports": 18700,
                     "Toys": 12400, "Garden": 2600, "Books": 2100, "Pets": 1400})
share = (revenue / revenue.sum() * 100).round(1).sort_values(ascending=False)
print(share.to_string())
small = int((share < 5).sum())
print("slices under 5%:", small, "of", len(share))
print("recommend:", "sorted bar chart" if small >= 2 or len(share) > 5 else "pie is acceptable")
```

```output
Electronics    33.5
Home           23.5
Beauty         15.5
Sports         13.8
Toys            9.2
Garden          1.9
Books           1.6
Pets            1.0
slices under 5%: 3 of 8
recommend: sorted bar chart
```

## Real-world applications

- **Monthly management pack.** A finance analyst receives the same ten metrics every month. Using the family table fixes the chart type once (trend lines for revenue, sorted bars for cost centres), so the pack looks consistent and readers learn where to look.
- **Dashboard review.** Before a dashboard ships, a reviewer applies the checks from this day: zero baseline on bars, sorted categories, no 3-D, direct labels instead of a legend. These checks catch most misleading dashboards in minutes.
- **Stakeholder disagreements.** When two teams read the same chart differently, the cause is often an axis that starts above zero or a dual axis. Computing the visual ratio, as in Example 2, turns an argument about taste into a number.
- **Automated reporting.** A function such as `choose_chart` can pick a default chart for new metrics in an automated report, and a human reviews only the exceptions.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Start with the question the reader must answer, then the variable types, then the chart.
- Position and length are read most accurately; angle, area and hue are weaker, so give the key variable the strong channel.
- Remove an element only if it answers no question, and sort bars unless the categories have a natural order.
- A bar needs a zero baseline because its length is its value; a line may use a non-zero baseline if the axis is clearly labelled.

### Common mistakes

- Choosing the chart type first and then forcing the data into it. Symptom: a chart whose title needs a paragraph of explanation. Fix: write the question first.
- Truncating the y axis of a bar chart so a small difference looks large. Symptom: bars that differ by 4 percent in value but 67 percent in height. Fix: start at zero, or use a dot plot if zero wastes space.
- Using a pie chart with many slices. Symptom: tiny slices with overlapping labels. Fix: a sorted bar chart.
- Leaving categories unsorted. Symptom: the reader scans every bar to find the maximum. Fix: sort by value.

### Revision notes

- Five families: comparison, trend, distribution, relationship, composition.
- Channel order: position, length, angle, area, hue.
- Visual ratio = (b - baseline) / (a - baseline); it equals the true ratio only at baseline 0.
- Sort bars; zero baseline for bars; label units; the title says the finding.
