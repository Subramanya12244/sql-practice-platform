## Objectives

- Turn a vague worry ("revenue looks low") into a precise question: which metric, compared with what, over which period.
- Build a hypothesis tree whose branches are separate and together cover the possible causes of a revenue drop.
- Test each hypothesis with a query or a computation, and record whether the evidence supports it, rejects it or is inconclusive.
- Split a revenue change into an orders effect and a basket-size effect, and one level further into lines per order and revenue per line.
- Write a short recommendation with the finding, the evidence, the confidence and the next step.

## Understand

### The business question

A scenario starts with a message such as: "Revenue in the third quarter of 1998 was well below the quarter before. What happened?" This is not yet an analysis question. An analyst first makes it precise: **which metric** (net revenue from order lines, as in Day 144), **compared with what** (the previous quarter, or the same quarter a year ago, which removes seasonality), and **how large** is the change in amount and percent. Only then does it make sense to look for causes. Also ask what the stakeholder will do with the answer: restock, change a price, call customers, or simply report. A diagnosis that does not support a decision is trivia.

### A structured diagnosis

Random exploration finds a pattern and a plausible story, and the story may be wrong. A structured diagnosis has four steps:

1. **Confirm the drop is real.** Check that the data is complete (are all orders in the period loaded? did a table change?), that the periods are comparable (same length, same definition of revenue) and compare with the same season a year earlier. A "drop" caused by a late data load is a data engineering ticket, not a business problem.
2. **Decompose the metric.** Use the identity from Day 144: revenue is orders times average order value, and the order value is lines per order times revenue per line (or units per order times price per unit). The decomposition shows which factor moved, and the others can be set aside.
3. **Localise.** Within the factor that moved, look for where: which category, customer, country or channel carries most of the change? A change concentrated in one place has a different cause from a broad decline.
4. **Explain and recommend.** State what the evidence supports, how sure you are, and what to do or check next.

### The hypothesis tree

A **hypothesis tree** lists the possible causes as branches so that none is forgotten. A good tree is **mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive** (MECE): the branches do not overlap and together cover everything. For the revenue drop the first level follows the identity `revenue = orders x AOV`: either fewer orders, or smaller orders, or a data problem. Each branch then splits. Fewer orders: fewer customers ordering, or the same customers ordering less often. Smaller orders: fewer lines per order, fewer units per line, lower prices, or higher discounts. Writing the tree before querying matters because it stops you from testing only the hypothesis you like. Each leaf gets a **test**: a query or computation that would look different if the hypothesis were true, with a decision rule fixed beforehand (for example, "a change in price per unit of less than 10 percent does not explain a drop of 60 percent").

### Testing and recording

For every hypothesis write the result as one of three verdicts: **supported** (the data shows the expected change, large enough to explain the drop), **rejected** (the data shows no relevant change) or **inconclusive** (the data cannot decide, for example because the sample is too small). Keep the table in the analysis, because it is also the skeleton of the final write-up. Two cautions. First, an effect that is large enough to matter is not necessarily the **cause**: a basket-size drop is a finding about where the money went, and the reason behind it (a changed promotion, the loss of a large customer, a new customer type) needs another hypothesis level or evidence from outside the data. Second, with small samples, such as the eight orders per quarter in this synthetic Northwind, a single order can swing a quarter, so state the sample size and treat the conclusion as a lead.

### Writing the recommendation

A recommendation has five parts, in this order: the **finding** in one sentence ("Third-quarter revenue fell 62 percent from the second quarter because the average order shrank, not because there were fewer orders"), the **evidence** (two or three numbers), the **confidence** and its limits (sample, data quality), the **recommendation** (what to do), and the **next step** (what would confirm it). Avoid recommending an action that the evidence does not reach. If the analysis shows where but not why, the honest recommendation is a specific piece of follow-up: look at the large customers of the earlier period and ask whether they stopped ordering.

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Diagnosing a drop quickly and correctly keeps a team from spending a week on the wrong cause, such as cutting prices when the problem is basket size. The misconception is that analysis means producing many charts. In a diagnosis, the chart count matters less than the discipline of the tree: each query exists to confirm or reject a named hypothesis.

## Syntax and key functions

```sql-syntax
WITH q AS (
  SELECT date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date)::date AS quarter,
         COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS orders,
         COUNT(*) AS lines,
         SUM(od.quantity) AS units,
         SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) AS revenue
  FROM orders o JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
  GROUP BY 1)
SELECT quarter, revenue / orders AS aov, lines::numeric / orders AS lines_per_order FROM q;
```

```python
orders_effect = (o1 - o0) * aov0
aov_effect = (aov1 - aov0) * o1        # the two add up to rev1 - rev0
lines_effect = o1 * (lpo1 - lpo0) * rpl0
rpl_effect = o1 * lpo1 * (rpl1 - rpl0)
```

| Function or formula | Returns |
|---|---|
| `date_trunc('quarter', d)` | The first day of the quarter of `d`. |
| `COUNT(*)` after a join to lines | The number of order lines. |
| `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)` | The number of orders. |
| `COALESCE(x, 0)` | `0` when `x` is NULL (a category with no sales in a quarter). |
| `SUM(x) FILTER (WHERE cond)` | A sum for the rows that meet a condition, giving one column per period. |
| `(o1 - o0) * aov0` | The effect of the change in orders at the old order value. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Is the drop real, and how large?

The question: third-quarter 1998 revenue against the quarter before and against the same quarter a year earlier. The query also shows the number of orders, so that a missing data load would be visible as a missing count.

```sql run
SELECT to_char(date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date), 'YYYY-"Q"Q') AS quarter,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS orders,
       ROUND(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount))::numeric, 0) AS revenue,
       ROUND((SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) / COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id))::numeric, 0) AS aov
FROM orders o
JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
WHERE o.order_date >= DATE '1997-07-01' AND o.order_date < DATE '1998-10-01'
  AND date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date) IN (DATE '1997-07-01', DATE '1998-04-01', DATE '1998-07-01')
GROUP BY date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date)
ORDER BY 1;
```

```output
quarter | orders | revenue | aov
1997-Q3 | 8 | 17802 | 2225
1998-Q2 | 8 | 8687 | 1086
1998-Q3 | 8 | 3323 | 415
```

The drop is real and large: revenue fell 62 percent from the second to the third quarter of 1998 and 81 percent from the third quarter of 1997. The number of orders is the same (8) in all three quarters, so no data load is missing orders. The first hypothesis branch, fewer orders, already looks unlikely; the order value fell from about 2,225 to about 415.

### Example 2: Test the basket hypotheses

Hypothesis tree, second level, under "smaller orders": fewer lines per order, fewer units per line, lower price per unit, higher discount. One query measures all four for the same three quarters. The decision rule fixed in advance: a factor must change by more than 25 percent to explain a drop of more than 60 percent, and the discount can only matter as much as the whole discount is worth.

```sql run
SELECT to_char(date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date), 'YYYY-"Q"Q') AS quarter,
       ROUND(COUNT(*)::numeric / COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id), 1) AS lines_per_order,
       ROUND(SUM(od.quantity)::numeric / COUNT(*), 1) AS units_per_line,
       ROUND((SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) / SUM(od.quantity))::numeric, 2) AS price_per_unit,
       ROUND((100 * (1 - SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) / SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity)))::numeric, 1) AS discount_pct
FROM orders o
JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
WHERE date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date) IN (DATE '1997-07-01', DATE '1998-04-01', DATE '1998-07-01')
GROUP BY date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date)
ORDER BY 1;
```

```output
quarter | lines_per_order | units_per_line | price_per_unit | discount_pct
1997-Q3 | 4.0 | 20.8 | 26.81 | 5.1
1998-Q2 | 2.0 | 21.5 | 25.25 | 6.7
1998-Q3 | 1.0 | 19.0 | 21.86 | 7.8
```

Lines per order fell from 4.0 to 2.0 and then to 1.0: the third-quarter orders contain one product each, a fall of 75 percent from the year before. Units per line fell about 9 percent and price per unit about 18 percent, both below the 25 percent threshold. The discount rate rose from 5.1 to 7.8 percent of list revenue, but even removing the discount completely would add under 10 percent to the revenue, so it cannot explain an 81 percent drop. Verdicts: lines per order supported, price rejected, discount rejected, units per line rejected as the main cause.

### Example 3: Localise by category

Where did the revenue go? The query puts the categories of the third quarter of 1997 and the third quarter of 1998 side by side, using `COALESCE` for a category that had no sales, and ranks them by the size of the change.

```sql run
SELECT c.category_name,
       ROUND(COALESCE(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) FILTER (WHERE date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date) = DATE '1997-07-01'), 0)::numeric, 0) AS q3_1997,
       ROUND(COALESCE(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) FILTER (WHERE date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date) = DATE '1998-07-01'), 0)::numeric, 0) AS q3_1998,
       ROUND((COALESCE(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) FILTER (WHERE date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date) = DATE '1998-07-01'), 0)
            - COALESCE(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) FILTER (WHERE date_trunc('quarter', o.order_date) = DATE '1997-07-01'), 0))::numeric, 0) AS change
FROM orders o
JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN products p ON p.product_id = od.product_id
JOIN categories c ON c.category_id = p.category_id
GROUP BY c.category_name
ORDER BY change, c.category_name;
```

```output
category_name | q3_1997 | q3_1998 | change
Meat/Poultry | 5098 | 0 | -5098
Condiments | 2589 | 0 | -2589
Beverages | 2618 | 666 | -1952
Dairy Products | 2050 | 170 | -1880
Confections | 1886 | 297 | -1589
Seafood | 1400 | 18 | -1382
Produce | 1446 | 1190 | -256
Grains/Cereals | 714 | 982 | 268
```

Seven of the eight categories fell, so the decline is broad, but the meat and poultry category alone accounts for about a third of the loss: it sold 5,098 in the third quarter of 1997 and nothing in 1998. With only eight orders in a quarter, this could be one large customer who did not return, which is the next hypothesis to check.

### Example 4: The decomposition and the written recommendation

The numbers from the queries go into pandas. Revenue is split into an orders effect and an AOV effect, and the AOV effect is split further into lines per order and revenue per line. The effects must add up to the change in revenue. The final lines are the written recommendation, assembled from the evidence.

```python run
r0, o0, lines0 = 17802.0, 8, 32      # third quarter of 1997
r1, o1, lines1 = 3323.0, 8, 8        # third quarter of 1998
aov0, aov1 = r0 / o0, r1 / o1
lpo0, lpo1 = lines0 / o0, lines1 / o1
rpl0, rpl1 = r0 / lines0, r1 / lines1

orders_effect = (o1 - o0) * aov0
lines_effect = o1 * (lpo1 - lpo0) * rpl0
rpl_effect = o1 * lpo1 * (rpl1 - rpl0)
print("revenue change:", round(r1 - r0))
print("orders effect:", round(orders_effect), "| lines per order effect:", round(lines_effect), "| revenue per line effect:", round(rpl_effect))
print("sum of effects:", round(orders_effect + lines_effect + rpl_effect))
print()
print("Finding: revenue fell", round((r1 / r0 - 1) * -100), "% year on year because orders became smaller, not fewer.")
print("Evidence: orders", o0, "to", o1, "| lines per order", lpo0, "to", lpo1)
print("Confidence: limited, since the sample is", o1, "orders per quarter.")
print("Next step: check whether the large customers of 1997 stopped ordering.")
```

```output
revenue change: -14479
orders effect: 0 | lines per order effect: -13352 | revenue per line effect: -1128
sum of effects: -14479

Finding: revenue fell 81 % year on year because orders became smaller, not fewer.
Evidence: orders 8 to 8 | lines per order 4.0 to 1.0
Confidence: limited, since the sample is 8 orders per quarter.
Next step: check whether the large customers of 1997 stopped ordering.
```

Almost the whole change comes from the number of lines per order, a smaller part from revenue per line and none from the number of orders. The recommendation does not claim a cause the data has not shown: it names the finding, the evidence, the confidence and the follow-up.

## Real-world applications

- **Weekly business review**: when a metric moves, the analyst uses a tree of hypotheses like this one to answer "what happened?" within a day, and the table of verdicts becomes the meeting note.
- **Incident analysis for data quality**: the first step of the diagnosis, checking that the order counts are complete, catches broken pipelines before anyone tries to explain a business cause.
- **Account management**: Example 3 finds a category or customer that stopped buying, which becomes the call list for sales representatives.
- **Forecast review**: understanding that a quarter was unusual because of one order shows why a forecast missed and whether to adjust the method (Day 152).

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Make the question precise: metric, comparison, size of the change and the decision it supports.
- Check data completeness first; compare with the same season a year earlier.
- Decompose with identities (orders x AOV, lines per order x revenue per line) and test the leaves of a MECE hypothesis tree with decision rules set in advance.
- Record verdicts as supported, rejected or inconclusive.
- A recommendation has finding, evidence, confidence, recommendation and next step, and it claims no more than the evidence shows.

### Common mistakes

- Starting with charts and no hypotheses. You will find a story that fits, which may not be the cause; write the tree first.
- Comparing a quarter with the previous quarter only and calling a seasonal dip a problem; check the year-over-year change.
- Counting orders with `COUNT(*)` after a join to lines; the number is lines, so use `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)`.
- Treating a basket-size finding as the reason. It says where the money went; the reason needs a further hypothesis.
- Reporting a conclusion without the sample size. With eight orders per quarter one customer can change everything.

### Revision notes

- Diagnosis steps: confirm, decompose, localise, explain and recommend.
- `revenue = orders x lines per order x revenue per line`; effects add up exactly when computed in sequence.
- Verdicts: supported, rejected, inconclusive.
- Recommendation: finding, evidence, confidence, recommendation, next step.
