## Objectives

- Detect outliers with the IQR rule and with z-scores, and explain when each one misleads.
- Investigate a flagged value in context before deciding whether it is an error, a rare real event or a normal value for its group.
- Describe the three missing-data mechanisms (completely at random, at random given other columns, not at random) and check which one the evidence supports.
- Compare treatment choices (keep, drop, cap, impute, flag) by measuring how each one changes a summary.
- Record each decision with the number of rows or values affected.

## Understand

### What an outlier is

An **outlier** is a value that lies far from the rest of the data. The word describes a position, not a diagnosis. An outlier might be a typing mistake (a price of 5,055 instead of 505), a real but rare event (one customer buying ten televisions) or an ordinary value for a group you forgot to separate (a 400 dollar order is large for toys and typical for electronics). The analyst's job is not to delete outliers but to find out which of those three they are, because each one calls for a different action: fix the error, keep the real event and report it carefully, or recognise the group.

### Detecting outliers with the IQR rule

You met the interquartile range (IQR) on Days 86 and 107. The **IQR rule** flags a value as an outlier when it lies below Q1 - 1.5 x IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 x IQR, where Q1 and Q3 are the 25th and 75th percentiles. It is the rule behind the dots on a boxplot. Because quartiles are barely moved by a few extreme values, the rule keeps working even when outliers are present. Its weakness appears with skewed data: in a right-skewed variable such as order value, the rule flags many genuine large orders as outliers, because the upper fence sits close to the box while the tail is long. The multiplier 1.5 is a convention. A multiplier of 3 flags only the most extreme values.

### Detecting outliers with z-scores

The **z-score** of a value is (value - mean) / standard deviation, the number of standard deviations the value is from the mean (Day 88). A common cut-off flags values with an absolute z-score above 3. The z-score works well for roughly bell-shaped data, but it has a built-in problem called **masking**: a very extreme value inflates the mean and, especially, the standard deviation that are used to judge it, so extreme values partly hide themselves and each other. For skewed data the z-score also misjudges, because the mean and standard deviation describe the tail poorly. Computing the z-score on a transformed scale, such as `np.log1p` of the value, often gives a fairer judgement for right-skewed variables.

### Outliers relative to the group

A value can be unusual for its own group without being unusual overall. Comparing a price with the median price of its category, as a ratio, finds a toy listed at ten times the toy median even though that price would be ordinary for electronics. The general lesson is to judge a value against the right comparison group, and to look at the flagged rows with their other columns before deciding anything.

### Missing data: three mechanisms

Missing values (Day 74) are only a nuisance if they are missing for no reason. The reason, called the **missing-data mechanism**, decides how risky it is to ignore them.

- **Missing completely at random (MCAR).** The chance of a value being missing is the same for every row, unrelated to any data. A sensor drops a reading by pure chance. Dropping these rows loses information but does not bias results.
- **Missing at random (MAR).** The chance of missingness depends on other columns you can see. Younger customers skip the age question, or one channel rarely collects ratings. The bias can be repaired by using those other columns, for example filling a value from the group the row belongs to.
- **Missing not at random (MNAR).** The chance of missingness depends on the missing value itself. Unhappy customers do not leave a rating, or high earners do not report income. Nothing in the observed data can fully repair this, and every summary of the observed values is biased in a direction you can reason about but not measure.

You cannot prove which mechanism holds from the data alone, but you can look for evidence. Compare the missing share across groups of other columns: if it differs strongly, the mechanism is not MCAR. Compare other columns between rows with and without the value.

### Treatment choices

Each choice changes your numbers, so choose deliberately and measure the change.

- **Keep** the value when it is a real observation. Report it, and consider using the median or a log scale so it does not dominate a summary.
- **Drop** rows only when you can justify that they are invalid, and always count them. Dropping because a value is inconvenient is not justified.
- **Cap** (also called **winsorise**) extreme values at a chosen percentile, for example setting everything above the 99th percentile to the 99th percentile. This limits the influence of extremes while keeping the row.
- **Impute** missing values by filling them: with the overall median, with the group median, or by other methods. Imputation keeps rows but invents values, so it narrows the apparent spread and should be avoided for the variable you are about to analyse most closely.
- **Flag** by adding a column such as `rating_missing`, so that later analysis can ask whether missingness itself is informative.

### Common misconception

"Outliers are errors, so delete them." Deleting a real large order from a revenue analysis removes exactly the customers who matter most. A second misconception is that filling missing values with the mean is neutral. It leaves the mean unchanged but shrinks the standard deviation and weakens every correlation involving that column.

### Why it matters

One extreme order can double an average, and a column that is missing for unhappy customers can make satisfaction look better than it is. Measuring how much a decision changes the result lets you tell a stakeholder how fragile a number is.

## Syntax and key functions

Outlier rules:

```python
q1, q3 = s.quantile([0.25, 0.75])
flag_iqr = (s < q1 - 1.5 * (q3 - q1)) | (s > q3 + 1.5 * (q3 - q1))
z = (s - s.mean()) / s.std()
flag_z = z.abs() > 3
```

Missing-data tools:

```python
df.isna().sum()
df.groupby("group")["col"].apply(lambda s: s.isna().mean())
df["col"].fillna(df.groupby("group")["col"].transform("median"))
df["col"].clip(upper=df["col"].quantile(0.99))
```

| Function | Returns |
|---|---|
| `s.quantile(q)` | Value at fraction q of the sorted data. |
| `s.clip(lower, upper)` | Values limited to the range (capping). |
| `s.fillna(value_or_series)` | Missing values replaced. |
| `groupby(...)[col].transform("median")` | Group median repeated on every row of the group. |
| `s.isna()` | Boolean mask of missing values. |
| `np.log1p(s)` | log(1 + s), compresses a right tail. |
| `s / s.groupby(g).transform("median")` | Value as a ratio to its group median. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: IQR rule and z-score on order totals

Both rules are applied to `order_total`, which is right-skewed. The IQR rule flags many orders; the z-score rule flags far fewer, because the large standard deviation hides moderate values. On the log scale the z-score flags almost nothing. The rules disagree because they measure different things, so a flag is a prompt to look, not a verdict.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

pd.set_option("display.width", 200)
pd.set_option("display.max_columns", 20)


def make_orders(messy=False):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 600
    category = rng.choice(["home", "beauty", "sports", "electronics", "toys"], size=n, p=[0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10])
    base = pd.Series({"home": 40, "beauty": 25, "sports": 55, "electronics": 180, "toys": 20})
    discount = rng.choice([0, 5, 10, 20], size=n, p=[0.6, 0.15, 0.15, 0.1])
    ship = np.clip(rng.poisson(3.5, n) + 1, 1, 12).astype(float)
    df = pd.DataFrame({
        "order_id": np.arange(1001, 1001 + n),
        "order_date": pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 180, n), unit="D"),
        "customer_id": rng.integers(1, 260, n),
        "channel": rng.choice(["organic", "paid", "email", "referral"], size=n, p=[0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]),
        "device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop", "tablet"], size=n, p=[0.55, 0.35, 0.10]),
        "category": category,
        "quantity": np.clip(rng.poisson(0.8 + discount / 20, n) + 1, 1, 6),
        "unit_price": (base[category].to_numpy() * rng.lognormal(0, 0.25, n)).round(2),
        "discount_pct": discount,
        "shipping_days": ship,
    })
    df.loc[rng.choice(n, 4, replace=False), "unit_price"] *= 8
    df["order_total"] = (df["quantity"] * df["unit_price"] * (1 - df["discount_pct"] / 100)).round(2)
    df["rating"] = np.clip(np.round(4.3 - 0.2 * ship + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)), 1, 5)
    df["returned"] = rng.random(n) < 0.06 + 0.06 * (category == "electronics") + 0.03 * (ship > 6)
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.05, "shipping_days"] = np.nan
    df.loc[rng.random(n) < 0.25, "rating"] = np.nan
    if messy:
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 25, replace=False), "channel"] = "Paid "
        df.loc[rng.choice(n, 3, replace=False), "quantity"] = -1
        df = pd.concat([df, df.sample(6, random_state=1)], ignore_index=True)
    return df
orders = make_orders()
s = orders["order_total"]
q1, q3 = s.quantile([0.25, 0.75])
iqr = q3 - q1
flag_iqr = (s < q1 - 1.5 * iqr) | (s > q3 + 1.5 * iqr)
z = (s - s.mean()) / s.std()
zlog = (np.log1p(s) - np.log1p(s).mean()) / np.log1p(s).std()
print("IQR fences:", round(q1 - 1.5 * iqr, 2), round(q3 + 1.5 * iqr, 2))
print("flagged by IQR rule:", int(flag_iqr.sum()))
print("flagged by |z| > 3:", int((z.abs() > 3).sum()))
print("flagged by |z| > 3 on log scale:", int((zlog.abs() > 3).sum()))
```

```output
IQR fences: -97.83 270.34
flagged by IQR rule: 79
flagged by |z| > 3: 13
flagged by |z| > 3 on log scale: 1
```

### Example 4: Missing not at random changes the answer

This simulation hides the lowest ratings: unhappy customers are more likely not to answer. The mean of the observed ratings then rises above the true mean of all ratings, a bias that no amount of extra data would repair. The true ratings are known here only because the simulation creates them.

The same outliers are treated four ways and the effect on the mean order total is measured. Dropping the IQR-flagged orders changes the mean a lot, capping at the 99th percentile changes it moderately, and the median barely moves. ### Example 6: Imputing with a group median and flagging

The missing `shipping_days` values are filled with the median of the order's category, and a flag column records which values were filled. The standard deviation before and after shows the effect of imputation: it shrinks slightly because the filled values sit in the middle. Keeping the flag lets a later analysis check whether filled rows behave differently.

## Real-world applications

- **Revenue reporting.** Before reporting average order value, an analyst compares the mean with the median and the capped mean, as in Example 5, and states how much a handful of large orders moves the figure.
- **Data entry and integration errors.** The group-relative ratio in Example 2 finds prices with a misplaced decimal, which would never be caught by a global rule.
- **Survey and feedback analysis.** Checking how missingness varies by group, as in Examples 3 and 4, shows whether a satisfaction score is trustworthy or reflects only people who chose to answer.
- **Feature preparation for models.** The imputed value plus a missing flag, as in Example 6, is a standard way to hand incomplete columns to later modelling work.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- An outlier is a position, not a diagnosis: investigate whether it is an error, a rare real event or normal for its group.
- The IQR rule resists extreme values but over-flags skewed data; the z-score is masked by extremes, so consider a log scale.
- Compare a value with its own group, as a ratio to the group median.
- Check missingness by group to judge MCAR, MAR or MNAR; MNAR biases every observed summary.
- Treatments (keep, drop, cap, impute, flag) all change results: measure the change and log the count.

### Common mistakes

- Deleting all flagged values automatically. Real high-value orders vanish and revenue is understated. Fix: inspect the rows first.
- Applying the z-score to a skewed column and concluding nothing is unusual. Fix: use the IQR rule or the log scale as well.
- Filling missing values with the mean and then reporting the standard deviation or correlations of that column, which are now too small. Fix: impute only where needed and state it.
- Assuming missing means random. Fix: compare the missing share across groups and across the other variables.
- Dropping rows with any missing value (`dropna()`) and silently losing a large share of the data. Fix: count the rows lost first.
- Not recording the decision. Fix: add the rows affected to the assumptions log from Day 106.

### Revision notes

- IQR rule: below Q1 - 1.5 IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 IQR. Z-score: `abs(z) > 3`.
- Group ratio: `s / s.groupby(g).transform("median")`.
- MCAR, MAR, MNAR; look at missing share by group.
- `clip(upper=...)` caps; `fillna(group_median)` imputes; add a flag column.
- Always measure the effect of the treatment on the summary.
