## Objectives

- Explain the difference between a population and a sample, and show how a biased sampling method gives a wrong estimate.
- Calculate the standard error of a mean and explain why it shrinks as the sample grows.
- Demonstrate the central limit theorem with a seeded simulation, and say what it does and does not promise.
- Build a confidence interval for a mean and for a proportion, and interpret it correctly.

## Understand

### Population and sample

A **population** is the complete group you want to know about: all one million orders, every customer, every part a machine will ever make. A **sample** is the part of it you actually observe. We sample because measuring everything is too slow or too expensive, or because the population includes future values that do not exist yet. The number computed from the whole population, such as its mean, is a **parameter**, and the same number computed from a sample is a **statistic**. The parameter is fixed but usually unknown, and the statistic is known but changes from one sample to the next. Statistics is the discipline of saying what a statistic tells you about the parameter, and how sure you can be.

### How a sample is chosen

The way the sample is chosen matters more than its size. In a **simple random sample**, every member of the population has the same chance of being picked, and picks do not depend on each other. This protects against **bias**, a systematic tendency of the sample to differ from the population. If you take the first 100 rows of a table that is sorted by amount, or survey only customers who answered an email, the sample is not random, and its mean differs from the population mean in a consistent direction. More data does not fix this: a million biased rows are still biased. In pandas, `df.sample(n=100, random_state=7)` draws a random sample of rows. The examples here draw random row positions with a seeded NumPy generator, `rng.integers`, so the results are repeatable.

### Sampling variation and the standard error

Take two random samples of 100 orders from the same table and their means will differ, just by chance. That variation between samples is **sampling variation**. Its size is measured by the **standard error**, the standard deviation of the sample mean across repeated samples. For a mean it has a simple formula: standard error = s / sqrt(n), where s is the sample standard deviation (with `n - 1`, Day 87) and n is the sample size. Two facts follow. A larger sample has a smaller standard error, but only as the square root: to halve the error you need four times as many observations. And a more variable population gives a larger standard error for the same n. Do not confuse the two standard deviations. The standard deviation s describes how individual values vary. The standard error describes how the mean would vary from sample to sample, and it is always smaller.

### The central limit theorem

Day 88 introduced the normal curve and said that averages often follow it. The **central limit theorem** (CLT) is the reason. It says that if you take many random samples of size n from a population with a finite mean and standard deviation, then the sample means are approximately normally distributed around the population mean, with spread equal to the standard error, and the approximation improves as n grows. This holds even when the population itself is skewed. The practical consequence is that you can use normal-curve calculations for means of reasonably large samples without knowing the shape of the data. For strongly skewed data, "reasonably large" can mean a few dozen or more, and the theorem says nothing about single values: individual orders remain skewed, only their averages become bell-shaped.

### Confidence intervals

A point estimate such as a sample mean of 33.2 is almost certainly not exactly the population mean. A **confidence interval** gives a range of plausible values. The usual interval for a mean is the estimate plus or minus a **critical value** times the standard error: mean ± t * s / sqrt(n). For a 95% interval, the critical value t comes from the t distribution with `n - 1` degrees of freedom, which is slightly wider than the normal curve for small samples and approaches 1.96 as n grows. In SciPy, `stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1)` returns it, and `stats.t.interval(0.95, n - 1, loc=mean, scale=se)` returns the whole interval.

The meaning of "95%" is easy to get wrong. It describes the method, not the one interval you have: if you repeated the sampling and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true population mean. For a particular interval the true mean is either inside or not, and you do not know which. It is wrong to say "there is a 95% probability that the mean is in this interval", and it is wrong to say that 95% of the data lie in the interval: that would be a much wider range. A wider interval comes from a higher confidence level, a smaller sample or more variable data.

For a **proportion**, such as the share of customers who renewed, the standard error is sqrt(p(1 - p) / n), with p the observed share, and the 95% interval is p ± 1.96 times that. This normal approximation works when both n times p and n times (1 - p) are at least about 10.

### Common misconception

Learners often think a bigger sample always means a better answer. A bigger sample reduces random error, but not bias. A second misconception is that a confidence interval says where most individual values lie. It says where the population mean plausibly is. The third is that "95%" is a probability for the interval you hold, as described above.

### Why it matters

Almost every number in a report comes from a sample: survey results, a month of data used to estimate next year, an A/B test. Quoting an estimate with its interval tells the reader how much to trust it, and the standard error tells you how large a sample you need before collecting it.

## Syntax and key functions

```python nocheck
df.sample(n=100, random_state=7)              # random rows
se = s.std() / np.sqrt(len(s))                # standard error of the mean
stats.sem(s)                                  # same value
stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=len(s) - 1)             # critical value for 95%
stats.t.interval(0.95, len(s) - 1, loc=s.mean(), scale=se)
p_hat = k / n; se_p = np.sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)
```

| Idea | Formula or call |
|---|---|
| Standard error of a mean | `s / sqrt(n)` |
| `stats.sem(x)` | standard error with `n - 1` |
| 95% critical value, `n - 1` df | `stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1)` |
| Confidence interval for a mean | `mean ± t * se` |
| `stats.t.interval(0.95, df, loc, scale)` | the interval directly |
| Proportion interval | `p ± 1.96 * sqrt(p(1 - p) / n)` |

## Learn by example

The examples use a made-up population of 10,000 order values from 0.01 to 100, where each value is the square of its rank divided by 100, so most values are small and a few are large, which gives a right skew (skewness about 0.63). The population mean is known, which lets you see how well each sample does.

### Example 1: A biased sample and a random sample

Taking the first 100 rows of a table sorted by size gives the wrong answer. A random sample of the same size is close.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
pop = ((np.arange(10000) % 100) + 1) ** 2 / 100
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
print(round(pop.mean(), 2), round(pop.std(), 2))
pop_sorted = np.sort(pop)
first100 = pop_sorted[:100]
random100 = pop_sorted[rng.integers(0, len(pop_sorted), size=100)]
print(round(first100.mean(), 2))
print(round(random100.mean(), 2))
```

```output
33.84 30.09
0.01
37.66
```

The first 100 sorted values are all tiny, so their mean is far below the population mean of 33.84. The random sample is much closer, though not exact.

### Example 2: Standard error of one sample

The sample mean, sample standard deviation and the standard error are calculated by hand and checked against SciPy.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
pop = ((np.arange(10000) % 100) + 1) ** 2 / 100
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
sample = pop[rng.integers(0, len(pop), size=50)]
n = len(sample)
s = sample.std(ddof=1)
se = s / np.sqrt(n)
print(round(sample.mean(), 2), round(s, 2), round(se, 2))
print(round(float(stats.sem(sample)), 2))
```

```output
31.42 27.45 3.88
3.88
```

The standard error is much smaller than the standard deviation of the values, because averaging 50 values smooths out the individual variation.

### Example 3: The central limit theorem

Thousands of sample means are drawn for three sample sizes. Their centre stays at the population mean, their spread follows sigma over the square root of n, and the shape becomes more symmetric than the population.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
pop = ((np.arange(10000) % 100) + 1) ** 2 / 100
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
sigma = pop.std()
print("population skew", round(float(stats.skew(pop)), 2))
for n in (10, 40, 160):
    means = np.array([pop[rng.integers(0, len(pop), size=n)].mean() for _ in range(2000)])
    print(n, round(means.mean(), 1), round(means.std(), 1), round(sigma / np.sqrt(n), 1), round(float(stats.skew(means)), 2))
```

```output
population skew 0.63
10 33.7 9.3 9.5 0.16
40 33.9 4.8 4.8 0.09
160 33.8 2.4 2.4 0.11
```

Each row shows the sample size, the mean of the sample means (always near 33.8), their standard deviation next to the theoretical standard error, and their skewness. Quadrupling n halves the spread, and the skewness stays well below the population's 0.63, which shows the means are more symmetric than single values.

### Example 4: Confidence intervals for a mean and a proportion

The 95% interval for a mean is built from the critical value, then checked with SciPy. A proportion interval uses the normal approximation.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
pop = ((np.arange(10000) % 100) + 1) ** 2 / 100
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
sample = pop[rng.integers(0, len(pop), size=40)]
n = len(sample)
se = sample.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
t_crit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1)
low, high = sample.mean() - t_crit * se, sample.mean() + t_crit * se
print(round(float(t_crit), 3), round(float(low), 2), round(float(high), 2))
lo2, hi2 = stats.t.interval(0.95, n - 1, loc=sample.mean(), scale=se)
print(round(float(lo2), 2), round(float(hi2), 2), low < pop.mean() < high)
k, m = 120, 400
p = k / m
se_p = np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / m)
print(round(p - 1.96 * se_p, 3), round(p + 1.96 * se_p, 3))
```

```output
2.023 23.5 42.42
23.5 42.42 True
0.255 0.345
```

The critical value for 39 degrees of freedom, 2.023, is a little above 1.96. Both ways of building the interval give the same limits. The renewal rate of 30% has an interval of about 25.5% to 34.5%.

### Example 5: What "95% confident" means

The interval is built for 1,000 different random samples, and we count how often it contains the true mean. This is a property of the method.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
pop = ((np.arange(10000) % 100) + 1) ** 2 / 100
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
hits = 0
trials = 1000
for _ in range(trials):
    sample = pop[rng.integers(0, len(pop), size=40)]
    se = sample.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(40)
    t_crit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, 39)
    if sample.mean() - t_crit * se <= pop.mean() <= sample.mean() + t_crit * se:
        hits += 1
print(hits / trials)
print(0.92 < hits / trials < 0.98)
```

```output
0.952
True
```

About 95% of the intervals catch the true mean. The skew of the population can lower the coverage slightly for small samples, which is why the result is close to 0.95 rather than exactly equal.

## Real-world applications

- **Survey reporting.** A poll of 1,000 customers reports a satisfaction share with its margin of error, which is half the width of the proportion interval.
- **Planning sample sizes.** The standard error formula shows an analyst how many records to audit: halving the margin of error needs four times as many.
- **Data quality audits.** A data engineer checks a random sample of loaded rows against the source and reports the error rate with an interval instead of a single number.
- **Business forecasting.** A finance team reports the average order value from last month's orders together with a confidence interval, so a change from the previous month can be judged against the uncertainty.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- A statistic from a sample estimates a parameter of the population; random sampling protects against bias, and larger samples do not remove bias.
- Standard error is s divided by the square root of n; it measures variation of the mean between samples.
- The CLT says sample means are approximately normal with mean equal to the population mean and spread equal to the standard error, even for skewed data.
- A 95% confidence interval is estimate ± critical value × standard error; the 95% describes the method over repeated samples.

### Common mistakes

- Using a convenient sample, such as the first rows or volunteers, and treating it as random. Sample with `df.sample` or `rng.integers`.
- Confusing standard deviation with standard error, which makes intervals far too wide or narrow. The error is `s / sqrt(n)`.
- Saying "the mean has a 95% chance of being in this interval". Say that the method captures the mean in about 95% of repeated samples.
- Using the proportion formula with a tiny count (for example 2 successes in 20). The normal approximation fails when `n * p` is below about 10.

### Revision notes

- Standard error: `s / sqrt(n)`; four times the data halves it.
- 95% t interval: `mean ± t.ppf(0.975, n - 1) * se`.
- Proportion: `p ± 1.96 * sqrt(p(1 - p) / n)`.
- CLT: means become normal; single values do not.
