## Objectives

- State precisely what a campaign measurement must answer: the extra sales caused by the campaign, net of its cost.
- Explain why a before-and-after comparison and a comparison with customers who were not targeted are both misleading, and show the size of the bias on data.
- Use a randomised holdout and a difference-in-differences calculation to estimate uplift, with an approximate interval.
- Compute the incremental return on investment, including a pessimistic case from the lower end of the interval.
- Write a recommendation that states the result, its uncertainty and the decision that follows.

## Understand

### The question

The scenario: a company sent a discount email to its loyal customers last month and spent 4,000 on it. The head of marketing says that those customers bought more afterwards, and asks whether the campaign paid off. The analyst has to turn this into a measurable question: **how many extra orders did the email cause, compared with what would have happened without it, and are the margin on those orders larger than the cost?** The words "would have happened without it" are the heart of the matter. This is the incrementality question of Day 148. Measuring it requires a **comparison group** that represents what the treated customers would have done if they had not received the email.

### Weak comparisons

Three comparisons are commonly made, and two are biased.

- **Before and after** the campaign, for the customers who received it. The difference mixes the effect of the campaign with everything else that changed between the two periods: season, other marketing, price changes, a holiday. If sales rise everywhere in the period, the campaign gets credit for the general trend.
- **Treated against untreated customers** when the treated customers were chosen. If the email went to the most loyal customers, they would have bought more than the others even without the email. The difference measures who was selected, not what the email did. This is **selection bias**, and it is usually the largest source of error, because marketers send campaigns to the customers they expect to respond.
- **Treated against a random holdout**, a group chosen by chance from the same eligible customers and not sent the email. Both groups have the same mix of customers, so the difference in their results is the effect of the campaign, within the margin of random noise. This is the reliable comparison. If no holdout was planned, a second-best way is to find a comparison group that behaved like the treated group in the period before the campaign.

### Difference in differences

When you have a group that was not treated and data from before and after, the **difference-in-differences** estimate removes both problems of the weak comparisons. Compute the change from before to after in the treated group, compute the same change in the comparison group, and subtract: `effect = (treated after - treated before) - (comparison after - comparison before)`. The change in the comparison group estimates what would have happened to the treated group without the campaign (the trend), and the subtraction removes it. It assumes that without the campaign both groups would have changed by the same amount, which is plausible when they were alike before the campaign and which you can check by comparing their earlier history. With a random holdout the before-period matters less, but the calculation also removes chance differences between the groups before the campaign.

### Uncertainty

A rate measured on a group of customers has random noise. For a difference between two rates `p1` and `p0` on groups of size `n1` and `n0`, the standard error is `sqrt(p1(1-p1)/n1 + p0(1-p0)/n0)`, and an approximate 95 percent interval is the difference plus or minus 1.96 standard errors (Day 148). A small holdout, often chosen to protect revenue, makes the interval wide. If the interval includes zero, or includes values at which the campaign loses money, the honest conclusion is that the evidence is not enough to say, and the recommendation is to extend or repeat the test, not to claim success or failure.

### The return on investment

Once the uplift is measured, `incremental orders = uplift x number of treated customers`, `incremental gross profit = incremental orders x order value x margin`, and `incremental ROI = (incremental gross profit - cost) / cost`. Use the lower end of the interval as a pessimistic case. The decision rule could be: "continue if the pessimistic case still covers the cost; repeat the test if only the central estimate does; stop if even the optimistic case does not". The central estimate alone hides the risk. Also note what the ROI leaves out: costs of staff time, effects on future purchases (some of the extra orders may be pulled forward from the next month), and customers who unsubscribe.

### The recommendation

The write-up has the same five parts as in Day 153: the finding, the evidence, the confidence, the recommendation and the next step. For a campaign: "The email caused about N extra orders per 100 customers (interval A to B). At the stated margin this returned X per 1 spent in the central case and Y in the pessimistic case. Recommend ... " A good recommendation also says what the decision depends on, for example the margin per order or the size of the future holdout.

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Marketing budgets are large, and campaigns that look successful in a naive comparison are often not. The misconception is that more data (a bigger "after" sample) fixes a biased comparison. It does not: a million customers in the wrong comparison give a very precise wrong answer. The comparison group matters more than the sample size, and the cheapest way to get a good comparison group is to plan the holdout before the campaign starts.

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Before and after

Twenty thousand customers are simulated (seeded). The campaign went to a random 80 percent of the loyal customers, and a random 20 percent of them were held out. The first comparison is the one the head of marketing made: the purchase rate of the customers who were sent the email, before and after.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 20000
d = pd.DataFrame({"loyal": rng.random(n) < 0.40})
d["sent"] = d["loyal"] & (rng.random(n) < 0.80)
base = np.where(d["loyal"], 0.20, 0.06)
d["before"] = rng.random(n) < base
d["after"] = rng.random(n) < base + 0.02 + 0.04 * d["sent"]
t = d[d["sent"]]
print("customers sent the email:", len(t))
print("purchase rate before:", round(float(t["before"].mean()) * 100, 1), "%  after:", round(float(t["after"].mean()) * 100, 1), "%")
print("apparent uplift (points):", round(float(t["after"].mean() - t["before"].mean()) * 100, 1))
```

```output
customers sent the email: 6391
purchase rate before: 20.6 %  after: 27.2 %
apparent uplift (points): 6.5
```

The rate rose by about 6.5 points. But purchase rates were rising for everyone in this period, and the holdout in Example 3 shows how much, so this number gives the campaign credit for the general trend.

### Example 2: Selection bias

The same data. The treated customers are now compared with all customers who were not sent the email. That group contains the loyal holdout and all the non-loyal customers, who buy much less in any case.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 20000
d = pd.DataFrame({"loyal": rng.random(n) < 0.40})
d["sent"] = d["loyal"] & (rng.random(n) < 0.80)
base = np.where(d["loyal"], 0.20, 0.06)
d["before"] = rng.random(n) < base
d["after"] = rng.random(n) < base + 0.02 + 0.04 * d["sent"]
t = d[d["sent"]]
u = d[~d["sent"]]
print("treated after:", round(float(t["after"].mean()) * 100, 1), "%  | not treated after:", round(float(u["after"].mean()) * 100, 1), "%")
print("apparent uplift (points):", round(float(t["after"].mean() - u["after"].mean()) * 100, 1))
print("share of loyal customers: treated", round(float(t["loyal"].mean()), 2), "not treated", round(float(u["loyal"].mean()), 2))
```

```output
treated after: 27.2 %  | not treated after: 9.2 %
apparent uplift (points): 17.9
share of loyal customers: treated 1.0 not treated 0.12
```

The apparent uplift is far larger, and the last line explains why: everyone treated was a loyal customer, while the untreated group is mostly not loyal. The comparison measures the difference between loyal and other customers, not the email.

### Example 3: Holdout and difference in differences

Among loyal customers only, the sent group is compared with the randomly held-out group. First the simple difference in the after period with its 95 percent interval, then the difference in differences, which also removes any chance difference in the before period.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 20000
d = pd.DataFrame({"loyal": rng.random(n) < 0.40})
d["sent"] = d["loyal"] & (rng.random(n) < 0.80)
base = np.where(d["loyal"], 0.20, 0.06)
d["before"] = rng.random(n) < base
d["after"] = rng.random(n) < base + 0.02 + 0.04 * d["sent"]
L = d[d["loyal"]]
t, c = L[L["sent"]], L[~L["sent"]]
pt, pc = float(t["after"].mean()), float(c["after"].mean())
se = (pt * (1 - pt) / len(t) + pc * (1 - pc) / len(c)) ** 0.5
uplift = pt - pc
print("treated:", len(t), "holdout:", len(c))
print("rate before: sent", round(float(t["before"].mean()) * 100, 1), "% | holdout", round(float(c["before"].mean()) * 100, 1), "%")
print("uplift after (points):", round(uplift * 100, 1), "| 95% interval:", round((uplift - 1.96 * se) * 100, 1), "to", round((uplift + 1.96 * se) * 100, 1))
did = (float(t["after"].mean()) - float(t["before"].mean())) - (float(c["after"].mean()) - float(c["before"].mean()))
print("difference in differences (points):", round(did * 100, 1))
print("trend in the holdout (points):", round(float(c["after"].mean() - c["before"].mean()) * 100, 1))
```

```output
treated: 6391 holdout: 1638
rate before: sent 20.6 % | holdout 19.4 %
uplift after (points): 6.0 | 95% interval: 3.7 to 8.2
difference in differences (points): 4.7
trend in the holdout (points): 1.8
```

The holdout is the fair comparison. Its own before-and-after change (about 1.8 points) is the general trend that Example 1 credited to the campaign. The treated group also started slightly higher than the holdout by chance (the "rate before" line), so the simple difference after the campaign (6.0 points) is a little too large, and the difference in differences (4.7 points) corrects for it. The interval, 3.7 to 8.2 points, is nearly five points wide even with 6,391 treated customers: a noticeable range for the decision.

### Example 4: Incremental ROI and the recommendation

The uplift and its interval turn into incremental orders and ROI, using a cost of 4,000, an average order value of 60 and a gross margin of 35 percent. The central case is the difference in differences, and the pessimistic case subtracts the half-width of the interval, and the printed lines are the recommendation.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 20000
d = pd.DataFrame({"loyal": rng.random(n) < 0.40})
d["sent"] = d["loyal"] & (rng.random(n) < 0.80)
base = np.where(d["loyal"], 0.20, 0.06)
d["before"] = rng.random(n) < base
d["after"] = rng.random(n) < base + 0.02 + 0.04 * d["sent"]
L = d[d["loyal"]]
t, c = L[L["sent"]], L[~L["sent"]]
pt, pc = float(t["after"].mean()), float(c["after"].mean())
se = (pt * (1 - pt) / len(t) + pc * (1 - pc) / len(c)) ** 0.5
did = (pt - float(t["before"].mean())) - (pc - float(c["before"].mean()))
cost, value, margin = 4000.0, 60.0, 0.35
for label, u in (("central", did), ("pessimistic", did - 1.96 * se), ("optimistic", did + 1.96 * se)):
    extra = u * len(t)
    roi = (extra * value * margin - cost) / cost
    print(f"{label:12} uplift {u * 100:5.1f} pts  extra orders {extra:6.0f}  incremental ROI {roi:6.2f}")
print()
print("Finding: the email raised the purchase rate by about", round(did * 100, 1), "points among loyal customers.")
print("Confidence: the interval is wide; the pessimistic case loses money.")
print("Recommendation: run it again with a larger holdout before scaling, and compare margin per order.")
```

```output
central      uplift   4.7 pts  extra orders    300  incremental ROI   0.57
pessimistic  uplift   2.4 pts  extra orders    156  incremental ROI  -0.18
optimistic   uplift   7.0 pts  extra orders    444  incremental ROI   1.33

Finding: the email raised the purchase rate by about 4.7 points among loyal customers.
Confidence: the interval is wide; the pessimistic case loses money.
Recommendation: run it again with a larger holdout before scaling, and compare margin per order.
```

The central case pays for the campaign and the optimistic case more than doubles the money, but the pessimistic case loses about a fifth of the cost. The interval is approximate: it takes the width from the simple after-campaign comparison and centres it on the difference-in-differences estimate. The decision rule says to repeat the test with a larger holdout (a larger holdout narrows the interval) before scaling the spend.

## Real-world applications

- **Campaign evaluation**: marketing teams report incremental uplift from a holdout instead of the raw response, and finance accepts the return because it is net of what would have happened anyway.
- **Test design**: Example 3 shows how wide an interval is for a holdout of 1,600 customers, which tells the team how large the holdout must be next time.
- **Retail promotions**: stores where a promotion ran are compared with similar stores where it did not, before and after, using a difference in differences.
- **Product changes**: the same logic evaluates a new checkout page shown to a random half of visitors.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- The question is the extra sales caused by the campaign, net of cost, measured against a comparison group.
- Before and after includes the trend; treated against untreated includes selection bias.
- A random holdout gives a fair comparison; difference in differences removes trend and chance differences.
- Report the interval and the incremental ROI in a pessimistic case too.
- Recommendations follow from the evidence and its uncertainty: scale, repeat or stop.

### Common mistakes

- Comparing the customers who received an offer with those who did not when the offer was targeted. The groups differ before the campaign; use a holdout.
- Giving the campaign the credit for a general trend by comparing before and after. Subtract the change in a comparison group.
- Quoting the central estimate without an interval. The reader cannot see whether the campaign could be losing money.
- Believing a bigger sample cures bias. A big biased comparison is precise but wrong.
- Leaving out costs and pulled-forward sales in the ROI. State what the ROI excludes.

### Revision notes

- `did = (t_after - t_before) - (c_after - c_before)`.
- Interval = uplift plus or minus 1.96 x standard error of the difference.
- `incremental ROI = (uplift x n x value x margin - cost) / cost`.
- Decision: scale if pessimistic ROI is above zero, repeat if only central, stop otherwise.
