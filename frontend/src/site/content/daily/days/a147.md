## Objectives

- Define a funnel as an ordered list of steps with a clear rule for who enters it, and count how many people reach each step.
- Calculate step conversion, overall conversion and drop-off, and explain the difference between them.
- Compare funnels between segments such as device and find the step that limits each one.
- Estimate the value of fixing a bottleneck with a stated what-if calculation, and explain why the biggest percentage drop is not always the biggest opportunity.
- Build an order-fulfilment funnel in SQL from dates that may be NULL.

## Understand

### What a funnel is

A **funnel** is a sequence of steps that people go through toward a goal, with fewer people reaching each step. A web shop funnel might be: visited the site, viewed a product, added to the cart, started checkout, paid. A sales funnel might be: lead, qualified, proposal, won. An operational funnel can be an order: placed, shipped, shipped on time. The word comes from the shape: wide at the top and narrow at the bottom. Three conditions make a funnel analysis meaningful. The steps must be **ordered**, so that reaching a step normally means passing the earlier ones. The **unit** of counting must be clear and the same at every step (sessions, visitors or orders, never a mixture). And there must be a **time window**: a visitor who adds to the cart on Monday and pays on Friday should either count or not by a rule written down in advance.

### Counting: reached each step

For each step, count the units that reached it. If the data is one row per session with a true/false column per step, a funnel is the sum of each column, but only if each column is already restricted to those who passed the earlier step. A frequent data error is a flag that is true for a customer who skipped a step (a paid order without a recorded cart event, because of a tracking gap). Then later steps can exceed earlier ones, which is a sign of a data problem, not a miracle. Check that the counts never increase down the funnel before computing anything else.

### Step conversion, overall conversion and drop-off

- **Step conversion** is the share of those who reached a step that went on to the next: `reached(next) / reached(this)`. It measures how well one step works and is the number to compare between segments.
- **Overall conversion** is the share of those who entered the funnel and reached the end: `reached(last) / reached(first)`. It is the product of all step conversions, which explains why a funnel with five steps of 70 percent each converts only 17 percent overall.
- **Drop-off** is the complement of step conversion, `1 - step conversion`, and the **number lost** is `reached(this) - reached(next)`. The count matters as much as the percentage, because a step that loses 40 percent of a small group may lose fewer people than a step that loses 10 percent of a large one.

### Finding the bottleneck

A **bottleneck** is the step that limits the whole funnel. Two measures help find it. The first is the lowest step conversion compared with a benchmark: your own history, another segment, or an industry figure that you have checked. The second is the size of the **opportunity**: a what-if calculation asking how many more people would reach the end if this step converted at the benchmark rate, and what they would be worth. Opportunity combines the drop-off with the number of people exposed to it, and it is what turns an observation into a priority. The what-if is not a forecast: raising a rate in practice costs effort and may be impossible, and fixing one step moves the problem to the next step.

### Segments and the misconception about averages

An overall funnel is an average over different groups, and averages hide problems. Mobile visitors may convert well up to checkout and then fail at payment because the form is hard to use on a small screen, while desktop visitors do not. Comparing step conversion by segment, as in the examples, shows this. A related trap is **mix**: if the share of mobile traffic rises (for instance because a campaign sends mobile users), the overall conversion falls even though no step got worse for any segment. Looking at segment funnels separates a real deterioration from a change in who is arriving (Day 144 showed the same idea for revenue mix).

### Why it matters, and what a funnel does not say

A funnel shows where in the process people leave. It does not say why they leave: a drop at checkout could be a surprise shipping cost, a broken button or a slow page, and each has a different fix. Pair the numbers with qualitative evidence (session recordings, surveys, support tickets) and with controlled tests. The misconception to avoid is treating those who dropped out as lost customers. Many return later through another session or a different device, and a strict window will count them as lost.

## Syntax and key functions

```python
reached = s[STEPS].sum()                       # counts per step
step_conv = reached / reached.shift(1)         # step conversion
overall = reached.iloc[-1] / reached.iloc[0]   # overall conversion
lost = reached.shift(1) - reached              # number lost at each step
```

```sql-syntax
SELECT COUNT(*) AS orders,
       COUNT(shipped_date) AS shipped,                                  -- counts non-NULL only
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE shipped_date <= required_date) AS on_time
FROM orders;
```

| Function | Returns |
|---|---|
| `df[cols].sum()` | Number of `True` values per boolean column. |
| `s.shift(1)` | The series moved down one place (the previous step). |
| `s.pct_change(fill_method=None)` | Relative change to the previous value. |
| `COUNT(col)` | Number of non-NULL values of `col`. |
| `COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE c)` | Rows meeting a condition. |
| `df.groupby(c)[cols].sum()` | Funnel counts per segment. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: A funnel table

Four thousand sessions are simulated (seeded) with one flag per step. The table shows how many reached each step, the conversion from the previous step, the conversion from the first step and the number lost.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 4000
s = pd.DataFrame({"device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop"], n, p=[0.6, 0.4])})
mob = (s["device"] == "mobile").to_numpy()
u = rng.random((4, n))
s["viewed"] = u[0] < np.where(mob, 0.55, 0.65)
s["carted"] = s["viewed"] & (u[1] < np.where(mob, 0.28, 0.34))
s["checkout"] = s["carted"] & (u[2] < np.where(mob, 0.60, 0.75))
s["paid"] = s["checkout"] & (u[3] < np.where(mob, 0.60, 0.85))
STEPS = ["viewed", "carted", "checkout", "paid"]
reached = s[STEPS].sum().astype(int)
t = pd.DataFrame({"reached": reached})
t["step_conv_pct"] = reached / reached.shift(1) * 100
t["of_all_pct"] = reached / len(s) * 100
t["lost"] = reached.shift(1) - reached
print("sessions:", len(s))
print(t.round(1).to_string())
print("counts never rise:", bool((reached.diff().dropna() <= 0).all()))
```

```output
sessions: 4000
          reached  step_conv_pct  of_all_pct    lost
viewed       2343            NaN        58.6     NaN
carted        715           30.5        17.9  1628.0
checkout      478           66.9        12.0   237.0
paid          338           70.7         8.5   140.0
counts never rise: True
```

The weakest step conversion is the one from viewing a product to adding it to the cart, but the largest number lost in absolute terms is the same step. The "counts never rise" line is the sanity check described above.

### Example 2: Funnels by device

Counts are grouped by device and converted to step conversion. The step conversions for mobile and desktop are placed side by side.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 4000
s = pd.DataFrame({"device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop"], n, p=[0.6, 0.4])})
mob = (s["device"] == "mobile").to_numpy()
u = rng.random((4, n))
s["viewed"] = u[0] < np.where(mob, 0.55, 0.65)
s["carted"] = s["viewed"] & (u[1] < np.where(mob, 0.28, 0.34))
s["checkout"] = s["carted"] & (u[2] < np.where(mob, 0.60, 0.75))
s["paid"] = s["checkout"] & (u[3] < np.where(mob, 0.60, 0.85))
STEPS = ["viewed", "carted", "checkout", "paid"]
by = s.groupby("device")[STEPS].sum()
sessions = s.groupby("device").size()
conv = pd.DataFrame({
    "sessions": sessions,
    "view_pct": by["viewed"] / sessions * 100,
    "cart_pct": by["carted"] / by["viewed"] * 100,
    "checkout_pct": by["checkout"] / by["carted"] * 100,
    "pay_pct": by["paid"] / by["checkout"] * 100,
    "overall_pct": by["paid"] / sessions * 100,
})
print(conv.round(1).to_string())
```

```output
         sessions  view_pct  cart_pct  checkout_pct  pay_pct  overall_pct
device                                                                   
desktop      1612      65.8      33.9          75.6     84.2         14.2
mobile       2388      53.7      27.7          58.0     52.9          4.6
```

Mobile converts worse at every step, and the gap is widest at payment. The overall conversion of a mixed audience is somewhere between the two, and moves with the share of mobile traffic.

### Example 3: An order-fulfilment funnel in SQL

The same logic applies to operations. Every order is placed. `COUNT(shipped_date)` counts only the orders with a shipping date (the others are NULL), and a `FILTER` counts those shipped by the required date. Percentages divide by the previous step.

```sql run
SELECT EXTRACT(year FROM order_date)::int AS yr,
       COUNT(*) AS placed,
       COUNT(shipped_date) AS shipped,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE shipped_date <= required_date) AS on_time,
       ROUND((100.0 * COUNT(shipped_date) / COUNT(*))::numeric, 1) AS shipped_pct,
       ROUND((100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE shipped_date <= required_date) / COUNT(shipped_date))::numeric, 1) AS on_time_of_shipped_pct
FROM orders
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
```

```output
yr | placed | shipped | on_time | shipped_pct | on_time_of_shipped_pct
1996 | 34 | 29 | 26 | 85.3 | 89.7
1997 | 33 | 28 | 26 | 84.8 | 92.9
1998 | 33 | 28 | 25 | 84.8 | 89.3
```

Orders with no shipping date are the leak at the first step; late shipments are the leak at the second. Note that the denominators differ: on-time is measured against shipped orders, not against placed orders, which is the step conversion rule.

### Example 4: Sizing the bottleneck

What if mobile payment converted like desktop payment? The code applies the desktop payment rate to the mobile checkouts and values the extra orders at an assumed average order value. The assumption is a variable at the top, so it can be challenged.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 4000
s = pd.DataFrame({"device": rng.choice(["mobile", "desktop"], n, p=[0.6, 0.4])})
mob = (s["device"] == "mobile").to_numpy()
u = rng.random((4, n))
s["viewed"] = u[0] < np.where(mob, 0.55, 0.65)
s["carted"] = s["viewed"] & (u[1] < np.where(mob, 0.28, 0.34))
s["checkout"] = s["carted"] & (u[2] < np.where(mob, 0.60, 0.75))
s["paid"] = s["checkout"] & (u[3] < np.where(mob, 0.60, 0.85))
STEPS = ["viewed", "carted", "checkout", "paid"]
by = s.groupby("device")[STEPS].sum()
pay_rate = by["paid"] / by["checkout"]
mobile_checkouts = int(by.loc["mobile", "checkout"])
extra = mobile_checkouts * float(pay_rate["desktop"] - pay_rate["mobile"])
aov = 45.0
print("mobile payment rate:", round(float(pay_rate["mobile"]) * 100, 1), "% | desktop:", round(float(pay_rate["desktop"]) * 100, 1), "%")
print("mobile checkouts:", mobile_checkouts)
print("extra paid orders if equal:", round(extra), "| extra revenue at AOV", aov, ":", round(extra * aov))
print("share of all paid orders this represents:", round(extra / int(by["paid"].sum()) * 100, 1), "%")
```

```output
mobile payment rate: 52.9 % | desktop: 84.2 %
mobile checkouts: 206
extra paid orders if equal: 64 | extra revenue at AOV 45.0 : 2900
share of all paid orders this represents: 19.1 %
```

This is a ceiling, not a forecast. It says that fixing mobile payment is worth looking at, and it is compared with the same calculation for other steps to set priorities.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- A funnel is ordered steps counting the same unit over a defined time window.
- Step conversion divides by the previous step; overall conversion divides by the first; both matter.
- Count the people lost as well as the percentage: opportunity is rate times exposure.
- Segment the funnel, because averages hide the segment where a step fails and mix changes move the overall rate.
- A funnel shows where people leave, not why.

### Common mistakes

- Dividing every step by the first step and calling it step conversion. The weak step is then hidden; divide by the previous step.
- Counting with `COUNT(*)` where `COUNT(col)` was intended, so NULL dates are counted as shipped.
- Ignoring a count that rises down the funnel. It signals a tracking gap or a different unit; fix the data first.
- Comparing two periods with different traffic mixes and blaming a step. Compare within segments.
- Treating the what-if as a forecast. It is an upper bound under an assumption.

### Revision notes

- Step conversion = reached(next) / reached(this); overall = last / first.
- Lost at a step = reached(this) - reached(next).
- `COUNT(col)` ignores NULL; `COUNT(*)` counts rows.
- What-if = exposed x (benchmark rate - current rate) x value per success.
