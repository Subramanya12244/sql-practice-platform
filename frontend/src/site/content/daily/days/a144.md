- Explain in business language what a revenue change means and which decision it supports.

## Understand

### What revenue is, and what the number hides

**Revenue** is the money a business earns from selling goods or services in a period. In an order database the safest definition is built from the lowest level of detail: for each order line, `unit_price * quantity * (1 - discount)`, summed over the lines you want. Notice the words "price at sale". Northwind stores a price on every order line (`order_details.unit_price`) and another one on the product (`products.unit_price`, the current list price). Historic revenue must use the line price, because the list price may have changed since the sale. Using the wrong one is the most common silent error in revenue reports: the report runs, the number looks plausible, and it is wrong.

A single revenue total hides almost everything that a manager needs. It does not say whether the business sold more things, sold the same things at higher prices, or shifted toward more expensive products. It does not say whether this quarter was strong or merely normal for the season. That is why revenue analytics is mostly about **decomposition**: splitting one number into parts that each have a different cause and a different owner.

### Revenue as a product of drivers

The first decomposition is the one on any dashboard: `revenue = orders * average order value`. The **average order value** (AOV) is revenue divided by the number of orders. If revenue falls, one of the two factors fell (or both). A marketing team owns the number of orders; a merchandising or pricing team owns AOV. A change of 10 percent in revenue can come from 10 percent fewer orders at the same AOV or from the same orders at a lower AOV, and the right follow-up question is different in each case. One more split goes one level down: `AOV = units per order * average price per unit`.

### Price, volume and mix

When a business sells several products, the second decomposition separates three effects between two periods:

- **Price effect**: the same units sold at a different price. Formula: the sum over products of `Q1 * (P1 - P0)`, where `Q` is units, `P` is price, `0` is the earlier and `1` the later period.
- **Volume effect**: the change in the total number of units, valued at the earlier average price: `(Qtotal1 - Qtotal0) * average price0`.
- **Mix effect**: the rest of the change in units, caused by the proportions between products changing. Selling the same total units but more of an expensive product raises revenue with no price change at all.

The three effects add up exactly to the revenue change, which is the check that you calculated them correctly. The distinction matters for decisions. A price effect says "customers accepted the price rise" (or that a discount was given). A volume effect says "demand grew". A mix effect says "customers traded up or down", which is a product-strategy question.

### Growth: what to compare with what

**Growth** is `(current - previous) / previous`, expressed as a percentage. The harder part is choosing the previous period. Comparing a quarter with the preceding quarter (quarter-over-quarter, QoQ) mixes growth with seasonality: a retailer will look terrible in January after December. Comparing with the same quarter a year earlier (year-over-year, YoY) removes the seasonal pattern because both periods have the same season. In SQL the previous-year value for a quarterly series is `LAG(revenue, 4)`, four rows back, and this only works when no quarter is missing from the series. If a quarter has no sales it has no row, and the offset silently points at the wrong quarter. Always check that the series is complete before using an offset.

Two cautions apply to percentages. Growth from a small base looks enormous (from 2 to 6 orders is 200 percent), so show the absolute change next to it. And percentages of percentages are confusing: say "points" for the difference between two rates.

### Seasonality

**Seasonality** is a pattern that repeats at a fixed interval, such as a peak every December. A simple measure is the **seasonal index** for each month: the average revenue of that calendar month divided by the average revenue of all months. An index of 1.30 means that month is normally 30 percent above average; 0.80 means 20 percent below. With an index you can compare an actual month with what is normal for it: revenue 10 percent below average in a month whose index is 0.80 is good news. You need at least two or three full years to estimate it, because with one year you cannot tell a seasonal pattern from a one-off event.

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Revenue meetings turn on the question "why did it change?", and an analyst who can only say "it went up 12 percent" gets the follow-up "so what?". The misconception to avoid is that growth is good in itself. Revenue can grow because of deep discounts that destroy margin, or fall because the business deliberately dropped a low-quality product line. Revenue is a starting point for the question, not the conclusion. In this lesson the data is the small synthetic Northwind (100 orders from 1996 to 1998), so the patterns are real arithmetic but the business story is only an exercise.

## Syntax and key functions

```sql-syntax
SELECT EXTRACT(year FROM order_date)::int AS yr,
       SUM(unit_price * quantity * (1 - discount)) AS revenue
FROM orders JOIN order_details USING (order_id)
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;

LAG(revenue, 4) OVER (ORDER BY yr, q)       -- same quarter, previous year
```

```python
price  = (q1 * (p1 - p0)).sum()
volume = (q1.sum() - q0.sum()) * (r0 / q0.sum())
mix    = (q1 - q0).mul(p0).sum() - volume
index  = monthly.groupby("month").mean() / monthly.mean()
```

| Function or formula | Returns |
|---|---|
| `EXTRACT(year FROM d)` | The year (or quarter, month) part of a date. |
| `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)` | Orders, not order lines, after a join to order lines. |
| `LAG(x, n) OVER (ORDER BY ...)` | The value `n` rows earlier, `NULL` when none exists. |
| `NULLIF(x, 0)` | `NULL` when `x` is zero; protects a division. |
| `(new - old) / old` | Growth rate of one period against another. |
| `groupby(...).mean() / mean` | Seasonal index per calendar month. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Revenue, orders and average order value by year

The join multiplies each order by its lines, so `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)` is needed to count orders. Revenue uses the line price. AOV is revenue divided by orders, and units per order shows the next level down.

```sql run
SELECT EXTRACT(year FROM o.order_date)::int AS yr,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS orders,
       ROUND(SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount))::numeric, 0) AS revenue,
       ROUND((SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) / COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id))::numeric, 0) AS aov,
       ROUND(SUM(od.quantity)::numeric / COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id), 1) AS units_per_order
FROM orders o
JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
```

```output
yr | orders | revenue | aov | units_per_order
1996 | 34 | 52325 | 1539 | 52.0
1997 | 33 | 40059 | 1214 | 49.8
1998 | 33 | 45936 | 1392 | 50.5
```

Orders per year are almost flat, so the movement between years comes from the average order value, not from how many orders were placed.

### Example 2: Year-over-year growth by quarter

A CTE computes quarterly revenue, then `LAG(revenue, 4)` fetches the same quarter one year earlier. The outer query keeps 1997 and 1998, where the previous year exists. The series has all twelve quarters, so the offset is safe. `NULLIF` guards the division.

```sql run
WITH q AS (
  SELECT EXTRACT(year FROM o.order_date)::int AS yr,
         EXTRACT(quarter FROM o.order_date)::int AS qtr,
         SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) AS revenue
  FROM orders o JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
  GROUP BY 1, 2
), g AS (
  SELECT yr, qtr, revenue,
         LAG(revenue, 4) OVER (ORDER BY yr, qtr) AS prior_year
  FROM q
)
SELECT yr, qtr,
       ROUND(revenue::numeric, 0) AS revenue,
       ROUND(prior_year::numeric, 0) AS prior_year,
       ROUND(((revenue - prior_year) / NULLIF(prior_year, 0) * 100)::numeric, 1) AS yoy_pct
FROM g
WHERE yr >= 1997
ORDER BY yr, qtr;
```

```output
yr | qtr | revenue | prior_year | yoy_pct
1997 | 1 | 7451 | 5217 | 42.8
1997 | 2 | 3460 | 18574 | -81.4
1997 | 3 | 17802 | 19336 | -7.9
1997 | 4 | 11346 | 9197 | 23.4
1998 | 1 | 15449 | 7451 | 107.3
1998 | 2 | 8687 | 3460 | 151.1
1998 | 3 | 3323 | 17802 | -81.3
1998 | 4 | 18477 | 11346 | 62.8
```

The percentages swing widely in both directions, which is what a very small, synthetic business with 8 or 9 orders per quarter looks like. In real data you would expect smoother numbers. Notice that no seasonal pattern is visible between years: the quarter with the highest revenue changes each year.

### Example 3: Price, volume and mix on three products

Two periods, three products, with units and prices written out. The code computes the three effects and checks that they add up to the revenue change. Product A and product C raised their prices, and the units moved toward the cheaper product B.

```python run
import pandas as pd

d = pd.DataFrame({
    "product": ["A", "B", "C"],
    "q0": [100, 50, 50], "p0": [10.0, 20.0, 40.0],
    "q1": [95, 70, 45], "p1": [11.0, 20.0, 45.0],
}).set_index("product")

r0 = float((d["q0"] * d["p0"]).sum())
r1 = float((d["q1"] * d["p1"]).sum())
price = float((d["q1"] * (d["p1"] - d["p0"])).sum())
volume = float((d["q1"].sum() - d["q0"].sum()) * r0 / d["q0"].sum())
mix = float(((d["q1"] - d["q0"]) * d["p0"]).sum()) - volume

print("revenue before:", r0, "after:", r1, "change:", r1 - r0)
print("price effect:", round(price, 1))
print("volume effect:", round(volume, 1))
print("mix effect:", round(mix, 1))
print("sum of effects:", round(price + volume + mix, 1))
```

```output
revenue before: 4000.0 after: 4470.0 change: 470.0
price effect: 320.0
volume effect: 200.0
mix effect: -50.0
sum of effects: 470.0
```

The change is split into three parts that add up exactly. Price and volume are positive. The mix effect is negative because the extra units went mostly to product B, which is cheaper than the earlier average price of 20, while the dearest product C sold fewer units than before.

### Example 4: A seasonal index

Three years of monthly revenue are generated from a seasonal pattern plus noise (seeded, so the output is stable). The index of each calendar month is the average for that month divided by the overall average. Then the latest month is compared with what is normal for it.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
months = pd.period_range("2023-01", periods=36, freq="M")
season = np.array([0.85, 0.80, 0.90, 0.95, 1.00, 1.00, 0.95, 0.95, 1.00, 1.05, 1.20, 1.35])
base = 100 + np.arange(36) * 0.8
revenue = pd.Series(base * np.tile(season, 3) * (1 + rng.normal(0, 0.03, 36)), index=months).round(1)

monthly = pd.DataFrame({"revenue": revenue})
monthly["month"] = monthly.index.month
index = monthly.groupby("month")["revenue"].mean() / monthly["revenue"].mean()
print(index.round(2).to_string())
last = revenue.iloc[-1]
print("last month revenue:", round(float(last), 1), "| index of its month:", round(float(index.loc[12]), 2))
print("share above the overall average:", round(float(last / revenue.mean() - 1), 2))
```

```output
month
1     0.83
2     0.78
3     0.86
4     0.94
5     0.98
6     0.99
7     0.93
8     0.96
9     0.99
10    1.07
11    1.25
12    1.41
last month revenue: 168.6 | index of its month: 1.41
share above the overall average: 0.49
```

December's index is about 1.4, so a high December is normal. The index also contains a little of the upward trend, because later months have higher base values and every calendar month appears in every year equally. With a strong trend a better method removes the trend first, and the simple index would then be misleading.

## Real-world applications

- **Monthly business review**: a revenue bridge of price, volume and mix tells a leadership team whether growth came from demand, from a price rise or from customers moving to dearer products, so that each owner explains their own part.
- **Pricing and promotions**: if the price effect is positive and the volume effect is negative, the business can see directly what a price increase cost in units and shows how sensitive demand is to price.
- **Planning and staffing**: a seasonal index converts an annual target into monthly targets and tells a warehouse how many temporary staff to hire for the peak.
- **Performance reporting**: using YoY instead of QoQ stops a normal post-holiday dip from triggering an unnecessary investigation, and the AOV and order count split shows which team to ask first.

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Revenue is `unit_price * quantity * (1 - discount)` taken from the order lines at the price at the time of sale.
- Revenue equals orders times AOV; one of the two changed whenever revenue changes.
- Price, volume and mix effects add up exactly to the change in revenue, which is your check.
- Compare with the same period a year ago to remove seasonality, and make sure the series has no missing periods before using `LAG` with an offset.
- Show absolute changes next to percentages, and tie every change to the team and decision it concerns.

### Common mistakes

- Using `products.unit_price` for historic revenue. The query runs but the numbers are wrong; use `order_details.unit_price`.
- Counting orders with `COUNT(*)` after joining to order lines. The count is the number of lines; use `COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)`.
- Using `LAG(x, 4)` on a series with a missing quarter. Growth is then computed against the wrong period; build the full calendar first or join to a calendar table.
- Quoting a huge percentage from a tiny base. Report the absolute numbers too.
- Treating a seasonal low as a decline. Compare with the seasonal index or the same period last year.

### Revision notes

- Orders by AOV, then units by price: `revenue = orders * units_per_order * price_per_unit`.
- `price = sum(q1 * (p1 - p0))`, `volume = (Q1 - Q0) * average p0`, mix is the remainder.
- `yoy = (x - x_last_year) / x_last_year`.
- `index = month average / overall average`.
