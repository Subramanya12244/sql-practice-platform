## Understand

### The question a t-test answers

On Day 89 you built a confidence interval for a mean, and on Day 90 you met the logic of a hypothesis test: assume a **null hypothesis** (no difference or no effect), then ask how surprising the observed data would be if the null were true. The **t-test** is the standard test for questions about means. Is the average delivery time different from the 27-minute target? Do customers in region A spend a different amount from customers in region B? Did the average basket change after a redesign for the same customers? The three questions correspond to the three versions of the test.

The **t statistic** has the same form in every version:

`t = (observed difference - hypothesised difference) / standard error of the difference`

It measures how many standard errors the observed difference lies from the value the null hypothesis says it should have. A t near 0 means the data agree with the null; a t far from 0 (roughly beyond ±2 for moderate samples) means they do not. The standard error uses the sample standard deviation, which is itself an estimate, and this makes the sampling distribution slightly wider than the normal curve. It is called the **t distribution**, and its width depends on the **degrees of freedom** (df), a number tied to the sample size. With a small sample the tails are heavier, with a large one it matches the normal curve.

### The p-value, carefully

The **p-value** is the probability, calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed. A p-value of 0.02 means: if there really were no difference, data this extreme would turn up about 2% of the time. It is **not** the probability that the null is true, **not** the probability that the result happened by chance, and **not** the size or importance of the effect. A small p-value says the data are hard to reconcile with the null; a large one says only that the data do not rule it out, which is not the same as proving no difference. A **significance level** (alpha, often 0.05) is the cut-off chosen before the test. Rejecting the null when it is actually true is the Type I error from Day 90, and its long-run rate is alpha.

A test is **two-sided** when you care about a difference in either direction (the default) and **one-sided** when only one direction matters and was chosen before looking at the data. A one-sided p-value is half the two-sided one when the data lean in the chosen direction, which makes it tempting to switch after the fact. Do not.

### One-sample t-test

The one-sample test compares the mean of one group with a fixed number. The difference is `mean - target`, and the standard error is `s / sqrt(n)`. The degrees of freedom are `n - 1`. It answers "is the average different from the target?", and the confidence interval for the mean tells you the plausible range for the true average.

### Two-sample t-test

The two-sample (independent) test compares the means of two separate groups, such as two regions. The difference is `mean_B - mean_A`. The standard error combines the two groups' variances. There are two versions. **Welch's t-test** does not assume the groups have the same variance and adjusts the degrees of freedom; it is a safe default. **Student's (pooled) t-test** assumes equal variances and pools them into one estimate. When the spreads and the sample sizes are different, the two give different answers, and the pooled version can be misleading. In `scipy` the argument `equal_var=False` selects Welch's.

### Paired t-test

Sometimes the two sets of measurements belong together, such as the same customers before and after a redesign, or two methods applied to the same orders. Then each row has a natural partner, and the right analysis works on the **differences**: compute `after - before` for each pair and run a one-sample test of whether the mean difference is 0. Pairing removes the variation between customers, which is often the largest source of noise, so it is more sensitive. Using an independent test on paired data throws that advantage away and can miss a real effect. Using a paired test on independent groups is just as wrong: it requires a true one-to-one pairing.

### Assumptions and how to check them

- **Independence.** The observations must not influence one another. Repeated measures of the same customer break this for an independent test, which is why the paired version exists. No statistic can repair a design that violates independence.
- **Roughly normal data, or a large enough sample.** The test relies on the sampling distribution of the mean being roughly normal. By the central limit theorem (Day 89) this holds for moderate samples even if the data are mildly skewed, but strongly skewed data with small samples are a problem. Look at a histogram or compare mean and median, and use `scipy.stats.shapiro` as a rough numerical check (a small p-value signals departure from normality). For heavily skewed or outlier-ridden data, a transformation such as the logarithm, or a rank-based test, may be more suitable.
- **Similar spread** for the pooled test only. Compare the standard deviations or use `scipy.stats.levene`. Using Welch's test avoids the issue.

### Effect size and confidence intervals

A p-value mixes the size of the effect with the sample size. With 5,000 rows per group, a difference of half a unit on a standard deviation of 15 produces a tiny p-value even though the difference is trivial. An **effect size** states the difference in a way that does not depend on the sample size. **Cohen's d** divides the difference between means by the pooled standard deviation. By convention, 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large, but the useful threshold depends on the business. For paired data, `d` can be computed as the mean difference divided by the standard deviation of the differences. Also report the **confidence interval** for the difference, which shows both direction and size with the uncertainty. "Statistically significant" is never the same as "important": always ask how large the difference is in the units of the business, and whether the interval excludes sizes that matter.

### Why it matters

Analysts are constantly asked "is this difference real?" The t-test gives a disciplined answer, provided that the design (independent or paired), the assumptions and the effect size are reported with the p-value.

## Syntax and key functions

```python
from scipy import stats

stats.ttest_1samp(sample, popmean)                       # one sample vs a number
stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)                   # Welch, two independent samples
stats.ttest_ind(a, b)                                    # Student, assumes equal variance
stats.ttest_rel(after, before)                           # paired
stats.ttest_1samp(sample, popmean, alternative="greater")  # one-sided
stats.shapiro(sample)                                    # normality check
stats.levene(a, b)                                       # equal-variance check
stats.t.ppf(0.975, df)                                   # critical value for a 95% interval
```

| Function | Returns |
|---|---|
| `ttest_1samp`, `ttest_ind`, `ttest_rel` | An object with `.statistic` and `.pvalue`; the order of `a` and `b` sets the sign of t. |
| `alternative=` | `"two-sided"` (default), `"greater"` or `"less"`. |
| `stats.shapiro(x)` | Statistic and p-value; a small p-value suggests non-normality. |
| `stats.levene(a, b)` | Statistic and p-value for equal variances. |
| `stats.t.ppf(q, df)` | The t value below which a share `q` of the distribution lies. |

Cohen's d for two groups, from the pooled standard deviation:

```python
sp = (((na - 1) * a.var(ddof=1) + (nb - 1) * b.var(ddof=1)) / (na + nb - 2)) ** 0.5
d = (b.mean() - a.mean()) / sp
```

## Learn by example

### Example 1: One-sample test, by hand and with scipy

Delivery times in minutes for 25 orders, simulated with a seed. The target is 27 minutes.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
t = rng.normal(33, 8, 25).round(1)
n, mean, s = len(t), t.mean(), t.std(ddof=1)
by_hand = (mean - 27) / (s / np.sqrt(n))
res = stats.ttest_1samp(t, 27)
print(round(mean, 2), round(s, 2))
print(round(by_hand, 3), round(res.statistic, 3), round(res.pvalue, 4))
print(round(stats.ttest_1samp(t, 27, alternative="greater").pvalue, 4))
tc = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1)
print(round(mean - tc * s / np.sqrt(n), 2), round(mean + tc * s / np.sqrt(n), 2))
```

```output
30.05 6.39
2.388 2.388 0.0252
0.0126
27.41 32.69
```

The mean is 30.05 minutes with a standard deviation of 6.39. The t statistic, 2.39 by hand and with `scipy`, says the mean lies 2.39 standard errors above the target. The two-sided p-value is 0.0252: if the true mean were 27, a result this far from 27 in either direction would occur about 2.5% of the time. The one-sided test (is it *greater*?) has half the p-value, 0.0126. The 95% confidence interval for the true mean is 27.41 to 32.69 minutes: it excludes 27, which agrees with rejecting the null at the 5% level. It does not say how important a delay of about 3 minutes is; that is a business question.

### Example 2: Two independent groups, Welch against pooled

Order values in two regions. Region B has a larger mean and a much larger spread.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
a = rng.normal(50, 10, 40).round(1)
b = rng.normal(56, 20, 35).round(1)
print(round(a.mean(), 2), round(b.mean(), 2), round(a.std(ddof=1), 2), round(b.std(ddof=1), 2))
w = stats.ttest_ind(b, a, equal_var=False)
s = stats.ttest_ind(b, a)
print(round(w.statistic, 3), round(w.pvalue, 4))
print(round(s.statistic, 3), round(s.pvalue, 4))
by_hand = (b.mean() - a.mean()) / np.sqrt(a.var(ddof=1) / len(a) + b.var(ddof=1) / len(b))
print(round(by_hand, 3))
print(round(stats.levene(a, b).pvalue, 4))
```

```output
46.05 56.25 8.21 18.63
2.994 0.0044
3.134 0.0025
2.994
0.0001
```

Region A averages 46.05 (standard deviation 8.21) and region B 56.25 (18.63). Welch's t is 2.994 with a p-value of 0.0044, and its formula, applied by hand, gives the same value. The pooled Student test gives 3.134 and 0.0025: it is a little more confident because it wrongly shares one variance between groups that are very different. Levene's test for equal variances has a p-value of 0.0001, a clear sign that the spreads differ, so Welch's test is the right choice here. Swapping `a` and `b` would only flip the sign of t.

### Example 3: Paired against independent

The same 30 customers' weekly spend before and after a redesign.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
before = rng.normal(100, 15, 30).round(1)
after = (before + rng.normal(3, 4, 30)).round(1)
paired = stats.ttest_rel(after, before)
independent = stats.ttest_ind(after, before)
print(round(paired.statistic, 3), round(paired.pvalue, 4))
print(round(independent.statistic, 3), round(independent.pvalue, 4))
diff = after - before
print(round(diff.mean(), 3), round(diff.std(ddof=1), 3), round(diff.mean() / diff.std(ddof=1), 3))
print(round(stats.ttest_1samp(diff, 0).statistic, 3))
```

```output
4.509 0.0001
0.917 0.3631
2.98 3.62 0.823
4.509
```

The average customer spent 2.98 more after the redesign. The paired test has t = 4.509 and p = 0.0001, a clear effect. The independent test on the same numbers gives t = 0.917 and p = 0.3631: it treats the customers as two unrelated groups, so the large differences between customers (a standard deviation of about 15) swamp the small, consistent shift. A paired test is exactly a one-sample test on the differences, which the last line confirms by returning the same t. The effect size for the paired data is 2.98 / 3.62 = 0.823, a large effect measured against the variation in the changes.

### Example 4: Significant is not the same as important

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
g1 = rng.normal(100, 15, 5000)
g2 = rng.normal(100.5, 15, 5000)
r = stats.ttest_ind(g2, g1)
pooled = np.sqrt((g1.var(ddof=1) + g2.var(ddof=1)) / 2)
print(round(r.statistic, 3), round(r.pvalue, 4))
print(round((g2.mean() - g1.mean()) / pooled, 3))
print(round(g2.mean() - g1.mean(), 2))
```

```output
2.359 0.0184
0.047
0.7
```

With 5,000 rows per group the test returns t = 2.359 and p = 0.0184, significant at the 5% level. Cohen's d is 0.047, far below the "small" convention of 0.2, and the observed difference in means is 0.70 on a base of 100, less than 1%. The p-value is small because the sample is large, not because the effect is big. Report the difference and its interval, and decide whether a 0.7% change matters to the business.

### Example 5: Checking assumptions

A roughly normal sample and a strongly right-skewed sample, with Shapiro-Wilk p-values.

```python run
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
normal = rng.normal(10, 2, 40)
skewed = -np.log(rng.random(40)) * 5
print(round(stats.shapiro(normal).pvalue, 4), round(stats.shapiro(skewed).pvalue, 4))
print(round(float(np.mean(skewed) - np.median(skewed)), 2) > 0)
logged = np.log(skewed)
print(round(stats.shapiro(logged).pvalue, 4))
```

```output
0.9505 0.0008
True
0.0322
```

The normal sample has a Shapiro p-value of 0.9505 (no evidence against normality). The exponential-like sample has 0.0008, which signals clear skewness; its mean is above its median (the third line is `True`), the usual footprint of right skew. After a log transform the p-value is 0.0322: better than 0.0008 but still below 0.05, so the transform helped without making the sample look normal. A t-test on logged values would compare typical values on a multiplicative scale instead of arithmetic means. Normality tests have little power with small samples and flag trivial departures in large ones, so use them together with a histogram and the sample size.

## Real-world applications

- **Checking a service target.** A one-sample test with a confidence interval shows whether average delivery or response time differs from the agreed target, and by how much (Example 1).
- **Comparing segments.** Welch's test compares average order value across regions or channels without assuming the same variability (Example 2).
- **Before-and-after studies.** A paired test measures the effect of a redesign or a policy on the same customers, with more power than an independent comparison (Example 3).
- **Reporting honestly.** Giving the effect size and interval with the p-value prevents a large sample from turning a trivial difference into a headline (Example 4).

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- `t = (observed - hypothesised) / standard error`; the p-value is how surprising the data are if the null were true.
- Use one-sample for a target, Welch for two independent groups, paired for matched measurements (a one-sample test on differences).
- Check independence, rough normality or a large sample, and (for the pooled test) similar variances.
- Report an effect size such as Cohen's d and a confidence interval, not only the p-value.
- A small p-value does not mean a large or important effect, and a large p-value does not prove no difference.

### Common mistakes

- Reading the p-value as the probability that the null is true or that the result is "due to chance".
- Running an independent test on paired data, so that a real effect is missed, or a paired test on unrelated groups.
- Using the pooled test when the two spreads clearly differ; use `equal_var=False`.
- Choosing a one-sided test after seeing which way the data lean.
- Treating "not significant" as "no effect" in a small sample.
- Calling a tiny effect important because the p-value is below 0.05 in a huge sample.

### Revision notes

- `stats.ttest_1samp(x, mu)`, `stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)`, `stats.ttest_rel(after, before)`.
- Cohen's d = difference in means / pooled standard deviation; paired: mean difference / standard deviation of differences.
- 95% interval: `mean ± stats.t.ppf(0.975, df) * se`.
- `stats.shapiro` and `stats.levene` as rough assumption checks.
