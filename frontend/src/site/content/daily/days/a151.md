## Understand

### Margin: what is left after the goods

**Revenue** is the money from sales, **cost of goods sold** (COGS) is what the goods that were sold cost the business to buy or make, and **gross profit** is the difference: `gross profit = revenue - COGS`. **Gross margin** expresses it as a share of revenue: `gross margin = gross profit / revenue`. A gross margin of 35 percent means that out of each 100 of sales, 35 remain to pay for staff, rent, marketing and profit. Gross margin is not net profit, because the costs below the gross profit line are not in it. Use the right name: **markup** is gross profit divided by cost (35 on a cost of 65 is a markup of 54 percent), and people confuse it with margin. The two are different numbers for the same product, so always say which one you mean.

Margin must be calculated on the right base. A blended margin for several products is the total gross profit divided by the total revenue, not the average of the individual margins, because a product with large sales should count more (Day 149 showed how a change of mix alone moves the blended margin). Discounts matter too: revenue is net of discounts, so a discount lowers margin directly.

### Budget, actual and variance

A **budget** is the plan for a period, with a target value for each line: revenue, costs, profit. The **actual** is what happened. The **variance** is the difference: `variance = actual - budget`, and **variance percent** is `variance / budget`. A budget is a benchmark, not a forecast of the truth, so a variance says "different from plan", not "good" or "bad". Whether it is good depends on the line.

### Favourable and unfavourable

The sign of the variance does not tell whether it is good, and finance teams use the labels **favourable** and **unfavourable** to avoid errors. For a revenue or profit line, actual above budget is favourable. For a cost line, actual above budget is unfavourable, because spending more than planned reduces profit. A report that colours all positive variances green will show an overspend in green. The rule, which you should write in the report, is: favourable when the variance increases profit, unfavourable when it reduces profit. A table with `variance` and a separate `effect on profit` column (variance for revenue, minus variance for cost) removes the ambiguity.

### Variance analysis: finding the cause

A single profit variance of minus 2,000 is not explainable until it is divided into causes. A common split for a product line uses units sold `Q`, price `P` and unit cost `C`, with 0 for budget and 1 for actual:

- **Volume variance**: `(Q1 - Q0) * (P0 - C0)`, the effect of selling more or fewer units at the budgeted unit margin.
- **Price variance**: `Q1 * (P1 - P0)`, the effect of charging a different price on the actual units.
- **Cost variance**: `-Q1 * (C1 - C0)`, the effect of unit costs being different from plan on the actual units (a higher cost is negative).

These three add up exactly to `actual profit - budget profit`, which is the check that the analysis is complete. For a business with several products there is also a mix effect (Day 144). The order of calculation matters a little: the price and cost effects are measured on actual units, and the volume effect at budget margin. If you reverse the order the individual numbers change slightly, although the total does not, so state the convention you used.

### Waterfall explanations

A **waterfall** (or bridge) chart starts with the budget profit, adds each variance as a step up or down, and ends at the actual profit. Its purpose is communication: in one picture a reader sees which factors added and which took away. Without a chart tool the same thing is a table with a running total: each row is a step and the cumulative column is where the bar ends. The ending total must equal the actual figure. Order the steps from the biggest effect to the smallest, or in the order of the profit statement, and label each step in words that a manager understands ("units were 10 percent below plan" rather than "volume variance"). A waterfall explains "what", and the next step in the analysis is "why".

### Why it matters, and the misconception

Budget reviews are one of the most frequent places where an analyst's work is read by senior people, and they ask the same three questions: how far are we from plan, which lines explain it, and what are we doing about it. The misconception is that a small total variance means nothing happened. Large offsetting variances can sum to nearly zero: a volume shortfall hidden by a price rise is a warning, not reassurance. Always look at the components, not only the total.

## Syntax and key functions

```sql-syntax
WITH budget(qtr, budget) AS (VALUES (1, 8000), (2, 8000))
SELECT a.qtr, a.actual, b.budget,
       a.actual - b.budget AS variance
FROM actual a JOIN budget b USING (qtr);
```

```python
gross_margin = (revenue - cogs) / revenue
variance = actual - budget
volume = (q1 - q0) * (p0 - c0)
price = q1 * (p1 - p0)
cost = -q1 * (c1 - c0)
```

| Function or formula | Returns |
|---|---|
| `(revenue - cogs) / revenue` | Gross margin. |
| `gross profit / cogs` | Markup (not the same as margin). |
| `actual - budget` | Variance. |
| `variance / budget` | Variance percent. |
| `VALUES (..), (..)` | An inline table of literal rows, used here for a budget. |
| `s.cumsum()` | Running total, used for the waterfall. |

## Learn by example

### Example 1: Gross profit, margin and markup by product

Three products have revenue and cost of goods sold. The code computes gross profit, margin and markup for each, and then the blended margin, which is not the mean of the three margins.

```python run
import pandas as pd

d = pd.DataFrame({
    "product": ["A", "B", "C"],
    "revenue": [50000.0, 30000.0, 20000.0],
    "cogs": [30000.0, 21000.0, 8000.0],
}).set_index("product")
d["gross_profit"] = d["revenue"] - d["cogs"]
d["margin_pct"] = d["gross_profit"] / d["revenue"] * 100
d["markup_pct"] = d["gross_profit"] / d["cogs"] * 100
print(d.round(1).to_string())
total = d[["revenue", "cogs", "gross_profit"]].sum()
print("blended margin:", round(float(total["gross_profit"] / total["revenue"] * 100), 1), "%")
print("mean of margins:", round(float(d["margin_pct"].mean()), 1), "%")
```

```output
         revenue     cogs  gross_profit  margin_pct  markup_pct
product                                                        
A        50000.0  30000.0       20000.0        40.0        66.7
B        30000.0  21000.0        9000.0        30.0        42.9
C        20000.0   8000.0       12000.0        60.0       150.0
blended margin: 41.0 %
mean of margins: 43.3 %
```

The margin and the markup differ for every product. The blended margin is 41 percent, while the simple mean of the three margins is 43.3 percent. The mean is higher because product C has the highest margin (60 percent) but the smallest sales, and a simple mean gives it the same weight as the large product A.

### Example 2: Budget against actual in SQL

The 1997 quarterly revenue of Northwind is computed from the order lines, and a budget is supplied as an inline `VALUES` table. The variance and variance percent are computed in the final query, with the label in words. Revenue is a profit-increasing line, so a positive variance is favourable.

```sql run
WITH actual AS (
  SELECT EXTRACT(quarter FROM o.order_date)::int AS qtr,
         SUM(od.unit_price * od.quantity * (1 - od.discount)) AS actual
  FROM orders o JOIN order_details od ON od.order_id = o.order_id
  WHERE o.order_date >= DATE '1997-01-01' AND o.order_date < DATE '1998-01-01'
  GROUP BY 1
), budget(qtr, budget) AS (
  VALUES (1, 8000.0), (2, 8000.0), (3, 12000.0), (4, 12000.0)
)
SELECT a.qtr,
       ROUND(b.budget::numeric, 0) AS budget,
       ROUND(a.actual::numeric, 0) AS actual,
       ROUND((a.actual - b.budget)::numeric, 0) AS variance,
       ROUND(((a.actual - b.budget) / b.budget * 100)::numeric, 1) AS variance_pct,
       CASE WHEN a.actual >= b.budget THEN 'favourable' ELSE 'unfavourable' END AS effect
FROM actual a
JOIN budget b ON b.qtr = a.qtr
ORDER BY a.qtr;
```

```output
qtr | budget | actual | variance | variance_pct | effect
1 | 8000 | 7451 | -549 | -6.9 | unfavourable
2 | 8000 | 3460 | -4540 | -56.7 | unfavourable
3 | 12000 | 17802 | 5802 | 48.3 | favourable
4 | 12000 | 11346 | -654 | -5.4 | unfavourable
```

Quarter 2 is 57 percent below budget and quarter 3 is 48 percent above it, yet the year total (40,059 against a budget of 40,000) is within one percent of plan. Offsetting variances hide problems in a total.

### Example 3: Favourable or unfavourable?

A small profit-and-loss table has revenue and cost lines. The sign convention is applied: for costs, the effect on profit is the negative of the variance. The sum of the effects equals the profit variance.

```python run
import pandas as pd

lines = pd.DataFrame({
    "line": ["revenue", "cogs", "marketing", "freight"],
    "kind": ["income", "cost", "cost", "cost"],
    "budget": [100000.0, 60000.0, 8000.0, 4000.0],
    "actual": [96000.0, 59000.0, 9500.0, 3600.0],
})
lines["variance"] = lines["actual"] - lines["budget"]
lines["effect_on_profit"] = lines["variance"].where(lines["kind"] == "income", -lines["variance"])
lines["label"] = lines["effect_on_profit"].apply(lambda v: "favourable" if v > 0 else "unfavourable")
print(lines[["line", "variance", "effect_on_profit", "label"]].to_string(index=False))
budget_profit = 100000 - 60000 - 8000 - 4000
actual_profit = 96000 - 59000 - 9500 - 3600
print("profit variance:", actual_profit - budget_profit, "| sum of effects:", lines["effect_on_profit"].sum())
```

```output
     line  variance  effect_on_profit        label
  revenue   -4000.0           -4000.0 unfavourable
     cogs   -1000.0            1000.0   favourable
marketing    1500.0           -1500.0 unfavourable
  freight    -400.0             400.0   favourable
profit variance: -4100 | sum of effects: -4100.0
```

Revenue and marketing are unfavourable; the cost of goods sold and freight are favourable because they are below budget. A positive marketing variance (spent more than planned) is unfavourable, which is the case that a plain green-for-positive rule gets wrong.

### Example 4: Volume, price and cost variance, as a waterfall

Budget: 1000 units at a price of 50 and a unit cost of 30. Actual: 900 units at 52 with a unit cost of 32. The three variances are computed and shown as a waterfall table with the running total, which must end at the actual profit.

```python run
q0, p0, c0 = 1000, 50.0, 30.0
q1, p1, c1 = 900, 52.0, 32.0
steps = [
    ("budget profit", q0 * (p0 - c0)),
    ("volume", (q1 - q0) * (p0 - c0)),
    ("price", q1 * (p1 - p0)),
    ("unit cost", -q1 * (c1 - c0)),
]
running = 0.0
for name, v in steps:
    running += v
    print(f"{name:14}{v:9.0f}{running:10.0f}")
print("actual profit:", q1 * (p1 - c1), "| bridge ends at:", running)
```

```output
budget profit     20000     20000
volume            -2000     18000
price              1800     19800
unit cost         -1800     18000
actual profit: 18000.0 | bridge ends at: 18000.0
```

A higher price (+1,800) is cancelled by higher unit costs (-1,800), and the whole shortfall of 2,000 comes from the lower volume. Read as a sentence: "profit is 2,000 below budget because we sold 100 fewer units; the price increase only paid for the cost increase."

## Real-world applications

- **Monthly management accounts**: a variance table with the favourable and unfavourable labels, as in Example 3, is the standard format of a budget review.
- **Margin protection**: a bridge such as Example 4 shows whether a price increase is only recovering supplier cost increases, which affects the next pricing decision.
- **Sales reviews**: quarter-by-quarter budget comparison (Example 2) shows which periods drove the result and where the forecast was wrong.
- **Executive summaries**: a waterfall with three or four labelled steps replaces a page of numbers when presenting to a leadership team (Day 141).

## Daily review

### Key takeaways

- Gross profit is revenue minus COGS; gross margin is gross profit over revenue; markup is over cost.
- Blended margin is total profit over total revenue, not the mean of margins.
- Variance is actual minus budget; favourable means it increased profit, which flips the sign for costs.
- Volume, price and cost variances add up to the profit variance; state the convention used.
- A waterfall ends at the actual figure; offsetting variances can hide problems in a total.

### Common mistakes

- Averaging product margins to get the total margin. Divide total profit by total revenue.
- Calling markup "margin". A 50 percent markup is a 33 percent margin; say which one you report.
- Colouring every positive variance green. Cost overspend is unfavourable; use an effect-on-profit column.
- Dividing a variance by the actual instead of the budget. Variance percent is relative to the plan.
- Reporting only the total variance. Offsetting effects hide trouble; show the components.

### Revision notes

- `margin = (revenue - cogs) / revenue`; `markup = (revenue - cogs) / cogs`.
- `variance = actual - budget`; `variance % = variance / budget`.
- Volume `(Q1-Q0)(P0-C0)`, price `Q1(P1-P0)`, cost `-Q1(C1-C0)`.
- Waterfall running total must equal the actual figure.
